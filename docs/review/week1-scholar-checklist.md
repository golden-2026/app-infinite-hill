# Week 1 scholar checklist (all paths)

Written 2026-11-10. Each path’s week-1 films (7 per path) need a scholar of that faith to read the script and look at the pictures before anything goes public. Below: each film’s story and source, its one-line point, and the specific things the writer flagged. Hindu week 1 scripts are in `prototypes/greybox/tools/films/week1/hinduism-scripts.json`.

How to review: watch the film on the test link (`/hill/lesson.html?door=<path>&day=<n>`), read the script below, and mark each flag OK or give the fix.

## Christianity

### Day 1: The Last Supper, Luke 22:14-23 with 1 Corinthians 11:23-25 (Mark 14:12-25)

- **Point:** Communion means sharing: Christians share bread and cup because Jesus asked his friends to remember him with that meal on the night before he died.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Luke 22:14-23 (KJV 22:19 'this do in remembrance of me'); Mark 14:12-25; 1 Corinthians 11:23-25; 1 Corinthians 10:16-17
- **Script:** The night before he died, Jesus shared a Passover dinner with his friends, in a borrowed upstairs room. He knew what was coming. He knew one friend at that table would hand him over. He took the bread anyway. He gave thanks. He broke it. And he passed it around, even to that friend. This is my body. Then the cup. And one request: this do in remembrance of me. Christians call it communion. It means sharing: one loaf, many people. And for many Christians, remembering means more than a memory. That night is present again. So when that tray comes down the row at church, it's carrying more than bread. It's carrying a dinner that never ended.

### Day 2: The baptism of Jesus in the Jordan, Mark 1:9-11 (with Matthew 28:19 and 1 John 4:8)

- **Point:** Christians believe in one God who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, three persons and still one God, and they see love at the center of who God is.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Mark 1:9-11 ('You are my beloved Son', Mark 1:11); Matthew 28:19; 1 John 4:8; Tertullian, Against Praxeas 2-3; Augustine, On the Trinity 8
- **Script:** A crowd stood in the Jordan River, waiting their turn. A wild preacher named John was baptizing people in its water. Then a carpenter from Nazareth waded in. Jesus. John baptized him. And as he came up out of the water, the sky tore open. The Spirit came down on him like a dove. And a voice from heaven said: You are my beloved Son. One river. One moment. All three. The Son in the water. The Spirit coming down. The Father's voice. Christians call it the Trinity. One God, three persons. Not three gods. The Bible says God is love, and love needs someone to love. So at the center of God, Christians see a relationship. Three, and still one.

### Day 3: Nicodemus comes to Jesus at night, John 3:1-8 (and John 19:38-42)

- **Point:** Born again, in Jesus's word anothen, also means born from above: a new start that comes from God's Spirit as a gift, and no one is too old for it.
- **Sources:** Day 3: John 3:1-8 (3:3 born again, 3:4 'How can a man be born when he is old?', 3:8 the wind); John 19:38-42 (Nicodemus brings spices)
- **Script:** Night in Jerusalem. A respected teacher named Nicodemus slipped through the dark streets to find Jesus. Maybe he didn't want to be seen. Jesus told him: no one can see the kingdom of God unless they're born again. Nicodemus didn't get it. How can a man be born when he is old? Climb back into his mother? But the word Jesus used has two meanings. Again. And from above. A new birth from God's Spirit, like the wind. You hear it, but you can't see where it comes from. Nicodemus left with questions. The next time we see him, he isn't hiding. He's carrying spices to bury Jesus. Nobody gives birth to themselves. It's a gift. And you're never too old to start again.

### Day 4: Philip and the Ethiopian official, Acts 8:26-39 (with Romans 6:3-4)

- **Point:** Baptism comes from the Greek for dip or plunge: going under the water and coming up is the picture of a burial and a rising, the way Christians are welcomed in.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Acts 8:26-39 (8:31 'How can I, except some man should guide me?', 8:36 'here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized?'); Romans 6:3-4; Liddell-Scott-Jones on baptizo
- **Script:** A desert road south of Jerusalem. A chariot rolling home to Ethiopia. Inside, a powerful official, treasurer to the queen, reading a scroll. He doesn't understand it. A man named Philip runs up beside the chariot. Do you understand what you're reading? How can I, unless someone guides me? So Philip climbs in and tells him about Jesus. Then, on that dry road, they pass water. Look, the official says, here is water. What prevents me from being baptized? He stops the chariot. They go down into the water together. Baptism comes from the Greek for dip, or plunge. Down, like a burial. Up, like rising to new life. Baby in a white gown, or sixteen in a pool: under the water, up again.

### Day 5: The woman at the well in Samaria, John 4:5-42

- **Point:** A testimony is a witness statement, from the Greek martys, a witness: saying plainly what happened to you, which is how a whole town first heard about Jesus.
- **Sources:** Day 5: John 4:5-42 (4:9 Jews and Samaritans, 4:29 'Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did', 4:39 believed because of the woman's testimony); Acts 1:8 (martys)
- **Script:** Noon. The hottest hour. A woman walks alone to a well outside her town in Samaria, a heavy jar on her shoulder. A tired traveler is sitting there. A Jewish man. Jesus. He asks her for a drink. She's stunned. Jews and Samaritans didn't mix. They talk, and he tells her her own story, everything she'd ever done. She leaves her water jar behind and runs into town. Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. And the Gospel says many believed because of her testimony. Testimony comes from the Greek for witness: someone who says what they saw. Not a sales pitch. Not a performance. Just: here's what happened to me. Your story counts too. Even a quiet one.

### Day 6: Pentecost, Acts 2:1-41 (with John 14:16-26, Luke 22:54-62, Galatians 5:22-23)

- **Point:** The Holy Spirit is the third name of the Trinity: in the Bible's old languages the same word means breath, wind and spirit, and Jesus called the Spirit the one called alongside you.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Acts 2:1-41; Luke 22:54-62 (Peter's three denials); John 14:16, 26 (the Paraclete); Genesis 1:2 / John 20:22 (ruach, pneuma); Galatians 5:22-23
- **Script:** Fifty days after Passover. Jesus's friends were packed into one house. Weeks earlier, Peter had sworn three times he didn't even know Jesus. Then a sound came from the sky, like a rushing wind. It filled the whole house. Something like flames rested on each of them. They spilled into the street, speaking. Visitors from every nation heard them in their own languages. And Peter, the one who'd been afraid, stood up in front of the crowd and spoke. In the Bible's old languages, spirit, breath and wind are one word. Jesus called the Spirit the one called alongside you. In your corner, on the day you're scared to speak. The Spirit grows quiet things: love, joy, peace. Closer than your own breath.

### Day 7: King Josiah and the scroll found in the temple, 2 Kings 22:3-13 and 23:1-3 (with Genesis 2:9, Revelation 22:1-2)

- **Point:** The Bible is not one book but a library (ta biblia, the books) that Christians read as one story, from a garden with a tree of life to a city made new.
- **Sources:** Day 7: 2 Kings 22:3-13 and 23:1-3; Genesis 2:9; Revelation 22:1-2; Liddell-Scott-Jones on ta biblia
- **Script:** Jerusalem. The temple needed repairs, so a young king named Josiah sent in workers. Then, during the work, the high priest found something. An old scroll. The book of the law. A scribe read it out loud to the king. And Josiah tore his clothes. His people had stopped living by these words. So he gathered everyone, young and old, and had it read to all of them. The word Bible means the books. It's a library: poems, laws, letters, four lives of Jesus, written over centuries. And Christians read it as one story. It starts in a garden with a tree of life. On the last page, that tree is back, in a city made new. That library is on your phone right now.

**Flags from the writer:**

- [ ] Day 1: 'he passed it around, even to that friend' follows Luke 22:21, where the betrayer is still at the table after the bread and cup; in John's account Judas leaves earlier, so some readers will say Judas did not receive the bread. 'That night is present again' is framed as 'for many Christians' because churches differ on real presence vs. memorial.
- [ ] Day 1: the borrowed upstairs room is from Mark 14:14-15 (a guest room lent by a householder); fine, but worth a glance.
- [ ] Day 2: 'The Father's voice' identifies the voice from heaven as the Father, which is the standard Christian reading but is an interpretation; the voice itself is depicted only as light, with no figure of God. 'All three in one moment' is a classic Trinitarian reading of the baptism scene, not a claim the text names the Trinity.
- [ ] Day 3: 'Maybe he didn't want to be seen' is a common interpretation of why Nicodemus came at night, flagged with 'maybe'. Picture 5 places the burial at evening in a garden near the tomb (John 19:41-42); no body is shown.
- [ ] Day 4: the official's quote is paraphrased from Acts 8:36-37 (verse 37 is missing from the oldest manuscripts and is not used). Calling him 'treasurer to the queen' follows 8:27 (Candace); the text also calls him a eunuch, which the film leaves out on purpose. Whether he went fully under is not stated, so pictures show wading in and coming up, which fits 'went down into the water' (8:38).
- [ ] Day 5: 'Jews and Samaritans didn't mix' paraphrases John 4:9 ('have no dealings', or in some translations 'do not share things in common'). Noon is the 'sixth hour' (4:6) in the usual reckoning; 'walks alone' and any idea that she was shunned are interpretation, so the script avoids saying she was an outcast and leaves out her marriages entirely.
- [ ] Day 6: the flames are 'like as of fire' (Acts 2:3) and painted as soft glowing light; speaking in tongues is described only as Acts 2 tells it (understood languages) because churches differ on tongues today. 'In front of the crowd' avoids claiming a number; 3,000 were baptized (2:41).
- [ ] Day 7: identifying the found scroll (often linked with Deuteronomy) is left open on purpose. 'His people had stopped living by these words' paraphrases 2 Kings 22:13. Protestant/Catholic/Orthodox book counts are left out of the film (they are in the lesson) to keep it short.
- [ ] All days: Jesus is never shown face-on (from behind, hands, or distant only); God is shown only as light and sky; the today family is the same fictional cast across all seven films.

## Catholicism

### Day 1: The sending on the mountain, Matthew 28:16-20, and Christians tracing the cross on their foreheads, Tertullian, De Corona 3 (around 200)

- **Point:** The sign of the cross is an ancient two-second prayer: the hand traces the cross over all of you while the words name one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Matthew 28:16-20 (28:17 some doubted; 28:19 the threefold name); Tertullian, De Corona 3 (tracing the sign on the forehead at every daily action); Catechism 2157
- **Script:** A hill in Galilee. Eleven friends climb it to meet Jesus, risen from the dead. Some of them still doubted. He sends them anyway. Go to every nation, he says, and baptize, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Less than two hundred years later, a writer named Tertullian noticed something. Christians traced a tiny cross on their foreheads all day. Getting dressed. Sitting down to eat. Lighting a lamp. That tiny cross grew into this one. The hand traces the cross Jesus died on. The words name one God. Forehead, chest, shoulder, shoulder: where you think, feel and carry. That's why a pitcher does it before the first throw. All of you, in two seconds.

### Day 2: The living bread and the followers who walked away, John 6:22-69; the Last Supper, Luke 22:19

- **Point:** Catholics believe the Eucharist is not a symbol but Christ himself, truly present, which is why he is believed to stay in the tabernacle beside the red lamp.
- **Sources:** Day 2: John 6:22-69 (crowd crosses the lake, 6:24; living bread and 'my flesh', 6:51; 'this saying is hard', 6:60; many walk away, 6:66; 'Lord, to whom shall we go?', 6:68-69); Luke 22:19; Catechism 1328, 1373-1381
- **Script:** A crowd chased Jesus across the lake. He'd just fed thousands with five loaves. Now he told them: I am the living bread. Whoever eats this bread will live forever. And the bread I give is my flesh. People argued. His own followers said: this is too hard. And many of them walked away. He didn't call them back. He let them go. Then he turned to his twelve: will you leave too? Peter said: Lord, to whom would we go? At his last supper, Jesus took bread, gave thanks, and said: this is my body. Gave thanks, in Greek: eucharistia. Catholics believe that at every Mass, the bread truly becomes him. Not a symbol. Him. The red lamp in an empty church? He stayed.

### Day 3: The father who ran, the parable of the prodigal son, Luke 15:11-32; Easter night, John 20:19-23

- **Point:** In confession a Catholic says their sins out loud to a priest and hears out loud that God forgives them, and the priest can never tell anyone.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Luke 15:11-32 (the prodigal son: pigs 15:15-16, rehearsed speech 15:18-19, father runs 15:20, cut off mid-speech 15:21-22); John 20:19-23 (Douay 20:23 quoted); Catechism 1422-1498, seal 1467
- **Script:** Jesus told a story. A young man demanded his inheritance early, left home, and blew all of it. Broke and starving, he took a job feeding pigs. So he headed home, rehearsing a speech. I've sinned. I'm not worthy to be your son. But his father saw him far off, and ran to him. Hugged him before he could finish. Threw a party. Catholics believe confession is that run. On Easter night, Jesus breathed on his friends and said: whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them. So you say it out loud, to a priest. You hear out loud: you're forgiven. And he can never tell a soul. The thing that's huge at two a.m.? Said out loud, it gets smaller.

### Day 4: Paul of Pherme and the three hundred pebbles, Palladius, Lausiac History 20; the tradition of the 'poor man's psalter'

- **Point:** The rosary's beads only keep count, so the hands stay busy and the mind is free to think through scenes from the lives of Jesus and Mary.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Palladius, Lausiac History 20 (Paul of Pherme, 300 prayers and pebbles, the virgin with 700, Macarius with 100); 'poor man's psalter' tradition; John Paul II, Rosarium Virginis Mariae; Catechism 971, 2708
- **Script:** In the Egyptian desert lived a monk named Paul. Every day he prayed three hundred prayers. He kept three hundred pebbles in his lap, and tossed one away with each prayer. Then he heard about a woman in a village who prayed seven hundred. He was crushed. He walked to the great old monk Macarius. Macarius said: I pray only a hundred. And my heart is at peace. The count was never the point. Later, tradition says, people who couldn't read the psalms prayed a hundred and fifty Hail Marys instead, counted on a string. That's the rosary. The beads keep the count, so your mind can walk through the lives of Jesus and Mary. The beads hanging from the mirror? Hands busy. Heart free.

### Day 5: Peter's denial by the fire, Matthew 26:58, 69-75 (the fire from Mark 14, Luke 22 and John 18)

- **Point:** Catholics honor the saints, imperfect people who kept getting up, and ask them to pray for us like friends, while worship belongs to God alone.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Matthew 26:58, 69-75 (followed afar off; three denials, the third with cursing; cock crows; wept bitterly); fire from Mark 14:54, Luke 22:55, John 18:18; Apostles' Creed (communion of saints); Catechism 946-962, 971, 2132
- **Script:** The night Jesus was arrested, his best friend Peter followed at a distance. He sat by a fire in the courtyard, warming his hands. A servant girl looked at him. You were with him. Peter said: I don't know him. Then again. Then a third time, with curses. A rooster crowed. Peter remembered, went outside, and wept bitterly. That same man is Saint Peter. The one holding the keys in church windows. Catholics count him the first pope. A saint isn't someone who never fell. It's someone who kept getting up. Catholics don't worship saints. Worship belongs to God alone. They honor them, and ask: pray for me. Like asking a friend. Living or dead, one family. That's the company you're in.

### Day 6: Jesus fasting forty days and tempted in the desert, Matthew 4:1-11; Ash Wednesday words from Genesis 3:19

- **Point:** Lent is forty days of prayer, fasting and giving before Easter, where giving up something small makes room to notice God and other people.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Matthew 4:1-11 (forty days; stones into bread; answer from Deuteronomy 8:3, paraphrased); Genesis 3:19 and the Roman Missal's Ash Wednesday formula; Code of Canon Law 1250-1253; Catechism 1434, 1438
- **Script:** Before he began his work, Jesus walked into the desert alone. Forty days. No food. At the end, he was starving. Then came the tempter. You're the Son of God? Turn these stones into bread. He didn't. He answered with the scripture: people don't live on bread alone, but on every word from the mouth of God. Hunger, it turns out, can make room. That's Lent. Forty days before Easter. It starts with a cross of ash on your forehead, and the words: remember, you are dust, and to dust you will return. Then prayer, fasting, and giving to the poor. You give up something small, and every reach for it becomes a reminder. Reach for your phone. Stop. Notice. Less, on purpose.

### Day 7: Juan Diego, the roses and the tilma, Our Lady of Guadalupe at Tepeyac, 1531, as told in the Nican Mopohua (traditional account)

- **Point:** Sacramentals are small holy things and gestures, like images, medals, holy water and palms, that are not magic but reminders pointing to God, and every family's version counts.
- **Sources:** Day 7: Nican Mopohua (traditional Nahuatl account of Guadalupe, attributed to Antonio Valeriano); Catechism 1667-1679 (sacramentals, not magic 1670, popular piety 1674-1676); Code of Canon Law 1166-1172; Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy (2001)
- **Script:** December, 1531. A hill outside Mexico City. Tradition says a poor Native man named Juan Diego met a lady there, who spoke to him in his own language. She asked for a church on that spot. The bishop wanted proof. So she sent Juan Diego up the bare, cold hilltop, where roses were blooming in December. He gathered them in his cloak, carried them to the bishop, and let them fall. And on the cloak was her picture. The Virgin of Guadalupe. Millions still visit that cloak. Catholics call holy things like that image sacramentals. A medal. Holy water. A palm. Your abuela's altar. Not magic. Not lucky charms. Reminders that point to God. Look around your family's house. Faith you can hold.

**Flags from the writer:**

- [ ] Day 1: 'Less than two hundred years later' compresses dates: the resurrection is about AD 30 and Tertullian's De Corona about AD 211; the lesson says 'around 200'. The line 'that tiny cross grew into this one' is a simplification of a gradual history.
- [ ] Day 2: The film says 'He didn't call them back' and implies Jesus meant his words literally; that is the Catholic reading the lesson itself gives, but other Christians read John 6 differently. The lines spoken by Jesus and Peter are close paraphrases of John 6:51, 6:60, 6:67-68 and Luke 22:19, not exact quotations.
- [ ] Day 3: Pairing the prodigal son parable with confession ('confession is that run') is a common Catholic teaching image, not an official definition; a scholar should confirm it reads as illustration. The Easter-night quote is Douay-Rheims John 20:23 verbatim.
- [ ] Day 4: Paul of Pherme's story is from the Lausiac History (early monastic literature, not scripture). 'My heart is at peace' paraphrases Macarius saying his conscience does not condemn him; in the source Macarius also gently rebukes Paul, which the film leaves out. The 'poor man's psalter' origin of the rosary is tradition, flagged as such in the script; early forms counted Our Fathers before Hail Marys.
- [ ] Day 5: Matthew does not mention a fire (Mark, Luke and John do); the film blends the Gospels. Calling Peter Jesus's 'best friend' is a popular way of speaking, not a biblical phrase.
- [ ] Day 6: The tempter is shown only as a shadow; check that this is acceptable. Jesus's reply is paraphrased from Matthew 4:4 (quoting Deuteronomy 8:3). The ash formula used is the Roman Missal's ('remember that you are dust'), which the lesson renders via Genesis 3:19 in Douay-Rheims.
- [ ] Day 7: Guadalupe is a Church-approved apparition and Juan Diego is a canonized saint, but the Nican Mopohua's historicity is debated by historians; the script marks it as 'tradition says'. Whether the tilma image itself is properly called a 'sacramental' (it is a venerated image within popular piety) should be checked; the script says 'holy things like that image'. Depicting the Lady's apparition on screen should follow the traditional image.

## Judaism

### Day 1: Onkelos and the mezuzah, Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 11a

- **Point:** A mezuzah is not a charm: it's a handwritten scroll of the Torah's words (hear, O Israel) on the doorpost, a reminder each time you cross the door of who you are.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 11a (Onkelos and the mezuzah); Gittin 56b (Onkelos as the son of Titus's sister); Deuteronomy 6:4-9; Maimonides, Laws of Mezuzah 5:4 (not an amulet)
- **Script:** The emperor's own nephew, Onkelos, became a Jew. The emperor was furious. He sent soldiers to bring him back. Onkelos talked with them, and they became Jews too. So the emperor sent more, with one order: don't say a word to him. They marched him out of his house. At the door, Onkelos touched a small case on the doorpost, and smiled. They had to ask: what is that? He said: a king sits inside, guarded from outside. With us, it's the other way around. And they became Jews too. That case is a mezuzah. Inside, no charm. Just handwritten words from the Torah: hear, O Israel. Many Jews touch it on the way out. A reminder of who you are.

### Day 2: The two angels who walk you home on Friday night, Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 119b; the two wordings of the Sabbath, Exodus 20:8 and Deuteronomy 5:12

- **Point:** Shabbat begins at sunset on Friday with two candles, for remember and observe, lit so the family can see each other's faces in peace.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 119b (the two ministering angels and the lit lamp); Exodus 20:8 and Deuteronomy 5:12 (remember, observe); Shabbat 23b (the Shabbat light for peace in the home)
- **Script:** Friday night. A man walks home from synagogue. The rabbis say two angels walk with him. One good, one bad. At his door, they look inside. If the lamp is lit, the table set, the bed made, the good angel says: may it be like this next week too. And the bad angel has to answer: amen. Even he can't argue with that light. That's why, just before sunset, Shabbat starts with two candles. One for remember the Sabbath, one for observe it. Light first, hands over the eyes, a blessing, then look. A light so the family can see each other's faces. The week stops. Even your phone can wait.

### Day 3: Hillel and the man on one foot, Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a; Hillel's saying in Mishnah Avot 2:5

- **Point:** A mensch, the Yiddish word for a real human being, is someone decent when nobody's checking, which is what Hillel taught: don't do to others what's hateful to you, and where no one acts humanely, be the one who does.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (Shammai's builder's cubit, Hillel's 'what is hateful to you'); Mishnah Avot 2:5 (where there are no men, strive to be a man)
- **Script:** A stranger walked up to the great teacher Shammai with a dare. Teach me the whole Torah while I stand on one foot. Shammai, a builder, chased him off with his measuring stick. So the stranger tried another teacher, Hillel. Same dare. Hillel didn't flinch. What's hateful to you, don't do to anyone else. That's the whole Torah. The rest is explanation. Now go and learn it. Hillel also taught: where nobody is acting like a real human being, try to be one. Yiddish gave that a single word. Mensch. Not a genius. Not a saint. Just decent, when nobody's checking. When the group chat piles on someone, be the mensch.

### Day 4: The night of leaving Egypt, Exodus 12:31-39; the seder's answer, Mishnah Pesachim 10:4-5 and the Haggadah ('we were slaves')

- **Point:** The seder retells the Exodus with matzah, the bread that had no time to rise, and its answer starts with 'we were slaves', because every generation tells it as their own story.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Exodus 12:31-39 (Pharaoh's 'get out', the kneading bowls bound in their cloaks, unleavened cakes baked on the way); Mishnah Pesachim 10:4-5; the Haggadah (the four questions; avadim hayinu)
- **Script:** Egypt. Midnight. After ten plagues, the king finally gives in: get out. Now. A whole people of slaves, and no time. The bread dough hadn't risen yet. So they wrapped it, still in the bowls, in their cloaks, threw it over their shoulders, and walked out into the dark. On the road, they baked it flat. Matzah. Ever since, on Passover night, families eat that bread. At the seder, the youngest asks: why is this night different from all other nights? And the answer doesn't start with once upon a time. It starts with: we were slaves. We. In every generation, each person should see themselves as if they walked out of Egypt. It's not their story. It's yours.

### Day 5: Mar son of Ravina breaks a precious cup at his son's wedding feast, Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 30b-31a; remembering Jerusalem, Psalms 137:5-6

- **Point:** A glass is broken at the end of a Jewish wedding so that even at the height of joy we remember what is still broken, starting with Jerusalem, and then everyone shouts mazel tov.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 30b-31a (Mar son of Ravina breaks a cup worth 400 zuz at his son's wedding); Psalms 137:5-6; Tosafot to Berakhot 31a and Rema, Orach Chaim 560:2 (the glass and remembering Jerusalem)
- **Script:** Ancient Babylon. A wedding feast. A great rabbi, Mar son of Ravina, is marrying off his son. The wine is flowing. The rabbis at the tables are getting louder. And louder. So he brings out his most precious cup, worth a fortune, and smashes it, right in front of them. Silence. Everyone sobers up. Later teachers kept the crash, and gave it a reason: Jerusalem, and its Temple, destroyed. Even at your happiest, remember what's still broken. That's why a Jewish wedding ends with a glass, wrapped in cloth, crushed underfoot. Then everyone shouts mazel tov. On your best day, you can still remember who isn't there.

### Day 6: Judith Kaplan's bat mitzvah, the first public bat mitzvah in America, New York, 18 March 1922; the ages of the commandments, Mishnah Avot 5:21 and Talmud Niddah 45b

- **Point:** You don't 'have' a bar or bat mitzvah, you become one: at twelve or thirteen the mitzvot, the commandments, become your own responsibility, and the ceremony just says it out loud.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Judith Kaplan Eisenstein's bat mitzvah, 18 March 1922, Society for the Advancement of Judaism (Jewish Women's Archive); Mishnah Avot 5:21; Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 45b (twelve for a girl, thirteen for a boy); Genesis Rabbah 63:10
- **Script:** New York, 1922. A Saturday morning. A twelve-year-old girl, Judith Kaplan, stands in front of her father's congregation. She says the blessings and reads from the Torah, out loud, from a printed book. A girl. In public. Almost nobody had ever seen that. It's remembered as the first public bat mitzvah in America. But here's the twist. By Jewish law, she was already bat mitzvah, the day she turned twelve. Boys, at thirteen. It comes on its own. Bat mitzvah: daughter of the commandment. Mitzvah: what the Torah asks of you. The ceremony just says it out loud. Until then, your choices were your parents' to answer for. Now, they're yours.

### Day 7: These and these are the words of the living God: the schools of Hillel and Shammai, Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b; an argument for the sake of heaven, Mishnah Avot 5:17

- **Point:** Machloket, disagreement, is how Jewish learning works: an argument for the sake of heaven keeps both sides, and the law followed Hillel because his students were humble and taught the other side first.
- **Sources:** Day 7: Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b (three years of dispute, the heavenly voice, why the law follows Beit Hillel); Mishnah Avot 5:17 (an argument for the sake of heaven); Mishnah Eduyot 1:5-6 (minority views recorded)
- **Script:** Two schools of rabbis, in the land of Israel, two thousand years ago. Hillel's students, and Shammai's. For three years they argued. How to live, how to pray, what the Torah really asks. Each side said: the law follows us. Then, the Talmud says, a voice came from heaven. These and these are the words of the living God. Both. But the law follows Hillel. Why? Because Hillel's students were kind and humble. They taught the other side's view along with their own, and even taught it first. ## Islam

### Day 1: The hoopoe and the queen of Sheba's letter, Qur'an 27:20-31 (the bismillah at 27:30)

- **Point:** Bismillah means 'in the name of God': Muslims start things with it, so nothing begins alone.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Qur'an 27:20-31 (hoopoe, Sheba, the letter; bismillah at 27:30); Qur'an 1:1; surah 9 opens without the bismillah
- **Script:** A small bird, a hoopoe, went missing, then came back with news. Far south, in Sheba, a queen sat on a mighty throne, and her people bowed to the sun. So the prophet Solomon sent the bird back, with a letter. It dropped the letter before the queen. She broke the seal and read it to her court. The most powerful king alive, and his letter didn't open with his own name. It opened: in the name of God, the Beneficent, the Merciful. That's bismillah. All but one surah of the Qur'an opens with it. Muslims say it before a meal, a drive, an exam. So you never start alone.

### Day 2: The scorpion-stung chief healed with al-Fatiha, Sahih al-Bukhari 5749 / Sahih Muslim 2201; with the hadith qudsi of the prayer as a conversation, Sahih Muslim 395

- **Point:** Al-Fatiha, the opening, is seven verses said in every prayer: mostly praise, one request (show us the straight path), and described as a conversation with God.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Sahih al-Bukhari 5749, 2276; Sahih Muslim 2201 (al-Fatiha recited over the stung chief); Qur'an 1:1-7; Sahih Muslim 395 (hadith qudsi: the prayer as a conversation); Sahih al-Bukhari 756 (no prayer without al-Fatiha)
- **Script:** A band of travelers, companions of the Prophet, reached a desert camp and asked for food. The tribe said no. Then their chief was stung by a scorpion. Nothing they tried worked. So they came running back: can any of you do something? One traveler knelt by the chief and recited seven short verses. The chief stood up and walked. Those seven verses? Every Muslim says them in every prayer. Al-Fatiha, the opening. Mostly praise, then one request: show us the straight path. A saying of the Prophet calls it a conversation, with God answering, line by line. Those words you've heard a thousand times? Someone answers them.

### Day 3: Bilal's footsteps heard in Paradise, Sahih al-Bukhari 1149 / Sahih Muslim 2458; with the hadith of wrongs leaving with the water, Sahih Muslim 244

- **Point:** Wudu, the washing before prayer, is a reset that isn't about dirt: as the water runs off, the wrongs run off with it.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Sahih al-Bukhari 1149; Sahih Muslim 2458 (Bilal's footsteps); Sahih Muslim 244 (wrongs leave with the water); Qur'an 5:6; Bilal's persecution and role as first mu'adhdhin: Ibn Ishaq, Sira
- **Script:** Bilal had once been enslaved, punished in the desert sun for his faith. Freed, he became the first voice to call Muslims to prayer. One morning, the Prophet asked him a question. Bilal, what do you do? In Paradise, I heard your footsteps ahead of me. Bilal's answer was small. Every time I wash for prayer, day or night, I pray after it. That's all. That washing is wudu: hands, mouth, face, arms, head, feet. It isn't about dirt. People do it already clean. A saying of the Prophet says that as the water runs off, the wrongs run off with it. Rough day? Rinse it off.

### Day 4: The parable of the two gardens, Qur'an 18:32-44 (ma sha' Allah at 18:39), with the command to say 'if God wills', Qur'an 18:23-24

- **Point:** Inshallah ('if God wills') is for plans about tomorrow; mashallah ('what God has willed') is for good that's already here; both come from the same surah.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Qur'an 18:32-44 (two gardens; 18:34-35 the boast, 18:39 ma sha' Allah, 18:42 the vines fallen on their trellises, wringing his hands); Qur'an 18:23-24 (inshallah)
- **Script:** Two men. One owned two gardens: grapevines, date palms, a river running between them. He walked through with his friend and bragged. I have more than you. I don't think this will ever end. His friend asked one question. When you walked in, why didn't you say: mashallah? What God has willed. Then it was all gone. The vines collapsed on their trellises. He stood there, wringing his hands. That's in the Qur'an, in the surah called the Cave. The same surah says: never say I'll do it tomorrow without, if God wills. Inshallah. So, inshallah for tomorrow. Mashallah for what's already here. Plan it fully. Then hold it open.

### Day 5: Ibrahim asks to see how God gives life to the dead, Qur'an 2:260; with the companions' troubling thoughts, 'that is clear faith', Sahih Muslim 132

- **Point:** Iman, faith, can grow and has room for questions: in the Qur'an a prophet asked to see so his heart could rest, and he wasn't turned away.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Qur'an 2:260 (Ibrahim and the four birds); Sahih Muslim 132 ('that is clear faith'); Qur'an 48:4 (adding faith to faith)
- **Script:** A man asked God for proof. Not just anyone: Ibrahim, Abraham, one of the greatest prophets. My Lord, show me how you give life to the dead. The Qur'an records the reply: do you not believe? Yes, he said. But so my heart can be at ease. He wasn't scolded. He was shown. Four birds, scattered across four hills. He called them, and they came flying back to him. Centuries later, the Prophet's companions confessed thoughts so dark they could barely say them. He told them: that is clear faith. Iman, faith, can grow. Questions are part of it. Ask, so your heart can rest.

### Day 6: The first revelation in the cave of Hira, Sahih al-Bukhari 3 and Qur'an 96:1-5; Ramadan as the month the Qur'an was revealed, Qur'an 2:185; the fast's purpose, Qur'an 2:183

- **Point:** Ramadan is the month the Qur'an first came, so Muslims fast from dawn to sunset: not to suffer, but to grow mindful.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Sahih al-Bukhari 3 (Hira, 'Read', returning trembling, Khadijah covering him); Qur'an 96:1-5; Qur'an 2:183, 2:185; Ramadan timing of the first revelation: Ibn Ishaq, Sira; Sunan Abi Dawud 2356 (breaking the fast with dates and water)
- **Script:** Outside Mecca there's a mountain with a small cave near the top. A man used to climb it to be alone, and think. One night, in Ramadan, tradition says, the silence broke. A voice: Read. He came down trembling. His wife Khadijah wrapped him in a cloak. That man was the Prophet Muhammad, and those were the first words of the Qur'an. That's why Ramadan matters. It's the month the Qur'an came. So Muslims fast, dawn to sunset: no food, not even water. Not to suffer. The Qur'an says it's so you'll be mindful. Then sunset. A date, some water. Hunger teaches you to notice.

### Day 7: The two old festival days of Medina replaced by two better ones, Sunan Abi Dawud 1134 / Sunan an-Nasa'i 1556; zakat al-fitr, Sahih al-Bukhari 1503; Ibrahim's ram, Qur'an 37:102-107; sharing the meat, Qur'an 22:36

- **Point:** Islam has two Eids, al-Fitr after Ramadan and al-Adha at the pilgrimage, and both are built so nobody is left out of the feast.

The second, Eid al-Adha, at the time of the pilgrimage. It remembers Ibrahim, and the ram given in place of his son. The meat is shared with family, neighbors, and people in need. Two Eids. ## Buddhism

### Day 1: The insulting brahmin and the uneaten food (Akkosa Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 7.2)

- **Point:** Karma means action and the intention inside it, so you own what you do and don't have to take on what others throw at you.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Akkosa Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 7.2) for the story; Nibbedhika Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63) for 'intention is kamma'; Upajjhatthana Sutta (AN 5.57) for 'owner of my actions'
- **Script:** A man stormed up to the Buddha, furious. Someone from his own clan had just become a monk. So he shouted every insult he knew. The Buddha let him finish. Then he asked one question. When guests come to your house, do you offer them food? Yes. And if they don't eat it, whose food is it? Mine, the man said. So it is with your insults, said the Buddha. I don't accept them. They stay with you. That's karma. Not the universe paying people back. Karma means action, and the Buddha said the intention inside it is what counts. Your choices shape you. When someone comes at you online, their words are theirs. What you do next is yours.

### Day 2: Never Disparaging Bodhisattva (Lotus Sutra, chapter 20), leading into the three bows to the three jewels

- **Point:** Buddhists bow three times to the three jewels: the Buddha (a person who woke up), the Dharma (his teaching) and the Sangha (the community), not to a statue.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Lotus Sutra ch. 20 (Sadaparibhuta / Never Disparaging Bodhisattva); Khuddakapatha 1 (the three refuges)
- **Script:** A monk named Never Disparaging had one practice. He bowed to every person he met. Rich, poor, strangers, rude ones. And he told each one: I would never look down on you. You can become a buddha too. People found it strange. Some got angry. They threw sticks and stones at him. He stepped back, out of reach, and bowed again, from a distance. That's from the Lotus Sutra. When Buddhists bow three times, it isn't to a statue. One bow to the Buddha, a person who woke up. One to the Dharma, his teaching. One to the Sangha, the people who walk it together. The three jewels. Bow to what can wake up in anyone. Even the kid who's rude to you.

### Day 3: The bowl of oil through the crowd (Janapadakalyani Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 47.20)

- **Point:** Sati, the old word behind 'mindfulness', means remembering to notice: holding your attention steady while everything pulls it away.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Janapadakalyani Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 47.20), the bowl of oil; sati related to Sanskrit smrti (memory)
- **Script:** A huge crowd packed the town square. The most famous star in the land was singing and dancing, and everyone was screaming. Then a man was handed a bowl, filled to the brim with oil. His task: carry it right between the crowd and the stage. Behind him walked a guard with a raised sword. Spill one drop, and that's the end of you. Would he look up at the show? Not once. The Buddha told this story, and then said: the bowl is mindfulness. The old word is sati. It's related to a word for memory. Not just paying attention. Remembering to, step after step, while everything screams at you to look away. Your phone buzzes all day. Sati is remembering your bowl.

### Day 4: The first sermon to the five companions at the deer park near Varanasi (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, SN 56.11; the five's snub from Vinaya Mahavagga I.6 / Majjhima Nikaya 26); doctor comparison from later teachers (Visuddhimagga XVI)

- **Point:** The four noble truths are a diagnosis, not a verdict: there is dukkha, it has a cause (craving), it can end, and there's a path.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11); the five's agreement to snub him and then serving him: Vinaya Mahavagga I.6 / Ariyapariyesana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 26); doctor comparison: Visuddhimagga XVI
- **Script:** Five monks had given up on their old friend. He'd quit their starving practice, so they called him soft. When he walked into the deer park near Varanasi, they agreed: don't stand, don't greet him. But as he came closer, they couldn't help it. One took his bowl. One brought water. And he told them what he'd found. One: there is dukkha, the ache of loss and change. Two: it has a cause, craving, wanting things other than they are. Three: it can end. Four: there's an eight-part path. The four noble truths. Later teachers compared it to a doctor's visit. Not a verdict that life is bad. A diagnosis, and a cure. Hard week? It's not just you. It's not the end.

### Day 5: Sujata's offering of milk rice before the awakening (Nidanakatha, the Jataka commentary) and the morning alms round; the Buddha's line on the fruit of giving (Itivuttaka 26)

- **Point:** Dana means giving: it fills the monks' alms bowls, it is a practice for the giver, and in the Buddha's teaching it comes first.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Nidanakatha (Jataka commentary) for Sujata's milk rice; Itivuttaka 26 for 'if beings knew the fruit of giving and sharing they would not eat without giving'; Theravada alms round practice
- **Script:** A seeker had starved himself for six years, trying to break through. He was skin and bone, sitting under a tree. A young woman named Sujata came with a golden bowl of milk rice, an offering she'd promised. She gave it to him. He ate. His strength came back. And that very night, sitting beneath another tree, he woke up. He became the Buddha. It began with a gift. That's dana: giving. Giving still fills Buddhist monks' bowls today. At dawn they walk with covered bowls. They don't ask. People give, and often the givers bow. Not begging: an exchange. The Buddha said, if people knew the fruit of giving, they'd never eat without sharing. Give first. Your lunch, your time, a hand.

### Day 6: Dona and the footprints, 'remember me as awake' (Dona Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 4.36), leading into Vesak

- **Point:** Buddha is a title meaning 'one who woke up', and on Vesak many Buddhists celebrate his birth, awakening and death on one spring full moon.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Dona Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 4.36); Vesak as the triple commemoration in Theravada lands
- **Script:** A priest named Dona saw footprints on a road. Not ordinary ones. Each held the mark of a wheel, with a thousand spokes. He followed them to a tree, where a man sat, calm and bright. Dona asked: are you a god? No. A heavenly spirit? No. A human being? No. Then what are you? Remember me, he said, as awake. In the old language: buddha. It's not a name. It's a title. One who woke up. That's who Buddhists celebrate on Vesak, a full moon in spring. In many lands it marks his birth, his awakening and his death, all at once. Lanterns everywhere. Not for a god. For a person who woke up. So waking up is something people do.

### Day 7: King Milinda and the monk Nagasena: the lamp burning through the night (Milindapanha II.2.1) and the lamp lit from a lamp (Milindapanha III.5.5)

- **Point:** Buddhist rebirth isn't a soul moving into a new body; like a flame lit from a flame, what carries on is neither the same nor someone else.
- **Sources:** Day 7: Milindapanha II.2.1 (lamp burning through the watches of the night, neither the same nor another) and III.5.5 (lamp lit from a lamp)
- **Script:** King Milinda loved to win arguments, so he put a hard one to a monk named Nagasena. When someone is reborn, is it the same person, or someone else? Neither, said the monk. Picture a lamp burning all night. Is the flame at midnight the same flame from the evening? No. Is it a different lamp, then? Also no. It's one lamp, flame lit from flame. Rebirth is like that. Many Buddhist traditions teach that one life is followed by another, shaped by karma. But no soul packs up and moves. What carries on is momentum, like one flame lighting the next. Even now, each moment is lit from the last. Who you were last year isn't who you are now. But it lit you.

## Sikhism

### Day 1: The Bhatt court poets and the cry 'wahe guru', their swayyas in the Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1389-1409 (Waheguru at Ang 1402-1404)

- **Point:** Waheguru, the Sikh name for God, means 'wow, the teacher': not a title but a cry of wonder, because Sikh scripture says God is beyond describing.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Guru Granth Sahib, Swayyas of the Bhatts, Ang 1389-1409 (Waheguru repeated in Bhatt Gayand's swayyas, Ang 1402-1404); Japji Sahib, Ang 1-8 (God beyond counting and description)
- **Script:** A family of court poets came to the Sikh Gurus. The Bhatts. Their whole job was praising kings, in the biggest words money could buy. Now they wanted to praise the Gurus, and the God they pointed to. So they tried. Bigger words. Grander lines. Nothing was big enough. Sikh scripture says God is beyond counting, beyond describing. So their poems, kept in the Guru Granth Sahib, break into one cry: wahe guru. Wow. The teacher. That's the name Sikhs say for God. Not a title, like king. A reaction. When words run out, you say wow. Good news? Waheguru. Hard news? Waheguru. It points past the feeling, to what's bigger.

### Day 2: Emperor Akbar sits in the pangat before meeting Guru Amar Das at Goindwal ('pehle pangat, phir sangat'), Sikh tradition

- **Point:** Langar is the free Sikh community kitchen where everyone, even an emperor, sits on the floor in one row and eats the same food, so nobody sits higher.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Sikh tradition of Guru Amar Das at Goindwal and the rule 'pehle pangat, phir sangat'; tradition of Emperor Akbar eating in the langar; langar origin at Kartarpur under Guru Nanak
- **Script:** The emperor of India, Akbar, rode to the small town of Goindwal to meet the third Sikh Guru. Silk, jewels, guards. But at the door, he was told the rule. First the meal, then the meeting. Sit in the row. So the most powerful man in India took off his shoes, sat down on the floor between farmers and laborers, and ate the same simple food from the same kitchen. That kitchen is the langar. Free. Vegetarian. Open to anyone, of any faith or none. No chairs, so nobody sits higher. At school, where you sit says who you are. In the langar row, it can't.

### Day 3: Guru Gobind Singh makes the turban (dastar) over uncut hair (kesh) part of every Sikh's identity, when fine turbans marked kings and nobles; Sikh tradition and Sikh Rehat Maryada

- **Point:** The Sikh turban isn't a hat: it's a crown tied fresh each day over uncut hair, given to every Sikh whatever family they came from.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Sikh Rehat Maryada (kesh, turban); Sikh tradition on Guru Gobind Singh giving all Sikhs the marks of nobility (turban) as Khalsa identity
- **Script:** In the world of the Gurus, a fine turban meant one thing. Power. Kings wore them. Nobles wore them. A farmer? A barber? Not them. Then the tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, asked his Sikhs to keep their hair uncut, as it grew, and made the turban part of who they are. Whatever family they came from. So the farmer tied one. So did the barber. Suddenly, ordinary people walked through the market wearing crowns. That's the dastar. Not a hat that comes off with the weather. A crown, tied fresh each morning, over hair left as it grew. The first try always comes out lopsided. Every try is still a crown you tie yourself.

### Day 4: Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur: the call for a head, the tent, and the Panj Pyare; Sikh tradition

- **Point:** The Panj Pyare, 'five beloved', were five strangers from different castes and corners of India who stood up and walked out as one family, the first of the Khalsa.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Sikh tradition of Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur; names and origins of the Panj Pyare (Daya Singh of Lahore, Dharam Singh near Delhi, Himmat Singh of Puri, Mohkam Singh of Dwarka, Sahib Singh of Bidar); Sikh Rehat Maryada
- **Script:** Spring, 1699. A huge crowd on a hillside at Anandpur, for the harvest festival. Then the tenth Guru steps out with a drawn sword and asks: who will give their head for the faith? Silence. One man stands. Daya Ram, a shopkeeper. He follows the Guru into a tent. The Guru comes out alone, and asks again. Four more stand, one at a time. A farmer. A water-carrier. A cloth printer. A barber. What happened in that tent, the tellings differ. But all five walked out alive, dressed alike, side by side. Five castes that would never share a meal. One family: the Panj Pyare, the five beloved. Family isn't only blood. It's who stands up beside you.

### Day 5: Bhai Ghanaiya gives water to the wounded of both sides at Anandpur (Sikh tradition), and the Ardas' closing words 'sarbat da bhala' (Sikh Rehat Maryada)

- **Point:** The Sikh standing prayer, the Ardas, ends by asking for sarbat da bhala, the good of all: everyone, not just Sikhs, even the people you're against.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Sikh tradition of Bhai Ghanaiya at Anandpur (water and marham/balm); Ardas as in the Sikh Rehat Maryada ('Nanak naam chardi kala, tere bhane sarbat da bhala')
- **Script:** A battle near Anandpur. In the dust, one Sikh, Bhai Ghanaiya, walks the field with a water bag. He kneels by a wounded Sikh, and gives him water. Then he kneels by a wounded enemy soldier. And gives him water too. Some Sikhs were furious. They took him to the Guru: he's helping the other side! Ghanaiya answered, as the story is told: I didn't see sides. I saw the same light in every face. And the Guru didn't punish him. He gave him a balm, to tend their wounds too. That's the heart of the Sikh standing prayer, the Ardas. It ends with three words: sarbat da bhala. The good of all. Not just us. Even the person you're not speaking to.

### Day 6: Guru Nanak disappears into the river Bein at Sultanpur and returns saying 'there is no Hindu, there is no Musalman', Janamsakhi traditions

- **Point:** Sikhi is its own religion, begun by Guru Nanak, not a branch of Hinduism or Islam; 'Sikh' means a learner.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Janamsakhi traditions (Puratan and Bhai Bala) of the river Bein at Sultanpur and 'na koi Hindu, na koi Musalman'; Encyclopaedia of Sikhism on 'Sikh' from Sanskrit shishya
- **Script:** In the town of Sultanpur, a young man named Nanak went down to the river Bein at dawn to bathe, like every day. He walked into the water. And he didn't come out. The town searched. Nets dragged the river. People gave him up for lost. On the third day, he walked out of the river, and for a long time said nothing. Then his first words: there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim. Some hear: under the labels, everyone is made by the one. Others hear: nobody lives up to their label. Either way, a new path began. Its followers are called Sikh. It means learner. So, Hindu or Muslim? Neither. A learner, on a path of its own.

### Day 7: Guru Hargobind takes up two swords, miri and piri, at his installation in Amritsar, 1606 (Sikh tradition); the parts of the Khanda emblem

- **Point:** The Khanda, the Sikh symbol, is a double-edged sword for truth, a circle without end, and two curved swords for the outer and inner life held together.
- **Sources:** Day 7: Sikh tradition of Guru Hargobind's installation in 1606 and the two swords of miri and piri (Baba Buddha officiating); Encyclopaedia of Sikhism on the Khanda emblem; Sikh Rehat Maryada on the Nishan Sahib
- **Script:** Amritsar, 1606. The fifth Guru had just been put to death by the Mughal authorities. His son, only eleven, became the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind. At the ceremony, he was offered the old signs of the role. He asked for something else. Two swords. One for miri, the world: standing up for people, justice, the hard outer life. One for piri, the spirit: prayer, kindness, the inner life. Both, worn together. Look at the Sikh symbol, the Khanda. Those two curved swords wrap around the outside. In the middle, a circle with no beginning and no end, and a double-edged sword that cuts truth from lies. No crown. No face. You've got an inner life and an outer one. Hold them together.

## My own religion

### Day 1: The old man at the frontier who lost his horse, Huainanzi chapter 18 (Chinese classic, about 139 BCE)

- **Point:** No one gets told the reason for life's turns, but 'what now?' always has an answer.
- **Sources:** Day 1: Huainanzi 18 (the old man at the frontier who lost his horse); lesson sources Kelemen 1999 and Banerjee & Bloom 2014 for the 'what now' frame
- **Script:** An old Chinese book, the Huainanzi, tells this. A farmer's horse ran off. Bad luck, said the neighbors. The old man said: who knows? Months later, the horse came home, leading fine wild horses. Great luck! Who knows? His son rode one, fell, and broke his leg. Terrible. Who knows? Then war came, and the young men were marched off to fight. The son, with his broken leg, stayed home. Notice what the old man never got: the reason. The book says the turns of luck can't be fully known. People still argue whether there's a plan behind them. But one question always has an answer. Not why. What now?

### Day 2: The Buddha gives up starving and breathless meditation and remembers the rose-apple tree, Majjhima Nikaya 36 (Mahasaccaka Sutta), Pali Canon; his breath practice per Samyutta Nikaya 54.8 (Buddhist)

- **Point:** Meditation isn't forcing a blank mind; it's feeling the breath, wandering, noticing and coming back.
- **Sources:** Day 2: Majjhima Nikaya 36 (Mahasaccaka Sutta: austerities, breathless meditation, the rose-apple tree memory, eating boiled rice and porridge, the five companions leave); Samyutta Nikaya 54.8 (before awakening he mostly dwelt in mindfulness of breathing); William James, Principles of Psychology ch. XI
- **Script:** Before he was the Buddha, he was a prince who gave up everything to find the truth. He starved himself to skin and bone. He even tried to stop breathing, until his head pounded like a drum. Nothing. Then he remembered something. As a boy, sitting in the shade of a rose-apple tree, he'd felt a calm that came on its own. No forcing. So he ate a bowl of rice. His five companions walked out in disgust. He's quit, they said. He hadn't. He'd found the way in. Buddhists still begin where he did: with the breath. Feel it. Wander. Notice. Come back. Your mind wanders twenty times in a minute? That's twenty reps.

### Day 3: The Roman triumph's reminder (Tertullian, Apologeticum 33) and Marcus Aurelius on death in the Meditations (4.17), Roman Stoic philosophy

- **Point:** Memento mori means remembering that you will die so that you live well now, not so that you live in fear.
- **Sources:** Day 3: Tertullian, Apologeticum 33 ('look behind you, remember you are a man'); Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.17 (Long 1862, paraphrased in modern English); camp setting from the Meditations' book headings (among the Quadi, at Carnuntum)
- **Script:** In ancient Rome, a winning general rode through the city in a golden chariot. Crowds roared his name. And, an old writer says, a voice behind him kept saying: look behind you. Remember you're only human. One emperor gave himself that reminder. Marcus Aurelius, ruler of the whole Roman world, sat in an army tent by lamplight, writing private notes about death. Again and again. You'd expect fear. It isn't there. Don't act as if you'll live ten thousand years, he wrote. While you live, while you can, be good. That's memento mori. Remember you will die, so today comes clear. The apology you keep saving for next month? It's this month's.

### Day 4: Shankara and the man with four dogs at Varanasi, the traditional framing story of the Manisha Panchakam (Hindu tradition, attributed to Adi Shankara)

- **Point:** Namaste means 'a bow to you', and the bow is meant for the person themselves, whatever their rank.
- **Sources:** Day 4: Manisha Panchakam (attributed to Adi Shankara) and its traditional framing story at Varanasi; Monier-Williams for namas / te
- **Script:** Hindu tradition tells this story. At dawn in Varanasi, a young teacher named Shankara walked down to the river Ganga. On the steps stood a poor man with four dogs, a man his society called untouchable. Move aside, Shankara said. The man didn't move. He asked: move what? This body, made of food, away from yours, made of food? Or the awareness in me, away from the awareness in you? It's the same. Shankara stopped. And bowed to him. Many Hindus hear that bow inside namaste. Namas: a bow. Te: to you. Not to someone's rank, or money, or mood. To them. So mean it. Even to the kid nobody sits with.

### Day 5: Nehemiah inspects Jerusalem's broken walls by night and rebuilds them, Book of Nehemiah 1-6 (Hebrew Bible)

- **Point:** Wishing alone isn't enough; what works is picturing the wish, then honestly facing what's in the way and planning for it.
- **Sources:** Day 5: Nehemiah 1:1-4 (cupbearer; broken walls, burned gates; weeps and prays), 2:1-15 (asks the king; inspects the walls by night), 3 (builders repair sections, some opposite their own houses), 4:9 (prayed and set a watch day and night), 6:15 (finished in fifty-two days); lesson's New Thought and Oettingen sources for 'manifesting is new'
- **Script:** In the Hebrew Bible, a man named Nehemiah poured wine for the king of Persia. Then news came: back home, Jerusalem's walls lay broken, its gates burned. He wept. He prayed. Then he did something most dreamers skip. He traveled to the city, rode out at night, and inspected every broken stretch of wall. Only then did he gather the people and make a plan: families rebuilt the sections near their own homes. When enemies threatened, the Bible says, they prayed, and set a guard. The wall rose in fifty-two days. Manifesting is new: 1800s America. But this old story knew its missing half. Picture the wish. Then walk the wall.

### Day 6: The speck and the plank, the Sermon on the Mount, Matthew 7:3-5 (Christian Gospels), with Carl Jung's idea of the shadow

- **Point:** What annoys us most in other people can point to something unclaimed in ourselves, what Jung called the shadow.
- **Sources:** Day 6: Matthew 7:3-5 (speck and beam); Mark 6:3 ('is not this the carpenter'); C. G. Jung, Aion ch. 2 'The Shadow' (paraphrased)
- **Script:** In the Gospel of Matthew, a crowd sits on a hillside in Galilee, listening to Jesus. People knew him as a carpenter. And he hands them a carpenter's picture. Why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye, he asks, and miss the plank in your own? Picture it. A man with a whole beam of wood sticking out of his eye, squinting, trying to pick a splinter out of his friend's. It's funny. Then it isn't. Centuries later, the psychiatrist Carl Jung named the pattern: the shadow. What we won't own in ourselves, we spot in everyone else. So when someone bugs you more than they should, ask: is any of this mine?

### Day 7: The Buddha and the bandit Angulimala, Majjhima Nikaya 86 (Angulimala Sutta), Pali Canon; 'intention is karma', Anguttara Nikaya 6.63 (Buddhist)

- **Point:** Karma means action, and the Buddha said the intention behind it is what counts, so karma is about what you are planting, not payback for others.
- **Sources:** Day 7: Majjhima Nikaya 86 (Angulimala Sutta: villages emptied, the Buddha walks his road, cannot be caught, 'I have stopped, you stop too', throws away his weapons, ordains); Anguttara Nikaya 6.63 (intention is kamma)
- **Script:** In the Buddha's time, a bandit named Angulimala terrified the forests of Kosala. Whole villages emptied. One morning, the Buddha walked straight down his road, alone. Angulimala grabbed his sword and ran after him. But however fast he ran, he couldn't catch a man who was only walking. Stop, monk! he shouted. The Buddha said: I have stopped. It's you who hasn't. That landed. Angulimala threw down his sword, and became a monk. The Buddha taught that karma isn't payback. It's action. And, he said, intention is what I call karma. The choice is the seed. So when a friend texts karma! about someone else, flip it. What am I planting, right now?
