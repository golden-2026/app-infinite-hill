# Side by side: our lesson next to a strong outside resource

Four famous stories, one from each of four traditions. For each: what our lesson actually says (the spoken hook and
teaching, word for word from the lesson script), and the outside resources to compare it with. We name and link those
resources; we don't copy them. Please check each link works before sending this out.

Our lessons are about 6 minutes. Most benchmarks are longer, so the fair question isn't "which has more?", it's
"minute for minute, which teaches better, and which would you come back to?"

## How to run the blind comparison (test users)

1. Pick one topic that fits the person (their own tradition, or one they're curious about).
2. Prepare two things, labelled **A** and **B** at random (flip a coin): our lesson (play it in the app, or hand them
   the text below) and one benchmark (a video segment of 6 to 10 minutes, or the same passage in the named
   translation). Don't say which one is ours, or that either is.
3. They take in both, then answer the questions below. Write down their answers, then tell them which was which and
   ask question 8.

### The questions

1. Which one did you understand better? (A / B / same)
2. Which one would you remember next week? What exactly would you remember from each?
3. Which felt more trustworthy, like it knew what it was talking about? Why?
4. Did either one feel preachy, shallow, or like an outsider talking about the tradition? Which, and where?
5. Which one made you think about your own life? Was that welcome or annoying?
6. If you could only have one of these every morning for a month, which? (A / B)
7. Anything in either that seemed wrong or left out something important?
8. (After the reveal) Knowing which was ours: what should ours borrow from the other?

Record: topic, which was A, answers 1 and 6 as A/B/same, and one line each for 2 to 8.

## 1. Rama's exile (Hinduism, our day 40, "Fourteen years")

**Line to carry:** accept, then walk. **Sources the lesson cites:** Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda 16 to 21;
Bhagavad Gita 2.47.

**Our hook:**
> Rama wakes up on his coronation morning. The city is decorated for him. The priests are ready. The water for the
> ceremony has been gathered from sacred rivers. A messenger comes: your father wants to see you. Rama walks into
> Kaikeyi's rooms expecting a blessing, and finds his father on the floor, unable to look at him, unable to speak.
> Kaikeyi speaks instead. Calmly, she tells him everything. Bharata will be king. You will go to the forest, dressed as a
> hermit, for fourteen years. Your father promised. Everyone in the room waits to see what Rama will do.

**Our teaching:**
> Rama says, in effect: so be it. I'll go. He doesn't argue. He doesn't bargain. He tells Kaikeyi he would have given
> the kingdom to Bharata gladly if anyone had simply asked. His only sorrow is that his father is in pain. Then he walks
> out. Valmiki notices one detail, and people have repeated it ever since: Rama's face didn't change. It looked the same
> when he heard he'd be king, and when he heard he'd be exiled. Now, that isn't coldness. The people around him are
> anything but calm. His mother Kausalya is devastated. His brother Lakshmana is furious and ready to fight their father
> for the throne. Rama is the steady one, and he talks Lakshmana down. Why does he accept? Because of dharma, your third
> word: doing what's yours to do. For Rama, keeping his father's word is his duty, even when it costs him everything.
> That's why tradition calls him maryada purushottam: the ideal person, who keeps within the bounds of right action. Not
> everyone finds this easy. Some readers ask whether Rama should have fought, and the tradition lets them ask. But the
> scene draws a line worth keeping. There is a difference between giving up and accepting. Giving up sits down.
> Accepting stands up and starts walking. Rama doesn't pretend it's fair. He just refuses to let the unfairness decide
> who he is. The Gita's line is hiding here too: the action is yours, the fruit isn't. Accept, then walk.

(Year 2 retells the same events slowly over several days, 520 to 527, if a reviewer wants the deeper version.)

**Benchmarks to compare against:**
- **The text itself:** the Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, in Ralph T. H. Griffith's public-domain translation
  (1870 to 1874), on sacred-texts.com (https://sacred-texts.com/hin/rama/), or verse by verse with Sanskrit and English
  at IIT Kanpur's Valmiki Ramayana site (https://www.valmiki.iitk.ac.in/).
- **A trusted retelling:** C. Rajagopalachari's *Ramayana* (Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan), the retelling many Indian families
  grew up with; or Arshia Sattar's abridged translation of Valmiki (Penguin). Books: name only, read the exile chapter.
- **What children actually meet:** Amar Chitra Katha's *Rama* comic, the most familiar popular version in many Hindu
  homes.

**What to look for:** does our lesson catch what the text dwells on (the long laments of Dasharatha and Kausalya,
Lakshmana's anger, Sita insisting on coming)? Is "accept, then walk" fair to a scene the tradition has argued over?

## 2. The Prodigal Son (Christianity, our day 558, "The younger son (Luke 15:11 to 24)")

**Line to carry:** while he was still far off. (Catholic door covers the same story on day 47, "The son who left".)

**Our hook:**
> Have you ever rehearsed an apology? In the car, in the shower, at three in the morning. You polish every line. You
> plan what you'll say if they shout, and what you'll say if they say nothing. You lower your expectations so you won't
> be disappointed. Today's story has a young man doing exactly that, on a long walk home. People call it the prodigal
> son, though Luke never gives it a title. Jesus just begins: a certain man had two sons. Today, the younger one.

**Our teaching:**
> The younger son asks for his share of the inheritance now, while his father is alive. Some commentators say that in
> that world it was close to saying I wish you were dead. The father divides it anyway. The son goes to a far country
> and wastes it all. Then famine. He ends up feeding pigs, animals his own people's law counted unclean, and in the King
> James Bible, "he would fain have filled his belly with the husks that the swine did eat: and no man gave unto him." As
> low as he can go. Then a turning point: "when he came to himself." Woke up, we'd say. And he works out a speech.
> "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of
> thy hired servants." And a plan: he won't ask to be a son again, just a worker who gets paid. Then the line that turns
> the whole story: "But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on
> his neck, and kissed him." He saw him a great way off. Some readers think he'd been watching the road. And he ran. Some
> commentators note that grown men of standing didn't run in public. The father doesn't care. The son starts the speech.
> Father, I have sinned, I'm no more worthy to be called your son. And before he can get to make me a hired servant, the
> father interrupts, calling to the servants: "Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand,
> and shoes on his feet." The plan to be a servant never gets said. Robe, ring and shoes: you don't put those on a
> worker. You put them on a son. And the father says why: "this my son was dead, and is alive again."

**Benchmarks to compare against:**
- **The text itself:** Luke 15:11 to 32 in the King James Version or the World English Bible (both public domain).
- **A popular video:** BibleProject (https://bibleproject.com), whose free animated videos on the Gospel of Luke are
  among the most watched Bible-teaching videos online. Use their Luke overview segment that covers Luke 15.
- **A university lecture:** Yale Open Courses, *Introduction to New Testament History and Literature* (Dale Martin,
  RLST 152), free at https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152. Use the lecture on Luke.
- **A classic reflection:** Henri Nouwen's *The Return of the Prodigal Son* (book; name only), built on Rembrandt's
  painting, which our door uses on day 991.

**What to look for:** our lesson stops at verse 24 and leaves the elder brother for the next day. Does the split weaken
it? Is "some commentators note that grown men of standing didn't run" handled as carefully as a scholar would?

## 3. The Exodus and Passover (Judaism, our day 87, "The night of the firstborn (Exodus 11 to 12)")

**Line to carry:** this night is different. (The seder itself is taught on day 135.)

**Our hook:**
> Every spring, at a table full of family, the youngest child who can read stands up, sometimes nervous, sometimes
> showing off, and sings a question. Ma nishtanah halailah hazeh mikol haleilot? Why is this night different from all
> other nights? Why the flat bread? Why the bitter herbs? Why do we dip, and lean? And the whole long evening that
> follows is the answer. It's called the seder, and it retells one night. Today: the night of the firstborn, the night
> the Torah calls Pesach.

**Our teaching:**
> Moses warns Pharaoh of one final blow: at midnight, every firstborn in Egypt will die. Then God gives the Israelites
> instructions, and they're very specific. Each household takes a lamb. On the fourteenth day of the month, they
> slaughter it, and put some of its blood on the two doorposts and the top of the door, with a bunch of hyssop. They
> roast it and eat it that night, with flat bread and bitter herbs, dressed for travel, shoes on, staff in hand, in a
> hurry. And no one is to go out of the house until morning. God says: "when I see the blood, I will pass over you."
> That's Pesach. From a Hebrew root that means to pass over, or, some say, to hover over, protecting. At midnight it
> happens. There's a great cry in Egypt, the text says, for there was not a house where there was not one dead. It's a
> terrible night. The Torah doesn't hide it, and Jewish tradition doesn't treat it as simple; we'll come to the spilled
> wine and the silenced angels later this week. And in the middle of the story, before anyone has even left, God tells
> them to make this night a memorial forever. It'll be retold. And the Torah says your children will ask: "What mean ye
> by this service?" And the commandment is: "And thou shalt tell thy son in that day." That's why a child asks the
> questions at the seder. Freedom isn't only something that happened. It's something you tell, out loud, to the next
> person, every year.

**Benchmarks to compare against:**
- **The text with commentary:** Sefaria (https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.12), free, with the JPS 1917 translation and
  Rashi's commentary side by side.
- **A popular explainer:** My Jewish Learning's "Passover 101" articles (https://www.myjewishlearning.com) or
  Chabad.org's Passover guide (https://www.chabad.org); both are what many Jews actually read before a seder.
- **A university lecture:** Yale Open Courses, *Introduction to the Old Testament (Hebrew Bible)* (Christine Hayes,
  RLST 145), free at https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-145. Use the lecture covering Exodus.
- **A popular video:** BibleProject's Exodus overview video (https://bibleproject.com). Note it's made by Christians;
  ask Jewish testers whether that shows.

**What to look for:** does our lesson give the death of the firstborn the moral weight Jewish commentary gives it, or
defer it too neatly to "later this week"? Is the Haggadah connection (the child's questions) the strongest hook?

## 4. The Buddha's first sermon (Buddhism, our day 62, "The first sermon")

**Line to carry:** the wheel turns. (Year 2 spends days 333 to 344 on the four truths in detail.)

**Our hook:**
> First speeches often vanish. The opening lecture of a new science, the first talk of a movement: nobody wrote it down,
> nobody knew it would matter. Buddhist tradition says it kept this one. Five men in a grove near Benares, and a man
> they once thought had given up is about to speak. They've agreed to listen. What he says is short, and monks still
> chant it. It sets out a whole path in a few plain lists. Its name is today's word.

**Our teaching:**
> Dhammacakka. Dhamma is the teaching, the way things are. Cakka is a wheel. The first sermon is called the
> Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta: the teaching that set the wheel of the dhamma turning. Some readers hear in it the old
> Indian picture of a wheel-turning king, whose rule rolls out across the land. Here the wheel isn't an army. It's an
> idea set in motion. He starts, of all places, with what to avoid. There are two extremes, he tells the five. One is
> chasing pleasure, living for the senses. The other is punishing yourself, starving, torturing the body. These five know
> that second road well; they walked it with him. Neither one works, he says. There is a middle way. Then come the four
> noble truths. First: there is dukkha, the unsatisfactoriness in life: birth, aging, sickness, losing what you love,
> getting what you don't want. Second: it has a cause, craving, the thirst that keeps reaching. Third: it can end. When
> the thirst is let go, the suffering it feeds goes too. Fourth: there's a path to that end, and he lays it out in eight
> parts: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness,
> right concentration. Notice what kind of teaching this is. It isn't a creed about gods or a story about the beginning
> of the world. Some readers hear it as a doctor's visit: here's the illness, here's the cause, here's the good news that
> it can heal, here's the treatment. And the wheel shows up today in surprising places. There's one in the middle of
> India's flag. It comes from the time of Ashoka, an Indian emperor who supported the Buddhist community, and whose stone
> pillar stood at the Deer Park itself.

**Benchmarks to compare against:**
- **The text itself:** Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 on SuttaCentral (https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11), Bhikkhu Sujato's
  translation, released into the public domain; or Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation on Access to Insight
  (https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.than.html), free for distribution.
- **A teacher's explanation:** Ajahn Sumedho's *The Four Noble Truths* (a free booklet from Amaravati Buddhist
  Monastery), or Thich Nhat Hanh's *The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching* (book; name only).
- **A scholar's introduction:** Rupert Gethin, *The Foundations of Buddhism* (Oxford; name only), chapter on the four
  truths.

**What to look for:** the sutta's own key move is that each truth has a task (to be understood, abandoned, realized,
developed). Our day 62 leaves that to year 2. Is a "list of four plus eight" on day 62 too much like a textbook? Does
the India-flag ending add or distract?
