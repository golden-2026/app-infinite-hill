# Christianity: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Christianity #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Christianity #1: "you've said it a thousand times" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** amen
- **The promise on the card:** you've said it a thousand times — at a table, at a funeral
- **Line to carry:** let it be so
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> You've said this word more times than you could count. At the end of grace, right before anyone's allowed to eat. At a funeral, quietly, along with everyone else. At the end of a song in a church you were only visiting. Maybe as a joke — amen to that. It sounds almost the same from one language to the next, and it's easy to hear it as the end. The prayer's over. You can open your eyes now. It doesn't mean that. It means something closer to the opposite. Today, in a few minutes, you find out what you've been agreeing to.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Amen is Hebrew. It comes from a root, aman, that means firm. Solid. The kind of thing you can put your weight on. The same root gives Hebrew its words for faithfulness and for trust. So when someone says amen, they aren't signing off. They're saying: yes. That's solid. I'll stand on that. Truly. So be it. It's old. In the book of Deuteronomy, a list of rules is read out loud to a whole people, and after each one the text says: "And all the people shall answer and say, Amen." A room answering yes. That still happens in church services today. Someone says something, and the room agrees out loud. Then comes a detail that's easy to miss. In the Gospels, Jesus uses the word in a strange place. Not at the end of what he says. At the start. The old King James Bible puts it this way: "Verily, verily, I say unto you." Underneath verily is the same word. Amen, amen. The Gospel of John has him say it like that again and again. Usually amen came after, to agree with something already said. He said it first, like a man vouching for his words before he spoke them. And the last book of the Bible goes one step further. It calls him "the Amen, the faithful and true witness." Not just someone who says yes. The yes itself.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Breathe in slowly, all the way. And on the way out, if you want to, say the word, out loud or just in your head. Amen. Not to end anything. To agree with something. You're here. You showed up. That's worth a yes.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Amen. Truly. So be it. The word you've said your whole life, and now you know it's a yes, not a goodbye. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: let it be so. Once today, when someone says something true — a friend, a stranger, a song — notice it, and say yes to it inside. That's day one. You showed up. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- amen = so be it · truly
- aman = firm, solid
- verily, verily = amen, amen
- the Amen = a name for Jesus in Revelation

*True or myth?*

- "amen means "the end"" - **myth**: it means "truly" or "so be it." it's a yes, not a sign-off.
- "Jesus sometimes started sentences with amen" - **true**: "verily, verily, I say unto you": again and again in John's Gospel.
- "amen comes from Latin" - **myth**: it's Hebrew, from aman: firm, solid. Greek and Latin kept it untranslated.

*The fork (what would you do?):* in Deuteronomy, rules are read aloud to a whole people. after each one, what does the crowd do?

1. stays silent until the end.
2. answers "amen" after every one. **(right answer)**
3. repeats the rule back.

Reveal: "and all the people shall answer and say, Amen." a room answering yes, line by line. church services still work that way.

*In the original language:* אָמֵן (say: ah-MEN). Hebrew. the Greek Gospels carry it over untranslated, and so do many languages after them.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: the end of a prayer.
- what it means: truly · so be it — you're agreeing with what was just said.
- what a scholar hears: the root aman — firm, faithful. Revelation 3:14 turns the word into a name: "the Amen, the faithful and true witness."

**How it's done (info card):** Christians say amen at the end of prayers, hymns and blessings, usually all together, as a way of saying "yes, we agree" to what was just prayed.

**Sources cited:** Deuteronomy 27:15 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 5:24 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Gospel of John (the double-amen sayings, e.g. 1:51, 3:3, 5:24) (The Bible); Revelation 3:14 (The Bible, KJV (1769))

## Christianity #2: ""forgive us as we forgive"" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** forgive
- **The promise on the card:** "forgive us as we forgive" — the one clause with a condition in it
- **Line to carry:** let something go
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, line three: give us this day our daily bread. Just today's, and for all of us. Your line was enough for today. Did you notice when there already was enough? Now, line four. Day seven.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Read the fine print. Contracts have it: the little clause that says, this only works if. Now look at the Lord's Prayer. Its lines are plain requests. Your kingdom come. Give us bread. Deliver us. But one line has a condition built right into it, and it's the line we're on today. Forgive us, as we forgive. It's a strange thing to pray, if you stop and listen. It asks to be forgiven the same way we forgive other people. Which means the person who cut you off, the friend who never paid you back, the sibling who said the thing, all of them just walked into the prayer. Line four of five.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> In the old King James Bible, Matthew chapter six, it reads: "And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors." Debts. Money language. Jesus spoke Aramaic, and scholars point out that in Aramaic one word could mean a debt or a sin: something you owe. So forgiving is like tearing up an IOU. Not collecting. Churches today say the line in different words. Some say debts. Others say trespasses, the word in the old Book of Common Prayer: "forgive us our trespasses." Luke's version says sins. Same idea, three doors into it. Now the Greek verb for forgive. It's aphiemi, and its first meaning is to let go. To release. To send away, the way you'd open your hand and let a bird fly off. Forgiving, in this word, isn't pretending it didn't happen, and Christian teachers often add that it isn't the same as trusting someone again. It's letting go of the debt. Then a detail in Matthew. When Jesus finishes teaching the prayer in Matthew, he goes back and comments on only one line. This one. "For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." Out of the whole prayer, that's the part he underlines. Christians have wrestled with it ever since. One long-standing reading: God's forgiveness is a gift, never a wage, and a forgiven person becomes someone who forgives. Read any way, the two go together. You can't hold your hand open to receive and keep it clenched at the same time. And this is day seven, the end of a week. Amen, grace, Jesus, our Father, the kingdom, bread, and now: let it go.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths with the bell. On the first, make a fist, and think of one small thing someone owes you. Not the biggest wound. Something small. On the second, keep holding it, and notice how tight that feels. On the third, open your hand as you breathe out. If you pray, you can say the line: forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. If you don't, just let the hand stay open.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Line four of the Lord's Prayer. Forgive us as we forgive: let go of the debt, the way you hope yours are let go. Seventh word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: let something go. One small thing today. The late reply, the dish left in the sink, the comment from last week. Open the hand on just one. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("the fourth line — tap the pairs."):

- aphiemi = to let go, to release
- debts = tearing up the IOU
- trespasses = the Book of Common Prayer's word
- Luke's version = says sins

*True or myth?*

- "every church says "trespasses" in the Lord's Prayer" - **myth**: some say debts, others say trespasses, and Luke's version says sins. same idea, three doors.
- "the Greek word for forgive first means to let go" - **true**: aphiemi: to release, to send away, to open the hand.
- "Jesus comments on every line of the prayer afterward" - **myth**: in Matthew he goes back to only one line: forgiving.

*The fork (what would you do?):* Jesus has just finished teaching the Lord's Prayer in Matthew. he goes back to comment on one line. which one?

1. thy kingdom come.
2. give us this day our daily bread.
3. forgive us, as we forgive. **(right answer)**

Reveal: "for if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." of the whole prayer, forgiving is the part he underlines.

*In the original language:* ἄφες (say: AH-fes). Greek: forgive, let go — the command form of aphiemi, the word in "forgive us" (Matthew 6:12).

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: saying sorry to God.
- what it means: letting go of what's owed, both ways. forgive us, as we forgive.
- what a scholar hears: in Aramaic, scholars note, one word could mean a debt or a sin. Matthew kept the money word; Luke wrote sins, then kept the debt in the next half: everyone indebted to us.

**How it's done (info card):** Christians pray this line every time they say the Lord's Prayer. Some churches say "debts," others "trespasses," from the Book of Common Prayer, and some use Luke's "sins."

**Sources cited:** Matthew 6:12 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Matthew 6:14 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Luke 11:4 (The Bible); The Lord's Prayer, Morning Prayer (Book of Common Prayer, Book of Common Prayer 1662)

## Christianity #3: "Lazarus, come out (John 11:1–44)" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Lazarus
- **The promise on the card:** Jesus weeps at his friend's tomb, then calls him out
- **Line to carry:** jesus wept
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Zacchaeus up a tree, called down by name. Your line was he knows your name. What did you make right? Today, a friend's name called into a tomb. Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Think of the phone call nobody wants. Someone you love is sick, and you're far away, and you have to decide how fast to come. Now go to Bethany, a village just outside Jerusalem. Two sisters, Martha and Mary, send word to Jesus about their brother Lazarus. Their words, in the King James Bible, are simple: "Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick." Not our brother. The one you love. They were close friends. And John says something puzzling: Jesus loved them, and so he stayed where he was two more days. By the time he arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. Today's word: Lazarus.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Martha hears he's coming and runs out to meet him on the road, and she's honest: if you had been here, my brother wouldn't have died. And he tells her: "I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live." Then Mary comes, and she falls at his feet and says the very same words: if you had been here. She's weeping, and everyone with her is weeping. And John says he was deeply moved and troubled. He asks, where have you laid him? They say, come and see. And then the shortest verse in the English Bible, two words: "Jesus wept." Think about that. He knows what he's about to do. He's about to call Lazarus out. And he still weeps. He doesn't skip past the grief to the happy ending. He stands in it with his friends. The people watching say: "Behold how he loved him!" At the tomb he says, take away the stone, and practical Martha objects, in the King James words: "Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath been dead four days." They roll it away anyway. He prays aloud, then shouts: "Lazarus, come forth." And the dead man walks out, still wrapped hand and foot in grave clothes, his face bound with a cloth. And the last line is almost tender in its plainness: "Loose him, and let him go." Somebody has to unwrap him. In John's Gospel this is the last and greatest sign before the cross, and it's the one that makes the authorities decide he must die. The name Lazarus comes from the Hebrew Eleazar: God has helped.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice. Sit, and let your shoulders drop. Is there one thing you haven't let yourself cry about? A loss, a disappointment, a door that closed, a person who's gone. Something you moved past too quickly because there was too much to do. Let it come to mind. You don't have to force tears. Just stop holding it back. If tears come, let them. Jesus wept at a tomb he was about to open. Grief isn't a lack of faith. If you pray, you can bring the grief as it is. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Lazarus. From the Hebrew Eleazar, God has helped. A friend four days in the tomb, wept over, and called out by name.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: jesus wept. Today, let yourself feel one grief instead of rushing past it, and if someone near you is grieving, don't hurry them either. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("who said what — tap the pairs."):

- the sisters' message = he whom thou lovest is sick
- Martha = by this time he stinketh
- the onlookers = behold how he loved him
- Jesus at the tomb = Lazarus, come forth

*True or myth?*

- "Jesus hurried to Bethany the moment he heard" - **myth**: John says he stayed where he was two more days.
- "Jesus wept is the shortest verse in the King James Bible" - **true**: two words, John 11:35.
- "Lazarus came out already unwrapped" - **myth**: he came out bound hand and foot. Jesus said: loose him, and let him go.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Martha. Jesus tells you to take away the stone from your brother's tomb. he's been dead four days. what do you say?

1. yes, right away.
2. Lord, by this time he stinketh. **(right answer)**
3. nothing, and walk away.

Reveal: practical Martha objects: he hath been dead four days. they roll the stone away anyway, and Lazarus comes out.

*In the original language:* ἐδάκρυσεν (say: eh-DAH-kroo-sen). Greek: he shed tears. A quieter word than the loud wailing of the mourners around him.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: jesus wept is the shortest verse, a trivia answer.
- what it means: he weeps at a grave he's about to open, and stands in the grief with his friends instead of skipping past it.
- what a scholar hears: John uses dakryo, quiet tears, for Jesus, and klaio, loud mourning, for Mary and the crowd, and in John this sign is what leads the authorities to plan his death.

**How it's done (info card):** Many churches read the raising of Lazarus near the end of Lent, and Orthodox Christians keep Lazarus Saturday the day before Palm Sunday. Jesus's words I am the resurrection, and the life are read at many Christian funerals.

**Sources cited:** John 11:3 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:25 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:35 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:36 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:39 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:43 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:44 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); John 11:1–2, 5–6, 17–21, 32–34, 41–42, 53 (The Bible)

## Christianity #4: "Without honor at home (Mark 6:1–6)" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Without honor at home
- **The promise on the card:** his hometown asks, isn't this the carpenter? and he can do little there
- **Line to carry:** isn't this the carpenter?
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, a small room, a small hand, and two Aramaic words: talitha koum, little girl, get up. Did you feed someone? Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> You know what it's like to go home for the holidays. You've changed. You've learned things, done things, maybe become someone new. And within an hour at the table, you're twelve again. Somebody brings up the time you broke the window. Your cousin still calls you by the old nickname. That's Jesus, today. He's healed crowds, calmed a storm, raised a child. And he walks back into Nazareth, the village he grew up in, and teaches in the synagogue on the sabbath. And the people who watched him grow up can't quite get past who he used to be. Today: without honor at home.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> At first they're amazed. Where did he get all this? Then, in the old King James Bible, the questions turn: "Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary?" They name his brothers, James, Joses, Judas and Simon, and say his sisters are right here with us. "And they were offended at him." Mark is the only gospel that calls Jesus himself the carpenter. Matthew says the carpenter's son. The Greek word is tekton, a builder, someone who works with wood and maybe stone. Doors, roofs, plows, yokes. Jesus worked with his hands, probably for most of his adult life, in a village of a few hundred people where everyone knew the builder. That's exactly the problem. How can the man who might have fixed our door be a prophet? Jesus answers with a line that became a proverb: "A prophet is not without honour, but in his own country, and among his own kin, and in his own house." Then comes one of the most striking sentences in Mark: "he could there do no mighty work, save that he laid his hands upon a few sick folk, and healed them." Could. Not would. Matthew, telling the same story, says he did not do many. And one more line: "he marvelled because of their unbelief." Usually the crowds marvel at him. Here he marvels at them. Early copyists seem to have found it uncomfortable too: some manuscripts of Mark change the question to the son of the carpenter. Many Christians read the scene as a sign of how much trust matters, and how familiarity can close a door. And many find it comforting that the Son of God had a hometown that didn't get him, and a trade, and calluses.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: be kind to someone you've known forever. Settle, and breathe. Think of someone you've known so long you've stopped really seeing them. A sibling. A parent. An old friend. A spouse. The coworker who's been there longer than the furniture. Notice the old label you carry for them. Then ask what might be true about them now that you've missed. Something they've learned, or carried, or become. If you pray, you can pray for them by name. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Without honor at home. Jesus in Nazareth, among the people who knew him as the carpenter, and couldn't see past it.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: isn't this the carpenter? Today, do one kind thing for someone you've known forever, and let them be someone new to you. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the story — tap the pairs."):

- the hometown = is not this the carpenter?
- tekton = a builder with his hands
- a prophet = without honor in his own country
- Jesus = marveled because of their unbelief

*True or myth?*

- "only Mark calls Jesus himself the carpenter" - **true**: Matthew calls him the carpenter's son.
- "in Nazareth Jesus healed no one at all" - **myth**: Mark says he laid his hands on a few sick folk and healed them.
- "Mark says he could do no mighty work there" - **true**: could, not would. Matthew says he did not do many.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you've come back to your hometown to teach, and the people who watched you grow up take offense. what does Jesus do?

1. leave that day and never return.
2. heal a few, and marvel at their unbelief. **(right answer)**
3. perform a great sign to convince them.

Reveal: he could do no mighty work there, except heal a few sick folk, and he marveled because of their unbelief.

*In the original language:* τέκτων (say: TEK-tone). Greek: a builder or craftsman, working in wood and sometimes stone.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: Jesus was a carpenter because Joseph was.
- what it means: his neighbors knew him as the village builder, and couldn't see past it.
- what a scholar hears: only Mark calls Jesus the tekton, and only Mark says he could do no mighty work there. Matthew softens both.

**How it's done (info card):** Many Christians honor Jesus's years as a working craftsman, and Catholics keep a feast of Saint Joseph the Worker on May 1; churches often draw on this story when talking about faith at home and at work.

**Sources cited:** Mark 6:3 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Mark 6:3 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Mark 6:4 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Mark 6:5 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Mark 6:6 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); Matthew 13:55, 58 (The Bible)

## Christianity #5: "The Word made flesh (John 1:14)" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** incarnation
- **The promise on the card:** God became human, born and hungry and tired
- **Line to carry:** the word became flesh
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the Trinity, and three angels at one table with a place left open. Your line was god is a communion. Who did you pull in to the table? Day three hundred.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Day three hundred. Think about your body today. Maybe you woke up stiff. Maybe you were hungry by eleven. Maybe your feet hurt, or your eyes are tired from a screen, or you just need a nap. Ordinary, slightly embarrassing, completely human things. Now here's a thought that has startled people for two thousand years. Christians say that God knows exactly what that feels like. Not from a distance, like a doctor reading a chart. From the inside. That God was once a baby who needed feeding in the middle of the night, a boy who scraped his knees, a man who walked until his feet were sore and sat down by a well because he was worn out. Today's word is the name for that claim, and the Gospel of John puts it in one short line.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Incarnation comes from Latin: in, plus caro, carnis, flesh. The same root as carnival and chili con carne. In-flesh-ment. John's Gospel begins with a poem about the Word, the one who was with God and was God, through whom everything was made. And then, in verse fourteen, the poem lands with a thud: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." Flesh. John could have said body, or human nature. He chose the plainest, most earthy word there is. The Greek is sarx: meat, skin, the part of you that gets hungry and bleeds. And dwelt, in Greek, is literally pitched his tent. God camped among us. The Gospels don't flinch from it. Jesus is born and laid in a feeding trough. After forty days in the desert, Matthew says simply, he was hungry. In John, "Jesus therefore, being wearied with his journey, sat thus on the well." He sleeps through a storm in a boat. At his friend's grave, in the shortest verse in the King James Bible: "Jesus wept." Christians of every church see the incarnation as the center of the whole story. Christmas is its feast. It means God didn't fix the world from outside. God came in. And it means your body, with its hunger and its tiredness, isn't beneath God. It was worth becoming. Early Christians put it this way: God took on everything human, so that everything human could be healed. Tomorrow, we'll see how the church held that together with the other half: that this tired man by the well was also, fully, God.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: honor your body today. Settle, and breathe. Start at your feet, and slowly move your attention upward: legs, belly, hands, shoulders, face. Wherever there's tiredness or ache, don't fight it. Just notice it, kindly. This body gets hungry and tired, and it's also where your whole life happens. **The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the crowds at the Jordan asking John what shall we do, and his answer: share your coat. Did one thing you had two of find a new home? Day five hundred fourteen.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Luke's family tree runs the wrong way. A family tree usually starts with the oldest name and works down to the youngest. Luke starts with Jesus, about thirty years old, and climbs. Son of Joseph, as was supposed. Son of Heli. Son of Matthat. Back through King David, back through Abraham, back past the flood to Noah, and still he keeps going. Dozens of names, a long ladder of fathers that's easy to skip. But it's worth reading to the very last rung, because of where Luke puts this list, and because of the strange, beautiful word he ends it on.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> First, the setting. People are wondering if John might be the Christ, and he says no: "I indeed baptize you with water; but one mightier than I cometh, the latchet of whose shoes I am not worthy to unloose." Then Jesus is baptized, and Luke adds a detail: Jesus was praying. "The heaven was opened, And the Holy Ghost descended in a bodily shape like a dove upon him, and a voice came from heaven, which said, Thou art my beloved Son; in thee I am well pleased." The voice has just said: my Son. And right there, before anything else happens, Luke drops in the genealogy. Matthew's Gospel has one too, but it begins with Abraham and runs forward, a story of Israel's kings. Luke's runs backward and doesn't stop at Abraham. It ends: "Which was the son of Enos, which was the son of Seth, which was the son of Adam, which was the son of God." The voice at the river says this one is my beloved Son, and the list underneath says he belongs to everyone. The two lists don't match name for name between David and Joseph, and Christians have explained that in different ways: some say Luke follows Mary's family, others say adoption or old family custom explains it. The texts themselves don't say. What Luke does make plain is the shape: from the river all the way back to the garden.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Yesterday, the church in the south, and a young woman walking her brother to a classroom church. Your line was she is the church. Whom did you thank? Day one thousand.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> The first word on this path was amen: let it be so. Then came the stories, the practices, the long reading of the text. A second year opened with in the beginning, and let there be light. A third opened with a promise: I will not fail you. In between, mornings. Some on a bus. Some at a kitchen table before anyone else was up. Some missed or rested, and then a fresh start. A few minutes each. Stacked up, they reach a number worth stopping for. Today: the thousandth morning.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The Bible uses the word thousand in a few surprising places, and none of them is a scoreboard. Psalm 90 carries the heading "A Prayer of Moses the man of God", and it says to God: "For a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past, and as a watch in the night." A thousand years, to God, is one night shift. The second letter of Peter echoes that line and turns it both ways: "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day." So a single morning can carry real weight, and a thousand of them are small in God's hands. Both at once. Psalm 84 weighs it differently again: "For a day in thy courts is better than a thousand." One day near God outweighs a thousand anywhere else. And then the book of Lamentations, a book of grief over a fallen city. In the middle of its grief comes this: "They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." That's the hinge. In these lines the faithfulness belongs to God, not to the count. The morning is new whether yesterday went well or not. A monk chanting the same psalms at dawn, a grandmother saying the same prayer at the kitchen sink, a commuter with headphones on a train: each is building a life out of small, repeated mornings. The long story this year has told was carried that way, again and again. Not by big moments alone. By people who got up and did one small faithful thing, and then did it again.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: nothing new today. Just stillness, and thanks. Settle, and breathe. Let a few mornings from this path come back on their own: a word that stuck, a story, the place you were sitting when you heard it. Don't sort them or grade them. Then let one thank-you rise, for anything at all: a person, a line, the plain fact of having come back. If you pray, you can give thanks in your own words, or with the psalm: a day in thy courts is better than a thousand. If you don't, just let the thanks be there, quietly. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The thousandth morning. A thousand days of a few minutes each. In the psalm, a thousand years are as yesterday in God's sight; in Lamentations, mercies are new every morning.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: a thousand mornings. Today, tell one person one thing you've learned on this path, in a single sentence, over coffee or by message. Day one thousand.

*True or myth?*

- "in Psalm 90, a thousand years in God's sight are like yesterday" - **true**: "as yesterday when it is past, and as a watch in the night."
- "2 Peter only says a thousand years are as one day" - **myth**: it runs both ways: "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years" as well.
- "Lamentations says God's mercies are new every year" - **myth**: every morning: "they are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."

*The fork (what would you do?):* the psalmist weighs one day near God against a thousand days anywhere else. which does Psalm 84 choose?

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a thousand days is a score to be proud of.

**How it's done (info card):** Some Christians keep a short daily time of prayer or reading, often in the morning, and some pray set psalms at fixed hours, as monks and nuns do, returning to the same words day after day.
