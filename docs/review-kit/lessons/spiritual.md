# Simply Spiritual: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Simply Spiritual #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Simply Spiritual #1: "every tradition starts here" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** breathe
- **The promise on the card:** every tradition starts here; before the words, before the books
- **Line to carry:** someone is in there — say hi
- **Length:** about 5:30 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Before anything else, notice something. You're breathing. You were breathing before you opened this, you'll be breathing after you close it, and it mostly runs without being asked. Tradition after tradition starts here. Before the books, before the words, before anyone argued about anything, there was a person sitting still, feeling the air go in and out. Monks do it. Grandmothers do it at the kitchen table. Basketball players do it before a free throw. This path is for people who don't belong to one religion, or who belong to a few, or who aren't sure yet. So we start with the one thing every single one of us already does. Today's word is breathe.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Here's the surprise hiding inside it. In several old languages, breath and spirit share a single word. In Latin, spiritus means breath. It comes from spirare, to breathe. That's where English gets spirit, and also inspire, which once meant to breathe into, and expire, to breathe out for the last time. In Greek, pneuma means wind, breath and spirit. In Hebrew, ruach does the same. In Sanskrit, the old language of India, prana is breath and the life that rides on it. In Chinese thought there's qi, often put into English as breath or vital energy. Each of these words grew up in its own language, and each tradition tells its own story about it. But each one looked at the same thing, a body that breathes and a body that doesn't, and named the difference with the word for air. The Bible tells it near the start of Genesis. In the King James words: "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." That's one tradition's picture, and it deserves to be heard as its own. There's a plainer side too. Breath is the one part of your body that runs by itself and also takes orders. You can't slow your heart just by deciding to. You can slow your breath, and a long, easy breath out tends to settle the body. That part needs no belief to work. So a word for spirit turns out to be something you can practice on a bus.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Sit however you're sitting. Breathe in slowly, all the way, and feel where the air goes: nose, chest, belly. Then let it out, a little longer than it came in. That's all. Nothing to believe, nothing to say. One breath, taken on purpose instead of by accident.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Breathe. The word underneath spirit, in Latin and Greek, in Hebrew and Sanskrit. The thing you've done since before you had a name. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: someone is in there — say hi. Three times today, whenever you remember, take one slow breath and notice who's doing the breathing. No answer needed. Just say hi. That's day one. You showed up. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("breath, in four languages — tap the pairs."):

- spiritus = Latin: breath, later spirit
- pneuma = Greek: wind, breath, spirit
- ruach = Hebrew: breath, wind, spirit
- prana = Sanskrit: breath, and the life in it

*True or myth?*

- "'spirit' and 'breath' come from the same word" - **true**: Latin spiritus, from spirare, to breathe. so do 'inspire' and 'expire.'
- "you can slow your heart just by deciding to" - **myth**: not directly. you can slow your breath, and a long breath out tends to settle the body.
- "in Genesis, God breathes life into the first human" - **true**: "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7).

*The fork (what would you do?):* in Genesis 2, God has shaped a human from the dust of the ground. it doesn't move yet. what comes next?

1. a word of command.
2. a breath, into the nostrils. **(right answer)**
3. a flash of light.

Reveal: "and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." in that story, life starts with a breath.

*In the original language:* spiritus (say: SPEER-ih-toos). Latin, from spirare, to breathe. English spirit, inspire and expire all come from it.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: air in, air out.
- what it means: the one thing you've done since before you had a name.
- what a scholar hears: Latin spiritus comes from spirare, to breathe; Greek pneuma and Hebrew ruach each carry breath, wind and spirit in one word.

**How it's done (info card):** Many paths begin with the breath: Buddhist and Hindu teachers teach watching the breath, yoga has breathing practices called pranayama, and many people of every faith take one slow breath before they pray or sit.

**Sources cited:** Genesis 2:7 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); spiritus / spirare (Latin word history); pneuma (Greek), ruach (Hebrew), prana (Sanskrit), qi (Chinese) (word histories); slow breathing and a longer breath out calm the body (physiology (general, well established))

## Simply Spiritual #2: "not you" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** thoughts
- **The promise on the card:** not you; weather
- **Line to carry:** let it pass
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday's word: body. Your line: come back down. How many times did you catch yourself up in your head? Every catch counts. Day seven, the end of your first week.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Here's a thought for you: you left the stove on. Did you? Probably not. But for half a second, your stomach believed it. Thoughts arrive like that all day, uninvited, sounding completely sure of themselves. One large study asked people through their phones, at random moments, what they were doing and what they were thinking about. Their minds were somewhere else almost half the time. Two words help with that. Not you. Weather. Today's word is thoughts.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> In 1890, William James described something everyone lives with and almost nobody had put well. He called it the stream of thought. Consciousness, he wrote, "is nothing jointed; it flows." A river, not a row of boxes. Thoughts come and go on it, and you didn't send most of them. Epictetus, the Greek teacher we met on day three, put attention to thoughts to a different use, nearly two thousand years earlier. In George Long's translation: "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things." For Epictetus the trouble is less the event than the judgment we add on top of it, and that judgment, he taught, is ours to examine. Many Buddhist teachers, especially in the Tibetan tradition, use a picture you may find helpful: the mind is like the sky, and thoughts are like weather. Clouds, storms, bright spells. They move through. The sky isn't the clouds, and it isn't harmed by them. Three voices, three pictures: a stream, an opinion you can question, weather in the sky. They don't say the same thing, and they don't need to. But each one, in its own way, treats a thought as something that happens in you, not the whole of you. And look at the week you just had. Breathe. Sit. Notice. The golden rule. The bell. Body. Thoughts. Seven words, and nearly every one a way to step back an inch from the stream and see it moving. That's a real week.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths, with the bell. On the first, just breathe. On the second, wait for a thought to show up. It will. When it does, name it quietly: thinking. Don't follow it, and don't fight it. On the third breath, watch it move on, the way weather does. Then you're back here, in the body that's breathing.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Thoughts. Things that happen in you, like weather. Not the whole of you. Seventh word, and the end of week one.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: let it pass. Today, when a thought shows up sounding very sure of itself, say inside: let it pass. Then feel your feet. Seven words on your strand now: breathe, sit, notice, the golden rule, the bell, body, thoughts. That's a week. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("three pictures of a thought — tap the pairs."):

- William James = the stream of thought
- Epictetus = disturbed by opinions, not things
- Tibetan teachers = mind like sky, thoughts like weather
- week one = seven words, starting with breathe

*True or myth?*

- "most people's minds wander only now and then" - **myth**: one large study found minds elsewhere almost half the time.
- "Epictetus said events themselves are what disturb us" - **myth**: "not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things."
- "William James described consciousness as a flowing stream" - **true**: "it is nothing jointed; it flows."

*The fork (what would you do?):* your friend is upset about a rude email. where would Epictetus point first?

1. at the email.
2. at the opinion your friend added to it. **(right answer)**
3. at the person who sent it.

Reveal: "men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things." the email happened; the judgment is the part that's yours to examine.

*In the original language:* δόγματα (say: DOG-ma-ta). Greek, dogmata: Epictetus's word in Enchiridion 5 for the opinions that disturb us. English dogma comes from it.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: the voice in your head, which is you.
- what it means: things that happen in you, like weather in the sky.
- what a scholar hears: chapter IX of James's 1890 Principles is titled "The Stream of Thought"; Enchiridion 5 locates the disturbance in dogmata, opinions.

**How it's done (info card):** In many forms of meditation, people let thoughts come and go without following them, often noting them simply as "thinking" and returning to the breath; Stoic practice examines the judgments behind troubling thoughts instead.

**Sources cited:** The Principles of Psychology, ch. IX (The Stream of Thought) (William James, William James 1890 (original English)); Enchiridion 5 (Epictetus, The Enchiridion, Epictetus, Long 1877); mind as sky, thoughts as weather (Tibetan Buddhist teaching image (general)); Killingsworth & Gilbert, "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind," Science 330 (2010) (psychology study)

## Simply Spiritual #3: "Two pockets" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Two pockets
- **The promise on the card:** Rabbi Simcha Bunim keeps two notes: "for my sake the world was created" and "I am dust and ashes" (Hasidic tale, drawing on Sanhedrin 37a and Genesis 18:27)
- **Line to carry:** reach for the right pocket
- **Length:** about 6:30 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Rabbi Zusya feared one question: why weren't you Zusya? Your line was be zusya. What did you do that only you would do? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Two slips of paper. That's all today needs. On a bad day a person can feel like nothing: small, useless, invisible. On another day, the same person can feel like the center of the universe, the smartest one in the room, owed something by everyone. It's possible to swing between the two in a single afternoon, and to reach for the wrong feeling at the wrong moment. There's a Hasidic teaching for exactly this. It's told of Rabbi Simcha Bunim, a Hasidic master in Poland. He said every person should carry two pockets, each with a slip of paper inside. Today's story is two pockets.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> In one pocket, a note that says: for my sake the world was created. In the other: I am dust and ashes. And the teaching is simply this: know which one to reach for. Both lines come from much older sources. The first is from the Mishnah, in tractate Sanhedrin. It asks why the Bible tells of one single human being created at the beginning, and answers, in part: to teach that a single life counts as a whole world. And so, it says, each person should say: for my sake the world was created. The second line is from Genesis. Abraham is standing before God, bargaining for the city of Sodom, pleading for any good people who might live there. And in the King James words he says he has dared to speak to the Lord, "which am but dust and ashes." Notice: Abraham says it while he is boldly arguing with God. Humility and courage in the same breath. So when do you reach for which? When you feel worthless, when you're crushed, when you think your life doesn't matter: reach for the first pocket. The world was made for you, too. Your life counts as a whole world. And when you feel proud, when you're sure you're above someone, when you're about to be cruel because you can: reach for the second. Dust and ashes. The trick isn't choosing one note forever. It's carrying both, and knowing which the moment needs. Neither is the whole truth alone. Together, they're a person.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: write both notes, one per pocket. If you have paper, tear two small slips. On one, write: for my sake the world was created. On the other: I am dust and ashes. Put one in your left pocket and one in your right. If you don't have paper, hold one hand, then the other. Settle, and breathe. Ask: which pocket does today need? Feel for it. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Two pockets. One note says the world was made for you; the other says you are dust and ashes. Reach for the one the moment needs. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: reach for the right pocket. Today, when you feel small, reach for the world made for you. When you feel too big, reach for the dust. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("which pocket, which source — tap the pairs."):

- for my sake the world was created = the Mishnah, Sanhedrin
- I am dust and ashes = Abraham, in Genesis
- feeling worthless = reach for the world made for you
- feeling above someone = reach for the dust

*True or myth?*

- "Abraham called himself dust and ashes while bowing in silence" - **myth**: he said it while arguing with God, pleading for Sodom.
- "the Mishnah teaches that a single life counts as a whole world" - **true**: so each person should say: for my sake the world was created.
- "the teaching says to keep only the humble note" - **myth**: carry both, and reach for the one the moment needs.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're about to put someone down because you can. which pocket do you reach for?

1. for my sake the world was created.
2. I am dust and ashes. **(right answer)**
3. neither; just keep going.

Reveal: Simcha Bunim's teaching: when pride rises, reach for dust and ashes. when you're crushed, reach for the world made for you.

*In the original language:* בשבילי נברא העולם (say: bish-vee-LEE neev-RAH ha-o-LAHM). Hebrew, bishvili nivra ha-olam: for my sake the world was created, from the Mishnah.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a cute idea about notes in pockets.
- what it means: carry both worth and humility, and use the one the moment needs.
- what a scholar hears: Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 (Talmud 37a) and Genesis 18:27, joined by Simcha Bunim of Peshischa, a Hasidic master of Peshischa in Poland.

**How it's done (info card):** Simcha Bunim's two pockets are taught in Jewish schools and sermons, and some people do carry the two notes; the lines come from Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 and Genesis 18:27.

**Sources cited:** Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5; Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 37a (Mishnah and Talmud); Genesis 18:27 (The Bible, KJV (1769)); the two pockets (traditional Hasidic teaching of Simcha Bunim of Peshischa) (Hasidic tales (oral tradition, collected later))

- Simcha Bunim of Peshischa (Przysucha), 1765–1827: 'early eighteen hundreds' and 'died in 1827'. Confirm.
- Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 paraphrased (created one person to teach that each is a whole world; each should say 'for my sake the world was created'). Confirm the paraphrase.
- Genesis 18:27 quoted from the KJV ('which am but dust and ashes'); JPS 1917 reads 'who am but dust and ashes' and may be preferred by a Jewish Keeper.

## Simply Spiritual #4: "The lion in the well" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The lion in the well
- **The promise on the card:** the hare shows the lion his own reflection in a well, and he leaps at it (Masnavi 1)
- **Line to carry:** that's my face
- **Length:** about 6:30 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the late hare walked slowly to the lion while the lion's rage grew. Your line was the late hare. Where did you take an extra minute? Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Somewhere at work there's a person who drives you up the wall. Always interrupting. Always needing to be right. Always making it about themselves. And maybe, late one night, a small, unwelcome thought slips in: do I do that? It's tempting to slam the door on that thought fast. Rumi doesn't. The hare has arrived, very late, in front of a lion half mad with hunger. Why are you late? the lion roars. And the little hare, bowing, begins his story. Today, the lion in the well.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Here's the hare's story. Your majesty, I set out on time, and another hare came with me as your meal. But on the road, another lion stopped us. I told him, we belong to the king of beasts. He laughed and said, who's your king? I'm the king here. He took my friend, and I barely got away to tell you. The lion is beside himself. Another lion, in my valley? Show me. So the hare leads him to a deep well. At the edge he hangs back and trembles. He's in there, the hare whispers, with my friend. The lion, in some tellings, tucks the hare under his arm for courage, and looks down. And there in the dark water he sees a lion glaring up at him, with a hare in its grip. He lets go of the real hare, roars, and leaps at the enemy. The water closes over him. The little hare runs home to the valley, and the animals rejoice. Now Rumi turns to us. The lion attacked his own face. He took his reflection for a rival and destroyed himself fighting it. And Rumi says: so many of the faults you see in others are your own nature, looking back at you from them. You leap at the reflection and drown in your own anger. He points to a saying of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, that the believer is a mirror to the believer. What bothers you in someone else may be showing you yourself. That doesn't mean every fault you see is yours. Sometimes the coworker really is rude. But it's worth leaning over the well and asking, before you leap: wait. Is that my face?

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: notice one fight with your own reflection. Settle, and breathe. Bring to mind someone who really bothers you. Name the exact thing: they're controlling, or careless, or loud, or always late. Then, gently, lean over the well. Is there a place in your life where you do some version of that too? You don't have to find one. If you do, don't scold yourself. Just say inside: that's my face. And notice what happens to the anger. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The lion in the well. The hare shows the lion his own reflection, and the lion leaps at it and drowns: fighting what is really his own face. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: that's my face. Today, the next time someone irritates you, pause before you leap, and ask if you're looking into the well. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("who did what — tap the pairs."):

- the hare = invents another lion on the road
- the well = where the other lion supposedly hides
- the lion = leaps at his reflection
- the animals = rejoice when the hare comes home

*True or myth?*

- "the hare fights the lion and wins" - **myth**: the hare never fights. he shows the lion his reflection, and the lion leaps at it.
- "Rumi says the faults we see in others can be our own" - **true**: the lion attacked his own face. Rumi says we often do the same.
- "Rumi says every fault you see in others is really yours" - **myth**: the lesson is to look before you leap, not that others are never at fault.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're the lion, looking down the well. a lion glares up at you, holding a hare. what do you do?

1. leap at him. **(right answer)**
2. look closer: is that my face?
3. walk away and let him keep the hare.

Reveal: the lion leaps and drowns. he fought his own reflection. Rumi's point is the second option: look before you leap.

*In the original language:* چاه (say: CHAH). Persian: a well. where the lion met his own face.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a clever hare tricks a dumb lion.
- what it means: the strong one destroys himself fighting his own reflection.
- what a scholar hears: Rumi reads the well as a mirror; he cites the saying al-mu'min mir'at al-mu'min, the believer is the believer's mirror (Abu Dawud 4918).

**How it's done (info card):** Teachers of the Masnavi often use the lion at the well to teach that the faults we see in others can reflect our own, pairing it with the saying that the believer is a mirror to the believer.

**Sources cited:** Masnavi 1 (the lion and the hare at the well, roughly 1.1300–1.1370) (Rumi, Masnavi); Sunan Abi Dawud 4918 (the believer is the believer's mirror) (Hadith, Sunan Abi Dawud)

## Simply Spiritual #5: "Is this what I feared?" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Is this what I feared?
- **The promise on the card:** Seneca: set aside days to live on very little and ask if this is what you dreaded (Letters 18)
- **Line to carry:** is this what I feared
- **Length:** about 6:30 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: plain food, Musonius's training. Your line was plain is training. What did you miss, and what didn't you? Day three hundred.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Day three hundred. Worth a breath of its own. Now a quieter question, one that can sit in the back of a mind for years: what if it all went away? The job, the savings, the comfortable apartment. What if I ended up with almost nothing? A fear like that can go unexamined, humming, shaping choices. Seneca, writing to his friend Lucilius in the middle of a noisy Roman holiday, suggested an experiment. Not thinking about the fear. Trying it, on purpose, for a few days. Today: is this what I feared?

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> It's December in Rome, the festival of Saturnalia. The whole city is feasting, and Seneca writes that Rome is sweating with it. In the middle of this he writes his eighteenth letter. His advice: set aside a certain number of days, and live on the scantiest, cheapest food, in rough, coarse clothing. And while you do, ask yourself one question. In Richard Gummere's translation of 1917: "Is this the condition that I feared?" He gives the reason with a picture everyone in Rome would know. Soldiers practice maneuvers in peacetime, digging trenches with no enemy in sight, so that they won't panic when the real day comes. The soul should do the same in good times: train, while fortune is kind, for the day fortune isn't. And he's clear it shouldn't be a show, like rich Romans who played at poverty in fancy little rooms. Make the bed really hard and the bread really coarse. Here's a lovely detail. In the same letter Seneca quotes Epicurus, the founder of a rival school, who kept certain days of eating very little to see whether he was missing any real pleasure. Seneca borrows good ideas from wherever he finds them, and says so. Seneca's bet is that the trial teaches something. The thing dreaded turns out to be survivable, even ordinary. Plain bread is still bread. A cold room is still a room. And a fear looked at in daylight has less room to hum. The point isn't to lose everything. It's to find out, ahead of time, what you'd still have.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Pick one comfort you'll go without today. Something small but real: the second coffee, the phone at lunch, the car for a short trip, the soft chair. Now, sitting here, imagine the moment you miss it. Let the little pang come. Then ask Seneca's question gently: is this what I feared? Stay with whatever answer comes. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Is this what I feared? Seneca's question for days of living on very little, practiced in good times so fear loses its grip. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: is this what I feared. Go without one comfort today, and when you miss it, ask the question. Day three hundred.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("is this what I feared — tap the pairs."):

- Saturnalia = the December feast in Rome
- soldiers = drill in peacetime
- Epicurus = ate little to test his pleasures
- Seneca's question = is this what I feared?

*True or myth?*

- "Seneca said to practice poverty in comfortable style" - **myth**: he said make the bed really hard and the bread really coarse, not a show.
- "Seneca quotes a rival school's founder in this letter" - **true**: he borrows from Epicurus, and says so.
- "the practice is meant for bad times only" - **myth**: it's for good times, like soldiers drilling in peace.

*The fork (what would you do?):* the whole city is feasting for Saturnalia. what does Seneca suggest to Lucilius?
