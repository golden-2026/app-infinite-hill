# Hinduism: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Hinduism #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Hinduism #1: "the oldest hello" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** namaste
- **The promise on the card:** you've said it after yoga class; the words inside say more than hello
- **Line to carry:** the light in me sees the light in you
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Two palms pressed together. It's on your phone's keyboard, between the thumbs-up and the clapping hands. It's how a yoga class ends. It's how a shopkeeper in Chennai greets a customer at the counter, and how a host in New Jersey welcomes a guest through the door. And there's a word that goes with it, a word plenty of people say without a second thought. Namaste. It sounds like a polite hello, and it is one. But it's built out of two older words, and once you hear them, the greeting gets bigger. Today, in a few minutes, you find out what you've been saying.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Break the word open. It's Sanskrit, and the dictionary splits it in two. Namas: a bow. Te: to you. Namaste. I bow to you. That's it. That's the whole literal meaning. So the next question is the interesting one. Bow to what, exactly? Not to your clothes, your job title, or how your day is going. The old texts of this tradition keep pointing inward. One of them, the Chandogya Upanishad, talks about a light that shines above the heavens, higher than everything, in the highest world. And then, in the same breath, it says that this light is the very same light that is inside a person. Out there and in here: one light. Put that next to the greeting, and you get today's line. The light in me sees the light in you. That isn't a dictionary translation of namaste. It's a reading of it, and texts like this one are why the reading makes sense. Notice what that reading assumes about a person. It doesn't open by saying: become worthy first, fix yourself, then come back. Its opening move is that the light is already in there. On a bad day, on no sleep, halfway through a long scroll late at night. Already there. In this reading, the work isn't earning it. It's learning to notice it, in yourself and in whoever is standing across from you. Read that way, a plain hello carries all of it.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. If you'd like, press your palms together in front of your chest. Breathe in, slowly, all the way. And as you breathe out, let your head tip forward, just a little. A small bow. Say the word inside, if you want: namaste. Not to anyone in particular yet. Just to the fact that someone is in here, breathing, and showed up today.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Namaste. I bow to you. And underneath it: the light in me sees the light in you. A word that was already in the air. Now you know what's inside it. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: the light in me sees the light in you. Try it once today, on a friend, a stranger, or the person who's hard to like. You don't have to say it out loud. Just see somebody that way for a second. That's day one. You showed up. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- namas = a bow
- te = to you
- palms together, head tipped = the gesture that goes with it
- the Chandogya Upanishad = the same light, inside a person

*True or myth?*

- "namaste literally means the light in me sees the light in you" - **myth**: literally it's namas, a bow, and te, to you: I bow to you. the light is a reading of it.
- "namaste is made of two Sanskrit words" - **true**: namas, a bow, and te, to you.
- "an Upanishad says the light above the heavens is the light inside a person" - **true**: the Chandogya Upanishad says it in the same breath: out there and in here, one light.

*The fork (what would you do?):* the Chandogya Upanishad describes a light shining above the heavens, higher than everything. where does it say that same light is found?

1. only in the highest world.
2. inside a person. **(right answer)**
3. in the sun at noon.

Reveal: it says the light above the heavens is the very same light that is within a person. that's the idea behind today's line.

*In the original language:* नमस्ते (say: na-mas-TAY). Sanskrit: namas, a bow, plus te, to you. I bow to you.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a yoga-class goodbye.
- what it means: I bow to you, and the light in me sees the light in you.
- what a scholar hears: namas (a bow) plus te (to you); the light reading leans on texts like Chandogya Upanishad 3.13.7, where the light beyond heaven is the light within a person.

**How it's done (info card):** Hindus greet one another with namaste or namaskar, palms pressed together at the chest and the head slightly bowed. The same gesture is made to elders, to guests and before images of the gods.

**Sources cited:** Chandogya Upanishad 3.13.7 (Chandogya Upanishad); namas; namaste (Monier-Williams, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (1899))

## Hinduism #2: "a handrail made of sound" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** mantra
- **The promise on the card:** your mind already loops a phrase all day; a mantra is choosing which one
- **Line to carry:** a handrail made of sound
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Ganesha, the remover of obstacles. Your line was clear the road, then begin. Did you name what was in the way? And look back: namaste, om, dharma, shanti, pranam, Ganesha. Six words. Day seven.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Nine in the morning, a song from the grocery store slips into someone's head, and it's still looping at lunch. Or it isn't a song. It's a sentence from last night's argument, or a worry about money, going around and around on its own. Nobody chose it. The mind grabbed a phrase and kept playing it. That isn't a personal flaw. Minds do that. The practice of mantra meets that habit with a practical question instead of a fight. If the mind is going to repeat something anyway, what if you picked what it repeats?

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Mantra. The Sanskrit dictionary takes it from man, to think, with tra, an instrument. An instrument of thought. A tool for the mind. And a traditional explanation hears a second meaning: the tra of protection, so a mantra is what protects the mind that dwells on it. You've already met a few. Om, from day two. Om shanti shanti shanti, from day four. Shri ganeshaya namah, from yesterday. There are many more. One is a single verse of the Rig Veda, the Gayatri mantra. In it, the one praying meditates on the bright, worthy light of the god Savitar, the sun as the power that rouses and sets things moving, and asks that he stir and guide our thoughts. A prayer about thinking, said to train the thinking. The Bhagavad Gita gives repetition a place of honor. In the tenth chapter, Krishna lists the forms in which he can be found, and among offerings, he says, he is japa: the quiet repetition of a mantra. Here's how it works in practice. The mind can only hold so much at once. If it's holding the mantra, there's less room for the replay. It's less like a spell and more like a handrail. On a dark staircase, you don't stop to study the dark. You put your hand on the rail and keep going. When the loop comes back, you don't argue with it. You go back to the rail. That's the whole move. Six days ago the strand started with namaste. Now there are seven words on it, and three things you've said along the way, om, the shantis and the line to Ganesha, turn out to have been mantras all along.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths with the bell, with the mantra you already know. Breathe in. On the way out, say om, silently, just in the mind. Again: in, and om. If something else drifts in, a song, a list, a worry, don't push it away. Just put your hand back on the rail. Third breath. In. Om. And notice whether it gave you somewhere to go.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Mantra. An instrument of thought, a tool for the mind, repeated on purpose. Seventh word. A week's worth.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Your line: a handrail made of sound. Today, once, when the replay starts, put your hand on the rail. Om, three times, silently. See what happens. That's week one: seven words, and what's inside each one. Tomorrow, the flower, the flame and the bell. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- man = to think
- tra = an instrument, a tool
- japa = quiet repetition of a mantra
- the Gayatri = a Rig Veda verse to the sun's light

*True or myth?*

- "mantra comes from words for thinking and a tool" - **true**: man, to think, and tra, an instrument: a tool for the mind.
- "the Gayatri mantra is a modern chant" - **myth**: it's a verse of the Rig Veda, asking the god Savitar to stir our thoughts.
- "in the Gita, Krishna names japa among offerings" - **true**: in the tenth chapter: among offerings, he says, he is japa, quiet repetition.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're three breaths into saying om silently, and the song from the grocery store comes back. what does the practice ask?

1. argue the song out of your head.
2. put your hand back on the rail: om. **(right answer)**
3. give up and try again tomorrow.

Reveal: you don't fight the loop. you go back to the mantra. the coming back is the practice.

*In the original language:* मन्त्र (say: MAN-tra). Sanskrit: man, to think, plus tra, an instrument. an instrument of thought.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a chant, or a catchy motto.
- what it means: a tool for the mind, repeated on purpose, so the mind has something to hold.
- what a scholar hears: man + tra, an instrument of thought; a traditional gloss hears trana, protection. Gita 10.25: among offerings, japa.

**How it's done (info card):** Hindus repeat mantras aloud, in a whisper or silently, sometimes counting them on a string of prayer beads called a mala; this repetition is called japa, and the Gayatri mantra is recited at dawn and dusk by some families.

**Sources cited:** Rig Veda 3.62.10 (the Gayatri mantra) (Rig Veda); Bhagavad Gita 10.25 (Bhagavad Gita); mantra; man; -tra (Monier-Williams, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (1899))

## Hinduism #3: "Vibhishana crosses" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Vibhishana crosses
- **The promise on the card:** Ravana's brother chooses the other side
- **Line to carry:** walk across
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: Angada in the court, the young prince whose foot no one could lift. Your line was plant your foot. Did you feel the ground before a hard answer? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A family dinner where everyone agrees about something that's wrong, heads nodding around the table, and one person who can't nod along. Today the serial rewinds a little, to a scene Valmiki puts before the bridge. Inside Lanka, Ravana calls his council. Monkeys are gathering on the far shore. What should we do? And one after another, his generals and ministers say: we'll crush them, great king. Then his younger brother stands up. Vibhishana. And he says the thing nobody in that golden room wants to hear.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Give Sita back, Vibhishana tells his brother. Taking her was wrong, and it will destroy Lanka. Make peace with Rama. He says it once, carefully. He says it again. And Ravana explodes. He calls his brother a traitor and a disgrace to the family, and in some tellings, including Tulsidas's, he kicks him in front of the whole court. So Vibhishana makes a choice that people are still arguing about. He rises into the air with four companions, crosses the sea, and hovers above Rama's camp. I'm Ravana's brother, he calls down. I've left everything. I've come for refuge. The monkeys are sure it's a trick. Sugriva says kill him. And Rama says no. In Valmiki's telling, Rama says it's his vow: anyone who comes to him even once asking for shelter, saying I am yours, he will protect, from anything. Even, he adds, if it were Ravana himself. Then Rama does something astonishing. Before a single battle, on the beach, he has Vibhishana anointed as the future king of Lanka. The tradition holds him two ways. Devotees revere Vibhishana as a great devotee who chose dharma over family. And there's also an old saying in Hindi that uses him the other way, roughly: the insider brings down Lanka. Traitor or conscience, the story makes you look at both. What it's clear about is this: he spoke first. He told the truth inside the room twice before he ever walked out of it. Leaving wasn't his first move. It was his last one.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Settle, and breathe. Think of a room you've been in that was bad for you. A group chat, a friendship, a job, a habit of talking about people. Did you ever say the true thing inside it? Or did you leave, or stay, without speaking? No judgment. Just look. Now think of a room you're in right now that isn't right. What would it sound like to say the true thing once, calmly? And if it didn't change, where's the door? Just notice. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Vibhishana crosses. Ravana's younger brother, who told the truth twice and then walked over to Rama's side.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: walk across. Today, if something around you is wrong, say the true thing once, kindly, before you decide whether to leave. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("who did what — tap the pairs."):

- Vibhishana = told Ravana to give Sita back
- Ravana = called his brother a traitor
- Sugriva = wanted the stranger killed
- Rama = promised refuge to all who ask

*True or myth?*

- "Vibhishana left without ever warning his brother" - **myth**: he told Ravana the truth in the council, more than once, before he left.
- "Rama had him crowned before the war began" - **true**: on the beach, Rama had Vibhishana anointed as the future king of Lanka.
- "everyone in the tradition remembers him the same way" - **myth**: devotees revere him; an old Hindi saying remembers him as the insider who brought Lanka down.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Rama. Ravana's own brother is hovering over your camp asking for refuge, and your allies say it's a trap. what do you do?

1. take him in. **(right answer)**
2. send him back.
3. hold him prisoner until the war ends.

Reveal: Rama takes him in: anyone who comes to him even once asking for shelter, he says, he will protect, even Ravana himself.

*In the original language:* विभीषण (say: vi-BHEE-shuh-nuh). Sanskrit: Vibhishana, Ravana's younger brother, who crossed over to Rama.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a traitor switches sides.
- what it means: a brother tells the truth inside the room, is thrown out, and asks for refuge, and Rama takes him in.
- what a scholar hears: Rama's vow of refuge to anyone who says 'I am yours' (Yuddha Kanda) became a key text for the idea of surrender to God.

**How it's done (info card):** Hindu tradition honors Vibhishana as a devotee of Rama and one of the long-lived ones of tradition, and Rama's promise of refuge to him is often recited as a verse of assurance; the Hindi proverb about 'the insider bringing down Lanka' shows the other way people remember him.

**Sources cited:** Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (Ravana's council; Vibhishana's counsel and exile; Rama's vow of refuge, roughly sargas 9–19) (The Ramayana of Valmiki); Tulsidas, Ramcharitmanas, Sundar Kand (Ravana kicks Vibhishana) (Ramcharitmanas)

## Hinduism #4: "The lamp in a windless place (6.18–6.28)" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The lamp in a windless place
- **The promise on the card:** the Gita's picture of a settled mind is a flame that doesn't flicker
- **Line to carry:** flame unwavering
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: how to sit. A clean place, a firm seat, a straight spine. Your line was the posture, the place. Did you find your spot? Today, what happens once you're sitting there. Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Light a small oil lamp, a diya, near an open window, and watch it. The flame bends, leans, jumps, nearly goes out, flares back. Every breath of air pushes it around. Now carry it into an inner room and shut the door. The flame straightens. It stands up tall and still, so still it almost looks painted. Same lamp. Same oil. The only difference is the wind. It's the kind of lamp lit at a home shrine. The Gita, chapter six, uses that picture for the mind.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Verse nineteen: as a lamp in a windless place does not flicker, that is the picture of the yogi whose mind is held, practicing union with the self. The Sanskrit is simple: dipa, the lamp; nivata, where there's no wind. Notice the lamp is still burning. A settled mind isn't a mind that's gone out. It's fully lit, just not being pushed around. Then the verses describe what that stillness opens onto. Where the mind comes to rest, seeing the self by the self, a person is content in the self. They know a joy beyond the senses, grasped by understanding, and once they're settled there they don't move from the truth. Having gained it, they count no other gain greater. Established in it, even heavy sorrow doesn't shake them. And then a definition I like a lot, from verse twenty-three: yoga is the unhooking from union with sorrow. Yoga is often heard as joining. Here it's also an unjoining: coming apart from the grip of pain. Now the practical part. Verse twenty-five: little by little, slowly, slowly, let the mind grow still. Shanaih shanaih: slowly, slowly. And verse twenty-six gives the whole method in one sentence. Wherever the restless, unsteady mind wanders off, draw it back from there and bring it under the self. That's it. Not never wandering. Coming back. Every time the wind moves the flame, you shut the door again. That's the word today: the lamp in a windless place.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Sit, and picture a small flame in the middle of your chest, or just in front of your closed eyes. Let it burn. Each time a thought blows in, notice the flame lean. Then, gently, shut the door and watch it straighten again. Don't be annoyed at the wind. Verse twenty-six expects it. The practice is the returning, slowly, slowly, as many times as it takes. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The lamp in a windless place. Gita six, nineteen: the settled mind is a flame that burns fully and doesn't flicker.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: flame unwavering. Today, when your attention gets blown around, don't scold it. Shut the door and bring it back, slowly, slowly. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the verses — tap the pairs."):

- dipa = the lamp
- nivata = a place with no wind
- shanaih shanaih = slowly, slowly
- verse 26 = wherever it wanders, bring it back

*True or myth?*

- "the still flame in 6.19 means a mind that has gone out" - **myth**: the lamp is still burning; it's fully lit, just not being pushed around.
- "verse 23 defines yoga as unhooking from sorrow" - **true**: yoga is the unjoining from union with sorrow.
- "the Gita expects a meditator's mind never to wander" - **myth**: verse 26 assumes it will, and says: wherever it wanders, bring it back.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're sitting, and your mind wanders off for the tenth time. what does verse 26 tell you to do?

1. stop; you're not cut out for this.
2. draw it back, from wherever it went. **(right answer)**
3. follow the thought to see where it goes.

Reveal: draw it back. the Gita's method is the returning, slowly, slowly, as often as it takes.

*In the original language:* दीप (say: DEE-puh). Sanskrit: dipa, lamp; the same root as diya and Diwali (dipavali, a row of lamps).

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: meditation means an empty mind.
- what it means: a mind like a lamp out of the wind: fully lit, not flickering, brought back whenever it wanders.
- what a scholar hears: 6.19 reads 'yatha dipo nivata-stho nengate', a lamp standing where no wind is doesn't waver; 6.23 defines yoga as 'duhkha-samyoga-viyoga', a play on joining and unjoining.

**How it's done (info card):** Hindus often light a lamp (diya) at the home shrine morning or evening, and some meditate by gazing at a steady flame; Gita 6.19's windless lamp is a classic image of the settled mind.

**Sources cited:** Bhagavad Gita 6.19 (the lamp) (Bhagavad Gita); Bhagavad Gita 6.20–6.23 (Bhagavad Gita); Bhagavad Gita 6.25–6.26 (slowly, slowly; bring it back) (Bhagavad Gita)

## Hinduism #5: "Patanjali 1.1–1.16: "now, yoga"; yoga is the stilling of the mind's turnings; practice and non-attachment (6 of 7)" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Patanjali 1.1
- **The promise on the card:** day three hundred, and Patanjali's three conditions for a practice that holds
- **Line to carry:** now
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Patanjali 1.12: practice and non-attachment, the two wings. Did you find one small thing to come back to? Your line: now. Today, the first wing up close. Day three hundred.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Three hundred days. However the path got you here, day after day or in fits and starts, it's a fair place to stop and ask a plain question. Some sittings feel like something. Some feel like nothing at all, like brushing your teeth. So what makes a practice actually hold? Not through a week of enthusiasm, but for years? Patanjali answers in a single line, with three conditions, and none of them is feeling good while you do it.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> First, thread thirteen defines practice. Abhyasa is the effort to stay there: to remain steady in that stillness. Not the stillness itself, notice. The effort. The returning. Practice is the coming back, which means every time you drift and return, that isn't failure. That's the practice happening. Then thread fourteen: it becomes firmly grounded when it's attended to for a long time, without interruption, and with devotion. Three conditions. A long time: dirgha kala. Not a weekend retreat. Seasons. Years. Without break: nairantarya. The commentators mean steadiness, a thread that keeps running. Some teachers read it kindly: not perfection, but a practice that doesn't wait for ideal conditions. And with devotion: satkara. **The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

## Hinduism #10: "the Ashtavakra Gita complete" (day 1000, year 3)

- **Today's word:** the Ashtavakra Gita complete
- **The promise on the card:** one verse says the one who takes themselves to be free is free, and the one who takes themselves to be bound is bound
- **Line to carry:** the Ashtavakra Gita complete
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the Ashtavakra Gita began: King Janaka's three questions, and a sage who answered, rest as the witness, free now. Did you catch the one who notices? Day one thousand.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Free, if you take yourself as free. Bound, if you take yourself as bound. That's the line, in paraphrase, from the first chapter of the Ashtavakra Gita. And the sage adds something odd. He doesn't present it as his own discovery. He says it's a saying people repeat, and that this saying is true: as the mind holds itself, so it goes. It's a strange thing to hear on the thousandth day of anything. A thousand days of practice could make a person feel they're finally getting somewhere, or that they still have miles to go. This verse would quietly ask about both: who is doing the counting?

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Read quickly, the verse sounds like a poster on a gym wall. Believe in yourself and anything is possible. But in the Ashtavakra Gita it means something stranger, and the verses around it show why. A few verses earlier, the sage tells Janaka that bondage is this one thing: seeing someone else as the seer, rather than yourself. Then he says the king has been bitten by the black snake of egoism, the thought, I am the doer. Drink the nectar of faith, he says, in the thought, I am not the doer, and be happy. And then comes the line. One who holds that they're free is free. One who holds that they're bound is bound. So the verse isn't about positive thinking. It's not saying, think hard enough and you'll get a better life. It's saying the binding was never in the world. It was in the taking, in what consciousness took itself to be. Ashtavakra's word for free is mukta. On his account, a person doesn't build their way to mukta. They stop insisting they're bound. A thousand days into this path, that cuts both ways, and the text wouldn't mind. The days of practice are real. The sitting, the reading, the lamps and the songs are real. But this book would say the one who sat for all of them was never in a cell to begin with. On its account, freedom is not a reward at the end of a long climb. It's a fact about the climber, available on day one, and on day one thousand.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Sit comfortably and let the breath move on its own. Notice the sense of being the one who is doing all this: sitting, breathing, trying. Then try the sage's small turn. Let the breath breathe itself, with no one pulling it in or pushing it out. Let sounds arrive without anyone needing to hear them. If the thought I'm doing this comes back, notice it as one more thought passing through. The bell holds the time.
