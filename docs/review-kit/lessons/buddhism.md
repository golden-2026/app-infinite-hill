# Buddhism: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Buddhism #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Buddhism #1: "a word for wishing well on people you don't like" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** metta
- **The promise on the card:** a word for wishing well on people you don't like
- **Line to carry:** may you be at ease
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> The coworker who replies all. The neighbor with the leaf blower at seven on a Saturday. The relative with opinions about your life at every holiday. Not villains. Just people who are hard to like. Maybe a face comes to mind already. Now here's an old Buddhist practice: people sit quietly and wish that person well. On purpose. Not because they've forgiven anything, and not because the person has earned it. Just as training, the way you'd train a muscle. It sounds almost backwards. Buddhists trace it to the Buddha's own teaching. Today, the word for it.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Metta. It's a word in Pali, the old language the Buddha's teachings were kept in. It comes from mitta, a friend. So metta is friendliness. A wish for someone's good, the plain warm kind you'd feel for a friend, pointed at anyone at all. You may know its Sanskrit cousin, maitri, or the name Maitreya, the friendly one, the Buddha that tradition says is still to come. There's a short teaching on metta, the Metta Sutta, still chanted in Buddhist homes and monasteries. It pictures a mother protecting her only child with her own life, and says to hold a heart like that toward every living being. Seen and unseen. Near and far. Born and not yet born. Every one. That's the size of it. And the Buddha's own verses put the reason bluntly. In Max Müller's old translation of the Dhammapada: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love—this is an old rule." An old rule. Not a new idea even then. How do people actually do it? Later teachers laid out a gentle order. Start with yourself, because it's hard to give what you've never felt. Then someone you love. Then someone neutral, the person who sells you coffee. Then, last and slowest, someone difficult. You don't pretend to like them. You just wish them what you'd want: to be safe, to be well, to be at ease. Some who practice it describe an odd side effect: it changes them more than anyone else. The difficult person is still difficult. They just carry less of that person around.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Bring back that face from a minute ago. You don't have to feel warm. Breathe in slowly. And on the way out, let a few words go toward them, out loud or just inside: may you be at ease. That's all. One breath of friendliness, pointed somewhere unusual.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Metta. Friendliness. A wish for anyone's good, even the people who make it hard. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: may you be at ease. Once today, when someone annoys you, in traffic, in a meeting, in a group chat, say it inside, just once, and notice what happens in you. That's day one. You showed up. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- metta = friendliness · goodwill
- mitta = a friend
- maitri = the Sanskrit cousin
- a mother with her only child = the picture of how wide it goes

*True or myth?*

- "metta means you have to like everyone" - **myth**: it's a wish for someone's good. you don't have to like them, or pretend to.
- "metta comes from the Pali word for friend" - **true**: mitta, a friend. metta is friendliness, pointed at anyone.
- "the Dhammapada calls "hatred ceases by love" a new idea" - **myth**: it calls it "an old rule."

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're learning metta the traditional way. who do you start with?

1. the most difficult person you know.
2. yourself. **(right answer)**
3. all beings everywhere at once.

Reveal: the classic order, set out by the commentator Buddhaghosa, starts with yourself, then a loved one, then a stranger, and only then someone difficult. the hard one comes last, and slowly.

*In the original language:* मेत्ता (say: MET-taa). Pali, written here in Devanagari: friendliness, goodwill. From mitta, a friend. Sanskrit: maitrī.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: being nice to people.
- what it means: a trained wish for anyone's good, from yourself outward to the people who are hard to love.
- what a scholar hears: mettā, from mitta, friend. the Karaniya Metta Sutta pictures a mother guarding her only child, and asks for a heart like that toward all beings.

**How it's done (info card):** Buddhists practice metta by silently repeating short wishes, such as may you be safe, may you be well, may you be at ease, first for themselves and then outward to loved ones, strangers, difficult people and all beings; the Metta Sutta is chanted in monasteries and homes.

**Sources cited:** Dhammapada 5 (The Dhammapada, Max Müller 1881); Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8; Khuddakapatha 9) (The Sutta Nipata); Visuddhimagga IX (the order of metta practice) (Buddhaghosa, The Path of Purification)

## Buddhism #2: "it can stop" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** the third
- **The promise on the card:** it can stop
- **Line to carry:** there is an end
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: the second truth, tanha, thirst. Your line was the wanting is the wobble. Did you find the thirst under a slow line? Day seven.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> One week. Look at your strand. Metta, the wish for anyone's good. The breath, in and out. Dukkha, the wobble in the wheel. The Buddha, a person who woke up. The first truth: there is dukkha. The second: it comes from thirst. If the week ended there, it would be a pretty heavy week. A diagnosis and a cause. But the Buddha didn't stop at two, and neither do we. A headache that's sat behind the eyes all afternoon finally lifts. Nobody marked the minute it arrived. Sometimes the leaving is the only part anyone notices. Today, the truth that says the wobble can end.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The third noble truth is nirodha. Cessation. Stopping. In the first sermon it's put simply: when the thirst fades away completely, when it's let go and released, dukkha ends. Remove the cause, and what it causes stops. The name for that ending is one you've heard: nirvana, in Sanskrit, or nibbana, in Pali. Teachers link the word to a flame going out, the way a lamp goes out when there's no more fuel. That's the image. Not a place you go. The fire of craving, with nothing left to burn. People sometimes hear that and think it sounds like nothingness. The texts don't describe it that way. The Dhammapada, in Max Müller's old translation, lists the best things there are: "Health is the greatest of gifts, contentment the best riches; trust is the best of relationships, Nirvana the highest happiness." Happiness. Not blankness. And there's an exchange in the discourses that uses the same fire. A wanderer asks the Buddha where a fully awakened person goes after death. The Buddha asks him: when a fire goes out in front of you, which direction did it go? North? South? The man admits the question doesn't fit. The fire just went out. The Buddha says this question is like that one. Buddhist traditions describe nirvana in different words, and the texts often say what it isn't. But the third truth itself is simple. The wobble is real. And it isn't permanent. There is an end.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths, with the bell. On the first, notice any small wanting that's here. On the second, notice any small ache. On the third, notice a moment, even a flicker, where neither is pulling at you. A gap. A small quiet between two thoughts. It might be tiny. That tiny thing is what the third truth is pointing at.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> The third. The third noble truth, nirodha: when the thirst ends, dukkha ends. The flame goes out. Seventh word, and a week done.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: there is an end. Today, notice one ache or irritation actually ending, a pain easing, a worry passing, and pay attention to it leaving the way you'd pay attention to it arriving. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the truth — tap the pairs."):

- nirodha = cessation · stopping
- nibbana = the Pali for nirvana
- a flame going out = the picture inside the word
- which way did the fire go? = the question that doesn't fit

*True or myth?*

- "nirvana means nothingness" - **myth**: the Dhammapada calls it "the highest happiness."
- "nirvana's image is a flame going out" - **true**: the fire of craving with nothing left to burn.
- "the Buddha said exactly where an awakened person goes after death" - **myth**: he answered with a question: when a fire goes out, which way did it go?

*The fork (what would you do?):* a wanderer asks the Buddha where a fully awakened person goes after death. what does the Buddha do?

1. names a heaven.
2. says they go nowhere at all.
3. asks which direction a fire goes when it goes out. **(right answer)**

Reveal: he asks about the fire. north, south, east, west? the question doesn't fit, and the Buddha says the wanderer's question is like that one.

*In the original language:* निरोध (say: ni-ROH-dha). Pali and Sanskrit: cessation, stopping. The third noble truth.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: nirvana is Buddhist heaven.
- what it means: the ending of craving, and with it dukkha: the flame with nothing left to burn.
- what a scholar hears: nirodha, cessation; nibbana, from a root meaning to blow or go out. in the Aggivacchagotta Sutta (MN 72) the Buddha refuses the question of where an awakened one goes, using the extinguished fire.

**How it's done (info card):** Buddhists speak of nirvana as the end of craving and of dukkha; in Theravada it is the goal of the path, and Mahayana traditions describe awakening in their own terms, while everyday practice keeps attention on letting go.

**Sources cited:** Dhammapada 204 (The Dhammapada, Max Müller 1881); Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11): the third truth (The Samyutta Nikaya); Aggivacchagotta Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 72): the fire gone out (The Majjhima Nikaya)

## Buddhism #3: "The man on the road" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The man on the road
- **The promise on the card:** Upaka the wanderer hears him, shakes his head, and takes another road (MN 26)
- **Line to carry:** not everyone is ready
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, should he teach? Brahma pleaded, and the Buddha saw people like lotuses at every depth. Your line was some are ready. Who did you share something with? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Not every first meeting goes well. A pitch gets a polite nod. A favorite song gets a shrug and a huh, good for you. The subject changes, and that's that. The Buddha has decided to teach. He's thought about who should hear first, and he's chosen his five old companions, the ones who left him when he ate. They're in a deer park near Varanasi, a long walk away. So he sets out on the road. And not far along it, he meets a stranger. Today, the man on the road.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The stranger's name is Upaka. He's a wanderer too, from a group called the Ajivikas, and he's struck by what he sees. Your face is so clear, friend, he says. Your skin so bright. Who's your teacher? Whose teaching do you follow? It's an open door. And the Buddha answers, the sutta says, in verse. He says he has no teacher; he found this himself. He has overcome everything, understood everything, and he's on his way to Kasi to set the wheel of the teaching turning, to beat the drum of the deathless in a world gone blind. It's a big answer. Upaka listens. And then he says something like: may it be so, friend. He shakes his head. And he takes a side road, and walks away. That's it. The stranger doesn't buy it. The Buddha himself tells this story, and doesn't hide it. He doesn't chase Upaka. He doesn't argue. He just keeps walking to the deer park. There's a coda, from the later tradition. Later, the commentary's story goes, Upaka fell in love and married a hunter's daughter named Capa, had a son, and later, unhappy, went looking for the Buddha after all, and became a monk. Capa's own verses survive in the Therigatha, the poems of the early nuns. So the man on the road wasn't lost. He just wasn't ready yet.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Sit comfortably and let the breath settle. Think of someone who disagrees with you about something that matters to you. It might be small, a movie, or big, how to live. Picture them shaking their head and walking off down a side road. Notice what rises in you: the urge to chase, to argue, to prove. Let it be there. Then, gently, let them go. You can wish them well on their road. You keep walking yours. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The man on the road. Upaka, a wanderer who asked the Buddha about his teaching, then said may it be so, shook his head, and took another road.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: not everyone is ready. Today, let someone disagree with you, and don't chase them down the side road. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("who's on the road — tap the pairs."):

- Upaka = shook his head and took a side road
- the Ajivikas = Upaka's group of wanderers
- Kasi = where the Buddha was going
- Capa = a hunter's daughter Upaka married

*True or myth?*

- "Upaka became the Buddha's follower on the spot" - **myth**: Upaka said may it be so, shook his head, and took another road.
- "the Buddha told the Upaka story about himself, failure and all" - **true**: in MN 26, without hiding that the first listener walked away.
- "the Buddha chased Upaka to change his mind" - **myth**: he kept walking to the deer park.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Upaka. a wanderer with a shining face tells you he's found the deathless with no teacher at all. what do you do?

1. fall at his feet.
2. say may it be so, shake your head, and take a side road. **(right answer)**
3. challenge him to a debate.

Reveal: Upaka takes the side road. the stranger doesn't believe. and the Buddha doesn't chase him.

*In the original language:* आजीवक (say: aa-JEE-va-ka). Pali: an Ajivika, a member of the wandering group Upaka belonged to.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: everyone who met the Buddha was converted on the spot.
- what it means: a stranger who asked about his teaching shook his head and walked away, and the Buddha let him go.
- what a scholar hears: MN 26 records Upaka the Ajivika's 'may it be so, friend' (hupeyya), his head-shake and the bypath; the commentary and Therigatha (Capa's verses) tell of his later return.

**How it's done (info card):** Buddhists retell Upaka's meeting as a reminder not to force the teaching on anyone, and in some traditions teachers wait to be asked before they teach, letting people come and go freely.

**Sources cited:** Ariyapariyesana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 26): the meeting with Upaka (The Majjhima Nikaya); Therigatha: the verses of Capa (The Therigatha)

## Buddhism #4: "One day (109–115)" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** One day
- **The promise on the card:** better one day seeing how things arise and pass than a hundred years without seeing it
- **Line to carry:** one day, awake
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, conquer yourself, and your line: the greatest conquest. Did you win one small inner fight, with one breath? Day one hundred eighty, the end of the thousands chapter.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Some single days stay clearer than whole years. A wedding. A birth. The day of a diagnosis. A day walking somewhere new, when the light, the food and the faces all seemed to glow. And some years slide by and leave almost nothing behind: the commute, the inbox, the same dinner. Length and life aren't the same thing. The last verses of chapter eight make that point six times over, and then name what makes a day worth more than a century. Today, verses one hundred nine to one hundred fifteen: one day.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The pattern goes like this. Verse one hundred ten, in Müller's English: someone may live a hundred years, careless and unrestrained, but “a life of one day is better if a man is virtuous and reflecting.” The next verses repeat the shape. A hundred years without wisdom, or one day with it. A hundred years idle and weak, or one day of real effort. And then verse one hundred thirteen: someone may live a hundred years without seeing how things begin and end, but “a life of one day is better if a man sees beginning and end.” Beginning and end. In Pali, udayabbaya: arising and passing away. In the Theravada tradition it names a stage of insight practice: seeing, not just knowing, that every thought, feeling, sound and moment comes up and goes. The commentary ties this verse to a woman named Patacara. In a single terrible stretch, the story says, she lost her husband, both her children, her parents and her brother. She came to the Buddha out of her mind with grief, and he calmed her and taught her. Her own verses, preserved in the collection of the early nuns, describe the turning point: washing her feet, she watched the water run down the slope. The commentary adds that it ran a short way, then farther, then farther still, and that she saw in it lives ending young, in middle age, and old. That same night, her verses say, her mind was freed. The last two verses of the chapter say the same of seeing the deathless and seeing the highest teaching. One day, seeing clearly, outweighs a hundred years of not seeing. Not because the years don't matter. Because a single day, lived awake, is where seeing happens.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Sit and settle. Let's practice seeing beginning and end, at the smallest scale. Watch one breath begin, and end. Then the next. Listen for a sound: notice where it starts, and where it fades. Feel a sensation in the hands or feet: notice it arise, and change, and pass. Don't hold on to any of it. Just watch things come up and go, like water running off onto the ground. Everything you notice has a beginning and an end. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> One day. Better one day seeing how things arise and pass away than a hundred years without seeing it.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: one day, awake. Today, watch this one day closely. Notice three things begin and end: a conversation, a mood, a meal. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("match the pieces of verses one hundred nine to one hundred fifteen."):

- a hundred years, careless = worth less than one day, reflecting
- udayabbaya = arising and passing away
- Patacara = watched water run off her feet
- beginning and end = what verse one hundred thirteen sees

*True or myth?*

- "these verses say a long life doesn't matter" - **myth**: they say one day lived awake outweighs a hundred years without seeing.
- "udayabbaya means arising and passing away" - **true**: in Theravada insight practice, seeing it directly is a named stage.
- "Patacara's own verses survive in the collection of the early nuns' poems" - **true**: they describe watching water run off as she washed her feet.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Patacara, washing your feet. you pour water; it runs a short way, then farther, then farther still. what did she see?

1. that the ground was uneven.
2. how lives come and go, like the water. **(right answer)**
3. that it was time to go home.

Reveal: in the commentary, the water's short and long runs showed her how beings die young, in middle age or old; her own verses say her mind was freed that night.

*In the original language:* उदयब्बय (say: u-da-YAB-ba-ya). Pali: arising and passing away, from verse one hundred thirteen.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: 'one day is better than a hundred years' means live fast.
- what it means: one day of seeing things clearly as they arise and pass outweighs a lifetime of not seeing.
- what a scholar hears: verse 113's 'apassam udayabbayam' names insight into arising and passing (udayabbaya-nana); Patacara's verses are Therigatha 112–116.

**How it's done (info card):** In insight meditation, practitioners watch sensations, sounds and thoughts arise and pass away, and the story of the nun Patacara is told as an example of this seeing bringing freedom from grief.

**Sources cited:** Dhammapada 110 (The Dhammapada, Max Müller 1881); Dhammapada 113 (The Dhammapada, Max Müller 1881); Dhammapada 109, 111–112, 114–115; Dhammapada-atthakatha, story of Patacara (verse 113) (The Dhammapada and commentary); Therigatha 112–116 (Patacara) (The Pali Canon)

## Buddhism #5: "Monastery and village" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Monastery and village
- **The promise on the card:** lay people support the monks; the monks teach and chant: an old exchange
- **Line to carry:** give and receive
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: sotapanna, and your line, entering the stream. Did you do one ritual with your eyes open? Day three hundred. Three hundred days on the path.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Six in the morning in a Thai village. A woman has been up since four, steaming rice and cooking a small curry. She sets it out on a low table by the road and waits, barefoot. A line of monks appears, walking slowly, bowls covered by their robes. She kneels and places rice in each bowl. None of them says thank you. That's not rudeness. It's the custom, and the relationship behind it goes back to the early texts. If anyone thanks anyone, often it's her. Today, on day three hundred: the monastery and the village, and what flows between them.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> By the old monastic rules, Theravada monks aren't meant to farm, to cook and store their own food, to handle money, or, unless they're sick, to ask for food. Practice varies from place to place, but the ideal is a life that depends on the village. Lay people give what the texts call the four requisites: food, robes, shelter and medicine. That giving is called dāna, generosity, and it's often the first practice a Buddhist child learns. In the other direction, the monastery gives what the village can't make for itself. Monks teach. They chant protective verses at births, new houses, illnesses and deaths. They're a quiet place in the middle of town, in some places a school for boys, and a living reminder that another way to live exists. In a short passage of the Itivuttaka, the Buddha tells the monks that householders are a great help to them, supplying their needs, and that the monks help householders in turn by teaching them. Each supports the other, he says, so that the holy life can be lived. Why doesn't the monk say thank you? Because in this tradition the giver is receiving too: the chance to be generous, which Buddhists call merit, a kind of goodness that grows in the one who gives. So the giver often thanks the monk for the chance. And the teaching itself is counted as the greatest gift. The Dhammapada says, in Max Müller's words, "The gift of the law exceeds all gifts." Law there means the Dhamma, the teaching. Rice goes one way; the teaching goes the other. Nobody is keeping a ledger. Everybody is giving and receiving at once.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Sit comfortably. Think of everything you've received today without asking: the water from the tap, the road someone paved, a meal someone grew, a kind word. Let the list grow for a few breaths. Then think of one thing you could give, freely, expecting nothing back. After the bell, if there's a monastery or temple near you, you're invited to offer something, food, time, a small gift. If there isn't, any place that feeds people will do. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Monastery and village. Lay people give food, robes, shelter and medicine; monks give teaching and blessing. An old exchange of giving and receiving.

## Buddhism #7: "Not inflamed" (day 514, year 2)

- **Today's word:** Not inflamed
- **The promise on the card:** not knowing the eye as it is, one is inflamed; knowing it, one is free
- **Line to carry:** not inflamed
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: six sets of six, and your line, not self, six times. Did a feeling arrive through one door and leave again? Today, what happens when we don't see those doors clearly. Day five hundred fourteen.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A bakery window at the end of a long day. Glazed pastries under warm light, a cake with a perfect swirl. The glass hasn't changed since morning, and the person walking past hadn't wanted cake a minute ago. Now there's heat, a pull, a little restlessness. The hundred and forty-ninth discourse has a word for that state. The Pali is saratta, usually rendered inflamed, or impassioned. And it says the fire doesn't start in the cake or the window. It starts in how things are seen. Today: not inflamed.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The discourse is called the great six sense bases, and it begins with a contrast. When someone doesn't know and see the eye as it actually is, the Buddha says, and doesn't know forms, eye-consciousness, eye-contact and the feeling that comes from it as they actually are, they become inflamed by lust for all of it. Inflamed, tied up, infatuated, fixed on the sweetness. And then things build. Craving grows. The heaps of clinging pile up for the future. Troubles of body and mind grow, torments grow, fevers grow. And the person feels it, in the body and in the mind. Now the other side. When someone knows and sees the eye as it actually is, and the forms, and the seeing, they aren't inflamed. Craving fades. The heaps of clinging shrink. The fevers go down. And the text says this person feels ease, in body and in mind. The key phrase is yathabhuta: as it actually is. Not as advertised, not as feared. Just as it is. Notice what's not being blamed. Not the eye. Not the world. The eye is fine. The cake is fine. The problem is the fog between them. And then a surprise. For a person who sees this way, the text says, their view simply is right view, their intention right intention, their effort right effort. The eightfold path, the text says, comes to fulfillment in them. Calm and insight, it says, run together, evenly yoked. Like two oxen under one yoke, pulling one cart.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: know one sense clearly. Rest one hand on something near you: a sleeve, a table, your own knee. First just texture and temperature. Then notice whether there's a small liking or disliking, a pull toward it or away. Don't fix it. Just name it: liking, or not liking, or neither. Now let the hand go still and rest with whatever's left of the touch. Notice the temperature of the mind. Is it warm, cool, still? The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Not inflamed. Seeing the senses as they actually are, yathabhuta, so the pull of what's seen doesn't set the mind on fire. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

**Games after the talk**

- not seeing clearly = craving and fevers grow
- calm and insight = yoked evenly together

Tradition says he first chanted the phrase aloud at his home temple, facing the sunrise. Let's open the phrase. Namu means something like devotion, or I take refuge in. Myoho is the wonderful law, or mystic law, the truth the sutra teaches. Renge is the lotus flower, which grows out of mud and blooms clean. Kyo is sutra, a teaching. Myoho Renge Kyo is the sutra's title; namu is the devotion placed in front of it. Put together: devotion to the Lotus Sutra of the wonderful law. Why a title? In Nichiren's teaching, the title isn't a label on the outside of a book. It holds the whole sutra inside it, the way a seed holds a tree. So chanting the title is a way of meeting the entire teaching with your own voice, not only with your eyes or your intellect. That makes it a practice open to anyone, not only to scholar monks. Nichiren was outspoken. He wrote that Japan's troubles came from turning away from the Lotus, he argued hard with the schools of his day, and tradition remembers that he was sent into exile for it. Today his followers belong to several groups, including Nichiren Shu, Nichiren Shoshu and Soka Gakkai, and they differ on how to read his teaching and on who carries it forward. What they share is the chant. For Nichiren Buddhists, saying the title is not a warm-up for practice. It is the practice.
