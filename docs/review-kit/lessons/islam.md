# Islam: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Islam #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Islam #1: "the greeting that is a wish" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** salaam
- **The promise on the card:** the greeting that is a wish; s-l-m — salaam, Muslim, Islam
- **Line to carry:** peace, upon you
- **Length:** about 5:45 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Maybe you've heard it in a shop, or at an airport gate, or across a family dinner table. Two people meet, and before anything else, before how are you, before the weather, one of them says: as-salamu alaykum. And the other one answers straight back: wa alaykum as-salam. It happens so fast it sounds like hello. It isn't hello. Hello is a noise we make to say I see you. This greeting is a wish. It hands the other person something. And the word at the center of it is one of the oldest and biggest words in the whole language of this path. It's hiding inside the name of the faith itself. Today's word is that one.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Salaam means peace. As-salamu alaykum means peace be upon you. Not I hope you're well. Peace, upon you, now. It's a gift given in the first second of meeting someone. Here's the thing almost nobody hears. Arabic builds its words from roots, usually three letters. This one is s, l, m. Put different vowels around it and you get salaam: peace. You get Islam: the surrender, the making of peace with God. And you get Muslim: a person who does that. So the name of the faith and the everyday greeting grow from the same root. Every time two Muslims say hello, they're saying a piece of the faith's own name to each other. Hebrew has the same root, which is why Jewish friends say shalom. The Qur'an even gives the word to God. Among God's names, in the fifty-ninth surah, is As-Salam: Peace itself. And the Qur'an sets a rule for greetings. In Pickthall's old translation of the fourth surah it reads: "When ye are greeted with a greeting, greet ye with a better than it or return it." So there's a small art to answering. If someone says peace be upon you, the minimum is to give it back. Better is to add something: and upon you peace, and the mercy of God, and His blessings. The greeting grows as it goes around. There's also a well-known hadith, a saying of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, recorded in Sahih Muslim, that ties the greeting to love itself. In it he tells his companions they won't truly love one another until they spread salaam among themselves. Not a feeling first and then a greeting. The greeting first, and the love grows from it.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Think of one person you'll see today. Breathe in slowly. And on the way out, if you want to, send them the word, in your head. Salaam. Peace, upon you. You don't have to say it out loud. Just mean it once.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Salaam. Peace. The greeting that's a gift, and the root inside Islam and Muslim. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: peace, upon you. Today, when someone greets you, give back a little more than you got. A fuller hello, a real smile, their name. Answer with better. That's day one. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- salaam = peace
- as-salamu alaykum = peace be upon you
- s-l-m = the root of salaam, Islam, Muslim
- As-Salam = Peace: a name of God in the Qur'an

*True or myth?*

- "as-salamu alaykum just means hello" - **myth**: it means peace be upon you. it's a wish, handed over in the first second.
- "salaam, Islam and Muslim share one Arabic root" - **true**: s-l-m. peace, the surrender that makes peace, and the person who does it.
- "the Qur'an says to answer a greeting with a better one, or the same" - **true**: surah 4, verse 86: "greet ye with a better than it or return it."

*The fork (what would you do?):* someone says to you: as-salamu alaykum. by the Qur'an's rule, what's the best answer?

1. a nod is enough.
2. give it back, or give back more. **(right answer)**
3. say it first next time instead.

Reveal: "greet ye with a better than it or return it" (4:86). the minimum is to return the peace. better is to add mercy and blessings.

*In the original language:* سَلَام (say: sah-LAAM). Arabic. peace. the root s-l-m also gives Islam (إسلام) and Muslim (مسلم).

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: an Arabic way of saying hello.
- what it means: peace be upon you — a wish, given before anything else is said.
- what a scholar hears: the three-letter root s-l-m, which also makes Islam and Muslim, and As-Salam, one of the names of God in surah 59.

**How it's done (info card):** Muslims greet one another with "as-salamu alaykum" (peace be upon you) and answer "wa alaykum as-salam" (and upon you peace), often adding "and the mercy of God and His blessings." The greeting is also said to close each daily prayer.

**Sources cited:** Qur'an 4:86 (The Qur'an, Pickthall 1930); Qur'an 59:23 (The Qur'an); Sahih Muslim 54 (Hadith)

## Islam #2: ""Praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds"" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Rabb
- **The promise on the card:** the lord who raises and tends every world, not just yours
- **Line to carry:** every world is tended
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: al-Fatiha, the opening, and its first verse, a doorway made of a name. Your line was in the name of the merciful. Did a gentle first sentence help? Day seven.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A week ago this started with one word. Salaam. Since then: bismillah, alhamdulillah, inshallah, the five pillars, and the opening of a prayer. Two of those words were already waiting inside that prayer. Bismillah is its first verse. And alhamdulillah is the very first word of the second. So today, as the week closes, the week comes back to meet us. Verse two takes the thank-you you learned on day three and points it somewhere enormous. Not at your day. At every world there is.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Verse two, in Pickthall's words: “Praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds.” In Arabic, al-hamdu lillahi rabbi l-'alamin. The first half is your day-three word, alhamdulillah. The small al- at the front makes it all praise, every kind, belonging to God. Now the new word. Rabb. English usually says Lord, and that's right, but it's thin. Rabb is the one who owns and the one who looks after. Classical commentators explain it with the picture of raising something: bringing a child, or a seedling, along step by step until it's grown. So a rabb isn't a distant owner with paperwork. It's the one who feeds, shelters and brings you up. And then: al-'alamin. The worlds. Not the world, the worlds, plural. Commentators have listed people, angels, animals, every kind of created thing; some say each kind is a world of its own. The ant colony under the sidewalk. The deep sea. The inside of a stranger's life on the train. All of it, the verse says, is being raised and tended by one Rabb. A hadith qudsi, a saying of God reported by the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and recorded by Muslim, adds a detail. It describes al-Fatiha as a conversation. When someone praying says this verse, God answers: My servant has praised Me. So the thank-you from day three isn't said into empty air. In the prayer, it's heard, and answered. Two verses down, five to go.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths, with the bell. First breath: your own world. One thing in it that's being looked after, even today. Second breath: a world that isn't yours. A bird, a stranger, a field somewhere you'll never see. Third breath: hold both at once. If you want, the Arabic rides out on the last one: al-hamdu lillahi rabbi l-'alamin.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Rabb. Lord: the one who owns, raises and looks after, here the Lord of all the worlds. Seventh word, and a full week on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: every world is tended. Today, notice one small world that isn't yours, a plant on a windowsill, a neighbor's routine, and whatever keeps it going. A week, done. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside verse two — tap the pairs."):

- al-hamd = all praise, every kind
- Rabb = the one who owns, raises and tends
- al-'alamin = the worlds, plural
- my servant has praised Me = God's answer, in a hadith qudsi

*True or myth?*

- "Rabb simply means owner" - **myth**: it means lord and owner, and also the one who raises and looks after what's his.
- "the verse says Lord of the world, singular" - **myth**: al-'alamin is plural: the worlds, every kind of created thing.
- "the first words of verse two are the day-three word, alhamdulillah" - **true**: al-hamdu lillah: praise be to God.

*The fork (what would you do?):* someone praying says: praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds. in the hadith qudsi recorded by Muslim, what happens next?

1. nothing: prayer is a one-way message.
2. God answers: My servant has praised Me. **(right answer)**
3. the angels repeat it back.

Reveal: the hadith describes al-Fatiha as a conversation. at this verse, God answers: My servant has praised Me.

*In the original language:* رَبّ (say: RABB). Arabic: lord, master, the one who raises and looks after. in al-Fatiha: rabbi l-'alamin, Lord of the Worlds.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: "Lord" — a title, like a king's.
- what it means: the one who owns and looks after, raising every world along.
- what a scholar hears: commentators gloss rabb with tarbiya, bringing a thing gradually to completion; al-'alamin, plural of 'alam, is read as every class of creation.

**How it's done (info card):** Muslims say this verse in Arabic in every unit of every prayer, and many say alhamdulillah, its opening word, many times a day. Rabb is also the word Muslims use in many personal prayers, often as Rabbi, my Lord, or Rabbana, our Lord.

**Sources cited:** Qur'an 1:2 (The Qur'an, Pickthall 1930); Sahih Muslim 395 (the hadith qudsi of al-Fatiha as a conversation) (Sahih Muslim)

## Islam #3: "The house of the Aziz" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The house of the Aziz
- **The promise on the card:** the lady of the house tries to seduce him, and he says: I seek refuge in Allah
- **Line to carry:** I seek refuge
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: sold for a few coins, and a boy the traders thought little of. Your line was the price they paid isn't your worth. Did you catch a low price on anyone? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Temptation rarely arrives when you're weak and alone in a dark alley. More often it shows up when life is finally going well. You've got the job, the trust, the key to the building. Nobody's watching. Yusuf has grown up in the house of the man who bought him. The Qur'an calls that man al-Aziz, a title, something like the mighty one, a high official of Egypt. Yusuf has reached his full strength, and the verse says he's been given wisdom and knowledge. He's trusted, he's handsome, he lives under their roof. And the lady of the house has noticed. Today: the house of the Aziz.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Surah Yusuf, verse twenty-three, told with great restraint. She, in whose house he was, tried to turn him from himself. She closed the doors, and said: come. And Yusuf's answer, in Pickthall's old translation: "I seek refuge in Allah!" In Arabic, ma'adha-llah. Then he gives his reasons: he's my master, he's treated me well; and wrongdoers don't prosper. Who does he mean by my master? Commentators split. Some say the Aziz, the man who gave him a good home. Others say his Lord, Allah. The Arabic word, rabb, can carry both, and the verse may be holding both at once: loyalty to the man who trusted him, and to the God who made him. Then comes a verse commentators read in more than one way: she desired him, and he would have desired her, had he not seen the clear sign of his Lord. What was the sign? The Qur'an doesn't say. Commentators have offered many answers; the text stays silent. What it does say is that Yusuf was human, the pull was real, and he turned. That phrase, I seek refuge in Allah, has a close cousin in daily Muslim life: a'udhu billah. The Qur'an tells its readers to seek refuge in Allah before reciting, and Muslims also say it when anger rises, or a bad thought knocks. Seeking refuge is not proving you're strong. It's admitting you need a place to run to, and running there. Yusuf didn't argue with the temptation, or try to out-think it. He named where he was going, and he went.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: find your refuge before the knock. Settle, and breathe. Think of the thing that tempts you most often. It might be small: the extra drink, the cruel comment, the scroll at two in the morning, the lie that would make things easier. Picture it knocking. Now, instead of arguing with it, picture turning and walking to somewhere safe. If you pray, you can say a'udhu billah, I seek refuge in Allah, and let the words be the door you step through. If not, choose one word that means safe to you, and practice going there. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The house of the Aziz. Where Yusuf, trusted and tempted behind closed doors, said: I seek refuge in Allah. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: I seek refuge. When temptation knocks today, don't argue with it; just turn and go to your refuge. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the story — tap the pairs."):

- al-Aziz = a title: a high official
- ma'adha-llah = I seek refuge in Allah
- rabb = master, or Lord
- a'udhu billah = said when temptation knocks

*True or myth?*

- "the Qur'an says Yusuf felt no pull at all" - **myth**: it says he would have desired her, had he not seen the clear sign of his Lord.
- "the Qur'an explains exactly what the sign was" - **myth**: it doesn't. commentators have offered many answers; the text is silent.
- "Muslims say a'udhu billah before reading the Qur'an" - **true**: seeking refuge in Allah opens the recitation, and is said when anger or temptation rises.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Yusuf. the doors are closed, nobody is watching, and the lady of the house says: come. what do you say?

1. nothing, and hope she stops.
2. I seek refuge in Allah! **(right answer)**
3. let me think about it.

Reveal: ma'adha-llah: I seek refuge in Allah. then his reasons: my master has treated me well, and wrongdoers don't prosper.

*In the original language:* مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ (say: ma-'AA-dhal-LAAH). Arabic, from Qur'an 12:23: "I seek refuge in Allah" — a close cousin of a'udhu billah.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a story about a scandal.
- what it means: a trusted young man, really tempted, who doesn't argue with it but runs to refuge in Allah.
- what a scholar hears: "he is my rabb" in 12:23 is read by some as the Aziz, his master, and by others as Allah; 12:24's burhan, the sign, is left unexplained.

**How it's done (info card):** Muslims say a'udhu billah, "I seek refuge in Allah," before reciting the Qur'an, when angry, and when tempted; Yusuf's words in the house of the Aziz are a well-known example.

**Sources cited:** Qur'an 12:23 (The Qur'an, Pickthall 1930); Qur'an 12:21–24 (The Qur'an); Qur'an 16:98 (seek refuge in Allah before reciting) (The Qur'an)

## Islam #4: "Al-Qadr: the Night of Power" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Al-Qadr
- **The promise on the card:** angels and the Spirit descend, peace until dawn (Surah 97)
- **Line to carry:** peace until dawn
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: Al-Bayyinah, and the verse that said all that was asked. Your line was sincere and upright. Did you do one good thing that no one will ever know about? Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Late in Ramadan, a mosque changes shape. In the last ten nights, the crowds grow. People who work all day come back at midnight and stay until the pre-dawn meal. Some sleep in the mosque. Children are woken up to join. They're looking for one night, and nobody knows for certain which one it is. Today's surah is five lines about that night: what it is, why it matters, and how it ends. Today: Al-Qadr, the Night of Power.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Surah ninety-seven, in Pickthall's translation: Lo! We revealed it on the Night of Power. Ah, what will convey unto thee what the Night of Power is! The Night of Power is better than a thousand months. The angels and the Spirit descend therein, by the permission of their Lord, with all decrees. The night is Peace until the rising of the dawn. Tradition holds that it's the night the Qur'an was first revealed. Qadr has two meanings, and scholars keep both: power, or honor, greatness; and measure, decree, the night on which matters for the year are measured out. A thousand months is about eighty-three years. One night, better than a whole life of ordinary nights. The Spirit, ar-ruh, is identified by commentators as the angel Jibril, who brought the revelation. So picture the sky that night: full, busy, angels descending. And yet the surah's last word on it isn't noise or power. It's salam. Peace, until the dawn. When is it? The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ said, in Bukhari: seek it in the odd nights of the last ten of Ramadan. The twenty-seventh is a night people watch especially closely, though scholars say it isn't certain, and some say it moves year to year. Some see the uncertainty as part of the gift: it keeps people awake for ten nights instead of one. Aisha asked the Prophet what she should say if she found it, and he taught her a short prayer asking Allah, who loves to pardon, to pardon her. Peace until dawn.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice for a coming morning: wake a little before dawn, and sit in the dark. No phone. For now, try a small version. Close your eyes, or dim the lights. Picture the sky just before sunrise, very still. Let the word salam, peace, rest on each out-breath. If you pray, you can ask for pardon in your own words, as Aisha was taught. Stay in the dark, and let it be peaceful. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Al-Qadr. Power and decree: the night the surah says the Qur'an was revealed, better than a thousand months, peace until dawn. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: peace until dawn. Tonight, before sleep, let the last thing you do be quiet: no screen, no argument, just a minute of peace in the dark. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the surah — tap the pairs."):

- qadr = power, and decree
- a thousand months = about eighty-three years
- ar-ruh = the Spirit, Jibril say commentators
- salam = peace until the dawn

*True or myth?*

- "everyone knows the Night of Power falls on the 27th" - **myth**: the Prophet said seek it in the odd nights of the last ten; the date isn't certain.
- "the Night of Power is better than a thousand months" - **true**: 97:3: a thousand months is about eighty-three years.
- "the surah says the Qur'an was revealed on this night" - **true**: We revealed it on the Night of Power (97:1).

*The fork (what would you do?):* Aisha asks the Prophet: if I find the Night of Power, what should I say?

1. ask for wealth for the year
2. ask Allah, who loves to pardon, to pardon her **(right answer)**
3. say nothing, and listen

Reveal: he taught her a short prayer asking for pardon (Tirmidhi). on this night, the request is forgiveness.

*In the original language:* سَلَامٌ هِيَ (say: sa-LAA-mun HI-ya). Arabic: peace it is, the opening of 97:5: peace until the rising of the dawn.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a special night in Ramadan.
- what it means: the night the Qur'an was first revealed, better than a thousand months, when angels descend, peace until dawn.
- what a scholar hears: qadr as power and as decree; Bukhari 2017 on the odd nights; Tirmidhi 3513 for Aisha's prayer; scholars differ whether the night is fixed or moves.

**How it's done (info card):** In the last ten nights of Ramadan many Muslims pray late into the night, read Qur'an and make supplication, seeking Laylat al-Qadr in the odd nights; some stay in the mosque in retreat (i'tikaf).

**Sources cited:** Qur'an 97:1–5 (The Qur'an, Pickthall 1930); Sahih al-Bukhari 2017 (seek it in the odd nights of the last ten) (Sahih al-Bukhari); Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3513 (Aisha's question) (Jami' at-Tirmidhi)

## Islam #5: "The elephant in the dark" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The elephant in the dark
- **The promise on the card:** people feel an elephant in a dark room, and each describes a different thing (Masnavi 3)
- **Line to carry:** a candle would help
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the reed flute, cut from its reed bed and crying to go home. Your line was listen to the reed. What sound made you ache? Day three hundred. Three hundred days on the path.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Two people describe the same event, the same boss, the same family dinner, and it sounds like they were at two different places. Each one is completely sure. Each one is partly right. And nobody's listening. In the third book of the Masnavi, Rumi tells a short story. Some people from India bring an elephant to a town where nobody has ever seen one, and they keep it in a dark house. People are curious, and they file in, one by one, to find out what an elephant is. It's too dark to see anything. So they reach out with their hands. Today, the elephant in the dark.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> One man's hand lands on the trunk, and he comes out and says: an elephant is like a water pipe. Another touches an ear: no, it's like a fan. Another feels a leg: it's a pillar. Another runs his palm along its back: it's like a throne. Each of them is describing something real. Each of them is wrong about the elephant. And Rumi adds the line the story turns on: if each of them had held a candle, their differences would have gone away. Rumi didn't invent the story. An older Persian poet, Sana'i, told a version, and a version was told in India centuries before either of them. It traveled far. But notice what Rumi does with it. He says the eye of the senses is like the palm of a hand: the palm can't hold the whole of the elephant. The problem isn't that the people are stupid, or lying. It's that they're in the dark, and they're trusting a hand to do the work of a light. For Rumi, the candle is the light that comes from God, the light of an inner sight that sees the whole. Readers have used the story in many ways since. At the very least, it asks for some humility: the thing you're touching is real, and it's probably not the whole elephant. A candle would help.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: hear someone's view without arguing. Sit, and breathe. Think of a person you disagree with, about something small or something big. Picture them in the dark room, describing the part of the elephant they're touching. What is their hand on? What's real in what they're saying? Just imagine listening to them to the end, without preparing your answer. If you pray, you can ask for light, for both of you. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The elephant in the dark. Rumi's story of people who each touch a part and call it the whole. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: a candle would help. Today, in one disagreement, ask one real question about what the other person is touching before you say what you're touching. Day three hundred.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what each one touched — tap the pairs."):

- the trunk = a water pipe

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A noodle shop on a busy street in a Chinese city. Steam on the windows. A cook slaps a rope of dough against the counter, stretches it, folds it, stretches it again, until it falls into the pot as a tangle of thin strands. The broth is beef. Everything here is Chinese, down to the chopsticks, and everything here is halal. Today: the Hui of China.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The Hui are Muslims who speak Chinese as their own language. Their story, as Hui families and historians tell it, begins with traders: Arab and Persian merchants who came to China's ports by sea and its cities by the Silk Road, and later others from Central Asia. Some stayed. They married local women, raised children who spoke Chinese, and kept the prayer. Over generations the newcomers became neighbors, and the neighbors became a people. That is the heart of the Hui story. They don't have a separate homeland language or a far-off country to point to. They are Chinese, all the way through, and Muslim, all the way through. Their word for halal carries the same double belonging. Qingzhen is usually rendered pure and true. It is written over restaurants and butcher shops, and it is also used for a mosque, a qingzhen si, a temple of the pure and true. Hui scholars went further. Writers such as Wang Daiyu and Liu Zhi explained Islam in classical Chinese, using the vocabulary of Confucian learning, so that Chinese readers could meet the faith in their own philosophy. There is a saying people quote about this land, often attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him: seek knowledge even as far as China. Hadith scholars have judged that saying weak, so it can't be leaned on as his words. But the Hui never needed it to belong. Their ancestors came, settled, and stayed. Chinese and Muslim.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

Then think about where you could find it, or how you could make a simple version this week. Before you eat it, if you are Muslim, you can begin with bismillah. If you're not, you can simply notice the hands that made it. The bell holds the time.
