# Judaism: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Judaism #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Judaism #1: "hello, goodbye, peace" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** shalom
- **The promise on the card:** hello, goodbye, peace — and a fourth meaning: whole
- **Line to carry:** be whole
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> One word, three jobs. In a café in Tel Aviv, someone comes in: shalom. Someone leaves: shalom. And on a greeting card, at the end of a letter, over a door: shalom, peace. Hello, goodbye, peace. The same word opens a conversation and closes it. But there's a fourth meaning hiding underneath the other three, and it's the one the rest of them grow from. Today, the first word on this path, and the wish that's folded inside it.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Shalom is Hebrew, and it grows from a root of three letters, shin, lamed, mem, and the root's first sense isn't quiet. It's whole. Complete. Nothing missing. The same root gives Hebrew shalem, whole, and a verb for paying back what you owe, so the account is square again. So peace, in this language, isn't only the absence of a fight. It's the presence of everything that should be there. A house with shalom has nothing broken in it. A body with shalom is well. When the Bible asks how someone is, it asks after their shalom. In Genesis, Jacob sends his son Joseph to check on his brothers, and the Hebrew says, more or less: go and see the shalom of your brothers. How whole are they? The word runs all through Jewish prayer. The priestly blessing, still said over congregations, and in some homes over the children on Friday night, ends with it. In the Jewish Publication Society's 1917 translation: "The LORD lift up His countenance upon thee, and give thee peace." Peace is the last word. The Amidah, the standing prayer, closes by asking for it too. And the Psalms don't treat peace as something that just happens to you. They say: "Seek peace, and pursue it." The rabbis noticed that verb. Other commandments, they taught, you do when the chance comes to you. Peace you go after. You chase it. So when someone says shalom at a door, coming or going, underneath the hello is an old wish: may you be whole. May nothing be missing.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Breathe in slowly, and notice one place in you that feels not quite whole right now. Tired. Worried. Rushed. On the way out, if you want to, say the word, out loud or inside: shalom. Not to fix anything. Just to wish it whole.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Shalom. Hello, goodbye, peace, and underneath all three: whole, with nothing missing. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: be whole. Once today, when you say hello or goodbye to someone, mean the old wish inside it: may nothing be missing for you. That's day one. You showed up. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- shalom = hello · goodbye · peace
- sh-l-m = whole, complete, nothing missing
- the priestly blessing = ends with peace
- seek peace = and pursue it

*True or myth?*

- "shalom just means "no fighting"" - **myth**: its root means whole: the presence of everything that should be there.
- "the priestly blessing ends with the word for peace" - **true**: "and give thee peace": shalom is its last word.
- "the Psalms say to wait for peace to arrive" - **myth**: "seek peace, and pursue it." the rabbis noticed: peace is chased.

*The fork (what would you do?):* Jacob sends Joseph to check on his brothers in the fields. in the Hebrew, what does he tell him to go and see?

1. whether the work is done.
2. the shalom of his brothers. **(right answer)**
3. how many sheep are left.

Reveal: go and see the shalom of your brothers: how whole they are. the Bible asks how someone is by asking after their shalom.

*In the original language:* שָׁלוֹם (say: sha-LOM). Hebrew: from the root sh-l-m, whole or complete.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a Hebrew hello.
- what it means: hello, goodbye and peace, all wishing one thing: may you be whole.
- what a scholar hears: the root sh-l-m also gives shalem, whole, and a verb for repaying a debt. peace is an account made square, nothing missing.

**How it's done (info card):** Shalom is said as hello and goodbye, and Shabbat shalom on the Sabbath; the priestly blessing, which ends with a prayer for peace, is said in synagogue and in some homes by parents over their children on Friday night.

**Sources cited:** Numbers 6:26 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Psalms 34:15 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Genesis 37:14 (Tanakh); Leviticus Rabbah 9:9 (other commandments when the chance comes; peace is pursued) (Midrash)

## Judaism #2: "even the animals rest" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** the Sabbath commandment
- **The promise on the card:** even the animals rest
- **Line to carry:** rest is for everyone
- **Length:** about 6:30 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: Genesis 1, and the refrain that comes seven times. Your line was it was good. Did you stop and look at one finished thing? Day seven, the end of your first week.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A farm in the world of the Torah, on the seventh day. The family isn't working. Fine. But look closer. The hired hand isn't working either. The foreign worker who lives at the edge of the village is sitting in the shade. And the ox, the one that pulls the plow six days a week, is standing in the field doing absolutely nothing, and nobody is allowed to put it to work. And one law wrote the animals in, by name. Today, at the end of week one: the commandment that rests everyone.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The Sabbath commandment is one of the Ten Words given at Sinai, the fourth in the Jewish count. It appears twice in the Torah, in two slightly different versions, and the difference is the lesson. In Exodus it begins: "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." And the reason given is creation: in six days God made the world, and on the seventh God rested. Rest is built into the world itself, right after the six days of making we met yesterday. In Deuteronomy, Moses repeats it, and it begins: "Observe the sabbath day, to keep it holy." This time the reason is different. It's Egypt. You were slaves, so rest: "that thy man-servant and thy maid-servant may rest as well as thou." As well as you. Not after you. Not instead of you. The same. Both versions list who rests: you, your son, your daughter, the servants, the stranger in your town, and the animals. Another law in Exodus puts it more plainly still, rest on the seventh day, "that thine ox and thine ass may have rest." The rabbis read those two versions as one. Remember and observe, they said, were spoken in a single utterance, the way only God could say two things at once. That's the two candles from day four. And so rest here isn't a treat you earn once your work is done. It's owed to everyone, and especially to whoever works for you.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths, with the bell, and a look back at your first week: shalom, the Shema, Shabbat, the candles, bracha, Genesis one, and today. On the first breath, think of someone who works so your life runs, a driver, a cleaner, a parent. On the second, wish them a real rest. On the third, give yourself one too.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The Sabbath commandment. Remember, and observe: rest, for you, your household, the stranger and even the animals. Seventh word. A full week.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: rest is for everyone. Today, make someone else's load lighter by one thing: a thank you, a task you take off them, an early finish. Day seven. Week one, done.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("two versions — tap the pairs."):

- remember = Exodus: because of creation
- observe = Deuteronomy: because of Egypt
- as well as thou = the servants rest the same
- the ox and the ass = rest too

*True or myth?*

- "the Sabbath commandment appears only once in the Torah" - **myth**: twice: remember in Exodus, observe in Deuteronomy.
- "the commandment includes animals" - **true**: the ox and the donkey rest with the household.
- "the two versions give the same reason" - **myth**: Exodus points to creation; Deuteronomy points to slavery in Egypt.

*The fork (what would you do?):* in Deuteronomy, Moses repeats the Sabbath commandment. what reason does he give for the servants' rest?

1. because God rested after creation.
2. because you were slaves in Egypt. **(right answer)**
3. because tired workers make mistakes.

Reveal: Egypt: you were slaves once, so your servants rest "as well as thou."

*In the original language:* זָכוֹר וְשָׁמוֹר (say: za-KHOR ve-sha-MOR). Hebrew: remember and observe, the opening words of the two versions.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a rule about going to worship.
- what it means: a rest owed to everyone in the house, down to the servants, the stranger and the ox.
- what a scholar hears: Exodus 20 grounds it in creation, Deuteronomy 5 in the exodus. the rabbis taught both were spoken in one utterance, a line Lecha Dodi sings on Friday night.

**How it's done (info card):** Jews read both versions of the Ten Commandments in the yearly Torah cycle, and the Friday-night hymn Lecha Dodi speaks in its first verse of remember and observe being said as one word.

**Sources cited:** Exodus 20:8 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Deuteronomy 5:12 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Deuteronomy 5:14 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Exodus 23:12 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:12–15 (Tanakh); Babylonian Talmud, Shevuot 20b (remember and observe in one utterance) (Talmud)

## Judaism #3: "Leaving Laban (Genesis 31)" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Leaving Laban
- **The promise on the card:** twenty hard years, stolen idols, and a heap of stones as a boundary of peace
- **Line to carry:** a heap of witness
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: Rachel's longing, and at last Joseph, may He add. Your line was long honestly. What did you let yourself say you hope for? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Jacob has been in Laban's house for twenty years now. He came with a walking stick; he has two wives, eleven sons, a daughter and huge flocks. And the mood has turned. Laban's sons grumble that Jacob got rich off their father. Laban's face, the Torah says, isn't toward him as it used to be. It's the moment, at a job or in a family, when the welcome has quietly run out. Then God speaks: go back to the land of your fathers, and I will be with you. Jacob calls Rachel and Leah out to the field, away from listening ears. And the two sisters, rivals until now, speak with one voice: our father has treated us like strangers. Whatever God told you, do it. Today: the escape.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> While Laban is away shearing sheep, Jacob loads everything onto camels and slips away. And Rachel, without telling Jacob, takes her father's household idols. Laban chases them for seven days and catches up in the hills of Gilead. But God has warned him in a dream: be careful what you say to Jacob. Laban searches every tent for his stolen gods. Rachel has hidden them in her camel's saddle, and she sits on them, and apologizes that she can't get up. So the household gods, the kind Abram walked away from long ago, end up sat on. Rashi, the medieval commentator, says she took them to pull her father away from idol worship. And then Jacob finally lets out twenty years of anger, in the old JPS translation: "in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep fled from mine eyes." You changed my wages ten times, he says. But the story doesn't end in a fight. It ends in a pile of stones. Jacob sets up a pillar, and the family heaps stones around it, and they eat there by the heap. Laban names it in Aramaic, Jegar-sahadutha; Jacob names it in Hebrew, Galeed. Two languages, one meaning: a heap of witness. And Laban adds: "The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another." This heap is a line. I won't cross it to harm you, and you won't cross it to harm me. Not friendship. Not love. A boundary, set in stone, so that peace can hold.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice. Settle, and breathe. Think of a relationship where something keeps getting crossed. A relative who drops by unannounced. A boss who texts at midnight. A friend who always borrows and forgets. You don't have to end anything. Picture a small heap of stones between you: a clear, calm line. What is the one sentence that would set it? Something like: I can talk after dinner, not during. Say it silently, kindly. If you pray, you can ask for a steady voice. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Leaving Laban. Twenty hard years, stolen idols, and a heap of stones named in two languages: a boundary that keeps the peace.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: a heap of witness. Today, set one boundary kindly, one clear sentence, said calmly, so the peace can hold. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the story — tap the pairs."):

- Rachel = hid the household idols in a saddle
- ten times = how often Laban changed the wages
- Galeed = a heap of witness, in Hebrew
- Jegar-sahadutha = the same name, in Aramaic

*True or myth?*

- "Jacob knew Rachel had taken her father's idols" - **myth**: she took them without telling him, and hid them in her camel's saddle.
- "the heap of stones got names in two languages" - **true**: Laban called it Jegar-sahadutha in Aramaic; Jacob called it Galeed in Hebrew. both mean heap of witness.
- "the heap marked a friendship pact" - **myth**: it marked a boundary: neither would cross it to harm the other.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Jacob. Laban has caught up with you after seven days, angry, and searched every tent. what happens next?

1. you fight, and part as enemies.
2. you set up a heap of stones as a boundary, and eat together. **(right answer)**
3. you go back to Laban's house.

Reveal: after Jacob's angry speech, they heap up stones, eat on them, and agree: neither will cross this line to harm the other.

*In the original language:* גַּלְעֵד (say: gal-AYD). Hebrew: a heap of witness. Jacob's name for the boundary heap.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: Jacob ran away from his father-in-law.
- what it means: after twenty hard years, the family leaves, and the quarrel ends in a heap of stones: a boundary that keeps peace.
- what a scholar hears: 31:47 gives the name in Aramaic, Jegar-sahadutha, and in Hebrew, Galeed: one heap, named in two languages.

**How it's done (info card):** Jews read Jacob's departure from Laban in the Torah portion Vayetze; Laban's words at the heap give the place a second name, Mizpah, the watchpost (Genesis 31:49).

**Sources cited:** Genesis 31:40 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Genesis 31:49 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Genesis 31:1–55 (Tanakh); Rashi on Genesis 31:19 (Rashi's commentary on the Torah)

## Judaism #4: "Vayera: here am I (Genesis 22:1, 7, 11)" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** hineni
- **The promise on the card:** Abraham answers "here am I" to God, to his son and to the angel
- **Line to carry:** here I am
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Isaac was born, and Sarah's laugh turned from doubt to joy. Your line was laugh with me. Who did you laugh with? Today, the last day of Vayera, and its hardest chapter. Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Someone calls your name from the next room. You say: yeah? What? In a minute. Half your mind is still on what you were doing. Now think of the few times you answered a call with all of you. A child crying at night, and you're up before you're awake. A friend on the phone, and you can hear that something's wrong, so you put everything down. There's a word in Hebrew for answering like that. It's not just yes. It means: I'm here, fully. Abraham says it three times in one chapter. Today's word.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The word is hineni. Here I am. The chapter is Genesis twenty-two, which Jews call the Akedah, the binding of Isaac. It's a hard story, and Jewish readers have argued over it for a long time. It begins: "God did prove Abraham, and said unto him: 'Abraham'; and he said: 'Here am I.'" Then God tells him to take his son, his only son, whom he loves, Isaac, and offer him up on a mountain. Abraham rises early and goes. On the third day, father and son walk up the mountain together, Isaac carrying the wood. And Isaac speaks: "My father." And Abraham says: "Here am I, my son." The same word. Isaac asks where the lamb is, and Abraham says God will provide. Then, at the top, when Abraham has bound Isaac and taken up the knife, an angel calls from heaven: Abraham, Abraham. And a third time he answers: here am I. And the angel says: don't lay your hand on the boy. Abraham sees a ram caught in a thicket and offers it instead. Isaac is not harmed. Readers disagree deeply about this story. Some see Abraham's complete faith. Some read it as a rejection of child sacrifice. Some modern readers ask why the man who argued for Sodom says nothing here. Whatever they make of it, readers keep coming back to the word. Hineni, to God. Hineni, to his son. Hineni, to the angel who stops him. Fully present to each. Later, when God calls, the prophet Isaiah will answer: "Here am I; send me." So that's Vayera: a visit to the sick, an argument for justice, a stranger's goodness, a laugh of joy, and here am I.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: say here I am, and mean it. Settle, and breathe. Silently say: here I am. Notice where you actually are: your body, this room, this moment. Then think of someone who might call your name today, a child, a parent, a friend, a coworker. Picture answering them not with in a minute, but with all of you: here I am. If you pray, you can say it to God too. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Hineni. Here I am: not just yes, but fully present, Abraham's answer to God, to his son, and to the angel.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: here I am. Today, when someone calls your name, put down what you're holding, turn, and answer with all of you. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("three calls, one answer — tap the pairs."):

- God calls = Abraham: here am I
- Isaac calls = here am I, my son
- the angel calls = Abraham, Abraham
- Isaiah = here am I; send me

*True or myth?*

- "Isaac is harmed on the mountain" - **myth**: the angel stops Abraham, and a ram is offered instead.
- "Jewish readers all agree on what the Akedah means" - **myth**: some see complete faith, some a rejection of child sacrifice, some modern readers ask why he didn't argue, as he did for Sodom.
- "Abraham answers 'here am I' three times in the chapter" - **true**: to God, to his son, and to the angel.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Abraham, walking up the mountain with Isaac. he says, "My father." how do you answer?

1. not now.
2. here am I, my son. **(right answer)**
3. keep walking in silence.

Reveal: "Here am I, my son." the same word he said to God, now to his son. fully present to each.

*In the original language:* הִנֵּנִי (say: hee-NAY-nee). Hebrew: here I am; fully present, ready.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: here I am just means I'm in the room.
- what it means: hineni is answering with all of you, and Abraham says it to God, to his son and to the angel.
- what a scholar hears: hineni at 22:1, 7, 11; Rashi on 22:1, 'a word of humility and readiness'; the Akedah read on Rosh Hashanah; Isaiah 6:8.

**How it's done (info card):** The binding of Isaac, the Akedah, is read on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, and some Jews recite it in the daily morning prayers; hineni is also the name of a prayer the cantor says before the additional (Musaf) service on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur in some communities.

**Sources cited:** Genesis 22:1 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Genesis 22:7 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Isaiah 6:8 (Tanakh, JPS 1917); Genesis 22:1–19 (Tanakh); Rashi on Genesis 22:1 (Rashi's commentary on the Torah)

## Judaism #5: "The defense attorney" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The defense attorney
- **The promise on the card:** Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev was famous for pleading his people's case before God
- **Line to carry:** always the defense
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday Simcha Bunim gave us two pockets and two notes. Your line was two pockets. Which note did you reach for? Day three hundred. Three hundred days on the path.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Some friends always take your side. You call them furious about someone, and they're furious with you. You call them ashamed of yourself, and they say: come on, you were exhausted, anyone would have done that. Sometimes they're too generous. But you always hang up feeling like someone is on your team. Long ago, in the town of Berditchev, in what is now Ukraine, there was a rebbe who played that role for his whole people, and he played it in the one courtroom that mattered most to him: before God.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> His name was Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev, a student of the Maggid of Mezritch. People called him the defense attorney of Israel. The Hebrew word is sanegor, an old word borrowed from Greek, meaning an advocate. Pirkei Avot already uses the courtroom picture: every good deed you do gains you an advocate, every wrong gains you an accuser. Levi Yitzchak decided his job was always to be the advocate. There are many stories. Here's one. One morning he saw a wagon driver standing by his wagon, wrapped in his prayer shawl and tefillin, praying. And at the same time, greasing the wheels of his wagon. Anyone else might have scolded him: you're praying and greasing wheels at the same time? Levi Yitzchak looked up and said, in effect: Master of the world, look at your people. Even while they grease their wagon wheels, they're praying to you. Same scene. Two ways to see it. He always chose the second. He's remembered for arguing with God, too, out loud, on behalf of suffering people, in the line of Moses, who pleaded for Israel after the golden calf. In this tradition, that's read not as disrespect but as a kind of love: taking both God and people seriously enough to argue. The stories come from Hasidic tradition and the details vary. But his reputation is solid: wherever others saw fault, he looked for the defense.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

## Judaism #7: "Ki Tavo: this day (Deuteronomy 26:16, 27:9)" (day 514, year 2)

- **Line to carry:** this day, new
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the farmer's story: arami oved avi. Your line was a wandering father. Did you hear, or tell, one line of family history? Today, two small words Moses keeps repeating. Day five hundred fourteen.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Remember the last time something was new. A phone with the plastic still on the screen. The first day at a job, when you noticed every hallway. A friendship in its first weeks, when every message felt important. Now picture that same thing a year later. The phone is scratched. The hallways are invisible. The messages are routine. Nothing changed but your eyes. Moses is speaking to a people who have lived with God's commandments for forty years. And he keeps calling it today.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Deuteronomy twenty-six, verse sixteen: "This day the LORD thy God commandeth thee to do these statutes and ordinances." This day. But many of these laws had been given at Sinai, long before. A chapter later, Moses and the priests say: "Keep silence, and hear, O Israel; this day thou art become a people unto the LORD thy God." This day? They'd been a people for four decades. In Hebrew, the word is hayom: today. Rashi, the great eleventh-century commentator from France, reads it as an instruction about how to hold something old. On the first verse he says, in effect: every day these commandments should be in your eyes as new, as though you'd been commanded them that very day. On the second: each day, see yourself as if you had entered the covenant today. It's a teaching about attention. The danger in a long practice usually isn't rebellion. It's autopilot. The prayer you can say without hearing it. The blessing muttered on the way to the first bite. The person across the table you've stopped really seeing. Notice that Rashi doesn't say pretend it's new. He says let it be new in your eyes. The text stays the same. The eyes are the part that can change. Jewish life is full of repetition built on this hope: the same prayers every morning, the same Torah read every year, and an expectation that this time something in them will catch you differently. Hayom. Not a date on a calendar. A way of looking.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: treat one old practice as new. Settle, and breathe. Choose something you do so often you've stopped noticing it. A blessing, a greeting, the way you make tea, the walk to the front door, saying goodnight to someone. Walk through it slowly in your mind, as if for the first time. What would a stranger notice? What would you see if you'd never done it before? Find one detail you'd stopped seeing. Later today, do the thing for real, with that detail in view. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: this day, new. Today, do one familiar thing as if it were the first time you'd ever done it. Day five hundred fourteen.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The Mishnah, in tractate Sotah, gives a list of things that may be said in any language. Among them: the Shema, the tefillah, which means the Amidah, and Birkat HaMazon, the grace after meals we met two days ago. The three pillars of daily prayer. Any language. The Talmud explains the Shema in a lovely way. Shema means hear. Some sages read it as: hear in any language that you hear, that you understand. If the words don't reach you, what have you heard? Grace after meals is a blessing, and you can't really thank someone in words you don't follow. The Amidah is a plea for mercy, and you plead in whatever words you have. Many authorities in Jewish law say it's best to pray in Hebrew if you understand it, and some say Hebrew should be used even if you don't. Reform prayer books include a great deal of the vernacular; Orthodox prayer books keep the Hebrew and print translations beside it. Communities weigh it differently. But the Mishnah's baseline stands: the Shema, the Amidah and grace are valid in a language you understand. In the Mishnah's view, prayer doesn't wait for the right accent.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: pray in your first language. Whatever language you dreamed in as a child, the one your grandmother scolded you in, use it now. If you pray, say a short prayer or blessing in it, slowly, and notice whether it lands differently. If you don't pray, say something you're grateful for in that language, out loud, and notice the same. If English is your only language, try saying it in the plainest English you can, the way you'd say it to a child. The bell holds the time.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> A man in a black beard and a formal coat stands at the front of a hall in Basel, Switzerland. In the seats are Jews from many countries: rabbis and students, doctors and businessmen, people who had never been in one room before. Many came in formal black coats, because he asked them to. He wanted the world to see that this was serious. He wrote plays, and he wrote for a Vienna newspaper. Now he was trying to win his people a state, starting with a meeting. His name is Theodor Herzl, and today's word is his name.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Herzl had not grown up dreaming of Zion. He was an assimilated Jew in Vienna, writing plays and reporting for a newspaper. What changed him, by his own telling, was what he saw of hatred against Jews, in Vienna and in France. Even Jews who did everything they were asked to do were not safe. So he wrote a short book called The Jewish State. Its argument was blunt: the hatred would not go away on its own, so Jews needed a homeland of their own, recognized by the nations, and they should go about it in the open, with diplomacy, money and organization. Then he did the organizing himself. He called the Zionist Congress in Basel, sought meetings with rulers and their ministers, and wrote letters without end. He also wrote a novel about the future land, and put a motto on its first page. In Hebrew it is known as im tirtzu: if you will it, it is no fairy tale. Here's what makes the motto interesting. It is a dreamer's line, and also a planner's line. The psalm about the return to Zion says, "We were like unto them that dream." Herzl's line agrees that it's a dream, and then insists that wanting it, and working for it, can make it real. Herzl did not live to see a state. And not every Jew agreed with him, not by a long way. Tomorrow you'll meet the many kinds of Zionism, and the Jews who opposed all of them. Today it's enough to see one man turn a longing into an agenda.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's today's practice: dream one practical dream. Sit back and breathe out slowly. Let one hope come up, something you have wanted for a long time and filed under someday. It can be small: a garden, a language, a call you've been meaning to make. Hold it for a moment as a dream. Then turn it over and ask the planner's question: what is the very first step, and when? Find one step small enough to take this week. The bell holds the time.
