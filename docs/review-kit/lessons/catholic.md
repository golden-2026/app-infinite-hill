# Catholic: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Catholic #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Catholic #1: "your grandmother before dinner" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** grace
- **The promise on the card:** your grandmother before dinner; a gift you didn't earn
- **Line to carry:** you are already held
- **Length:** about 5:45 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> The food is on the kitchen table, steam coming off the potatoes, and somebody's already reaching for the bread. Then a hand on the wrist. A grandmother, saying: wait. Grace first. Heads go down. A few quick words, a quick amen, and then everybody eats. A kid at a table like that could easily grow up thinking grace means the little speech before dinner. It does. But the word is much bigger than the speech. It's the reason for the speech. Today's word is the first one on this path, and the words after it will keep coming back to it.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Grace comes from the Latin word gratia. It means a favor. A gift. Something given freely, not paid for. It's the same root that gives English the word gratitude, and the word gratis, meaning free of charge. The New Testament was written in Greek, and there the word is charis, which also means a gift freely given, and a kind of loveliness. So when Catholics talk about grace, they mean God's gift of himself, given before anybody earned it. The letter to the Ephesians says it plainly, in the Douay-Rheims Bible, the old Catholic translation into English: "For by grace you are saved through faith, and that not of yourselves, for it is the gift of God." Not of yourselves. That's the surprise at the heart of the word. You don't climb up to grace. It comes down to you. Catholics say it arrives in many ways: in baptism, in the sacraments, in prayer, in an ordinary Tuesday when a friend calls at exactly the right moment. And here's where the dinner table comes back in. The traditional Catholic grace before meals goes like this: "Bless us, O Lord, and these thy gifts, which we are about to receive from thy bounty, through Christ our Lord. Amen." Listen to what it notices. Thy gifts. Thy bounty. The food on the table is a small picture of the big thing. Somebody grew it, somebody cooked it, and before any of that, Catholics say, somebody gave the world to begin with. So saying grace isn't paying a fee before eating. It's noticing that you were given something. That's the whole shape of today's word: a gift you didn't earn, and the moment you notice it.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Before you breathe, think of one thing you have today that you didn't earn. The air, maybe. A person. A roof. Then breathe in slowly, all the way, and let it out. If you pray, you can say thank you with that breath. If you don't, just let yourself notice the gift.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Grace. A favor, a gift freely given. Catholics say it comes first, before anyone earns it. First word on your strand.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line to carry: you are already held. Grace means the gift came first. Today, before one meal, even a snack at your desk, pause for a single second and notice that it was given to you. That's day one. See you tomorrow.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- gratia = Latin: a favor, a gift
- charis = Greek: a gift freely given
- gratis = free of charge
- thy bounty = from the grace before meals

*True or myth?*

- "grace only means the words said before dinner" - **myth**: the words are named after the bigger thing: gratia, a gift freely given.
- "grace and gratitude come from the same root" - **true**: both come from Latin gratia, a favor or gift.
- "in Catholic teaching, grace is earned by being good enough" - **myth**: "not of yourselves, for it is the gift of God." it comes first.

*The fork (what would you do?):* the letter to the Ephesians says people are saved by grace. where does it say grace comes from?

1. from trying hard enough.
2. from being born into the right family.
3. from God, as a gift. **(right answer)**

Reveal: "not of yourselves, for it is the gift of God." the whole point of the word is that it isn't earned.

*In the original language:* χάρις (say: KHAH-rees). Greek: grace, favor, a gift freely given. Latin translated it gratia.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: the little speech before dinner.
- what it means: a gift you didn't earn, and the moment you notice it.
- what a scholar hears: charis in Greek, gratia in Latin. the same Latin root gives English gratitude and gratis, free of charge.

**How it's done (info card):** Many Catholic families say a short grace before meals, often the traditional "Bless us, O Lord," and many make the sign of the cross before and after it.

**Sources cited:** Ephesians 2:8 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner, 1752)); Grace before meals (traditional Catholic table prayer, public domain (original): traditional English form); Catechism of the Catholic Church 1996–1999 (Catechism of the Catholic Church)

## Catholic #2: "not statues" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** the saints
- **The promise on the card:** not statues — people with terrible flaws who kept going
- **Line to carry:** the company you're in
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Mary: a young woman, a question, and a yes. Your line was: let it be. And that's a week. Grace, the sign of the cross, the Gospel, the sign of peace, the Eucharist, Mary. Day seven.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Walk into an old Catholic church and you'll see them everywhere. Plaster figures in niches. Faces in stained glass. A man holding keys, a woman holding a wheel, a guy with a lamb. They look calm and a little unreal, like people who never had a bad day in their lives. That's the picture most people carry of a saint: someone born good, floating above it all. Catholics would tell you that picture is almost exactly wrong. The man holding the keys once swore three times that he'd never met his best friend. Today's word: not statues, people.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Saint comes from the Latin sanctus, holy. In the New Testament, Paul writes letters to the saints in Rome and Corinth, and he means ordinary believers, people with jobs and arguments and debts. Later, the word came to mean especially the people the Church believes are now with God in heaven, and holds up as examples. So take the man with the keys. That's Peter. On the night Jesus was arrested, Peter was asked three times whether he knew him, and three times he said no. Then a rooster crowed, and the Gospels say he went out and wept bitterly. That's the first leader of the apostles, the one Catholics count as the first pope. Or take Augustine, one of the greatest thinkers the Church ever had. In his Confessions he admits that as a young man he prayed, more or less, make me pure, but not yet. Or Paul himself, who spent his early years hunting down Christians. The saints aren't people who never fell. They're people who fell and kept getting up. Here's the part Catholics love. They believe in what the old creed calls the communion of saints: that the living and the dead who belong to Christ are one family, and the family can still pray for each other. So a Catholic might ask a saint to pray for them the way you'd ask a grandmother. The statues are something like family photos on the wall. Nobody prays to the plaster. It's a reminder of who else is in the room. Other Christians see it differently: many Protestants honor the saints as examples and don't ask them for prayers. But the stories are the same stories. Flawed people, still going.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for the end of week one. Three breaths, with the bell. Think of one person, alive or gone, who failed at something and kept going, and who you're glad you knew. On the first breath, picture their face. On the second, remember what they got wrong. On the third, remember that they kept going. If you pray, you can thank God for them.

**The word** (spoken, 20 sec)

> The saints. Holy ones: people who fell and got up, and who Catholics believe are still one family with the living. Seventh word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: the company you're in. Today, when you mess something up, remember that the saints did too, and kept going. Day seven. One week.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("who's in the company — tap the pairs."):

- Peter = said no three times, then wept
- Augustine = make me pure, but not yet
- Paul = once hunted down Christians
- sanctus = Latin: holy

*True or myth?*

- "saints are people who never did anything wrong" - **myth**: Peter denied Jesus three times. Paul hunted Christians. they fell and got up.
- "in Paul's letters, saint means ordinary believers" - **true**: he writes to the saints in Rome and Corinth: the whole church.
- "Catholics pray to the statue itself" - **myth**: the statue is a reminder, like a family photo. Catholics ask the saint in heaven to pray for them.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Peter, standing by a fire the night Jesus is arrested. a servant girl asks if you know him. what does Peter do?

1. says yes, and is arrested too.
2. says no, three times. **(right answer)**
3. says nothing and walks away.

Reveal: three times he says he doesn't know him. then the rooster crows, and he weeps. Catholics count him the first pope.

*In the original language:* ἅγιος (say: HAH-gee-os). Greek: holy, set apart. Paul's word for all believers; Latin sanctus.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: perfect people carved in plaster.
- what it means: flawed people who kept going, and who Catholics believe are still one family with the living.
- what a scholar hears: hagioi, Paul's name for every believer; later, formal canonization; the communion of saints, sanctorum communio, from the Apostles' Creed.

**How it's done (info card):** Catholics celebrate saints' feast days through the year and on All Saints' Day, November 1; many are named for a saint and ask the saints, especially their patron saint, to pray for them.

**Sources cited:** Matthew 26:69–75 (The Bible); Augustine, Confessions 8.7 (Confessions); Romans 1:7; 1 Corinthians 1:2 (The Bible); Apostles' Creed: the communion of saints; Catechism of the Catholic Church 946–962 (Apostles' Creed; Catechism of the Catholic Church)

## Catholic #3: "The basin and towel (John 13:1–15)" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** The basin and towel
- **The promise on the card:** at the last supper he kneels and washes their feet
- **Line to carry:** as I have done to you
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the perfume, a jar broken over his head. Your line was she has done a beautiful thing. What did you give lavishly? Day sixty. Today, the last supper, and a towel.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Sandals, dusty roads, animals in the streets. When guests came in from the road, water was brought for their feet. In the scriptures, washing someone else's feet is a servant's work: when David sends for Abigail, she answers that she will wash the feet of his servants. Now it's the night before Jesus dies. He's at supper with his closest friends, and John says he knows his hour has come. John begins the scene with a sentence like a doorway, in the Douay-Rheims: "having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them unto the end." Unto the end. To the very last. And then, in the middle of the meal, he gets up from the table. Today: the basin and the towel.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> John slows down and gives every movement. He rises from supper. He lays aside his outer garments. He takes a towel and ties it around his waist. He pours water into a basin. And he begins to wash the disciples' feet, and to dry them with the towel. The teacher is on his knees, doing the servant's job, going from one friend to the next. And John has already told us that Judas, who will betray him, is at that table. His feet get washed too. Then he comes to Peter, and Peter can't bear it. Lord, are you going to wash my feet? Jesus says: you don't understand now, but you will. And Peter: "Thou shalt never wash my feet." Jesus answers: "If I wash thee not, thou shalt have no part with me." And Peter swings the other way: then not only my feet, Lord, but my hands and my head. When he's finished, Jesus puts his garments back on, sits down, and asks: do you know what I have done to you? You call me Teacher and Lord, and you're right. So if I've washed your feet, you ought to wash one another's. "For I have given you an example, that as I have done to you, so you do also." Catholics relive this on Holy Thursday, at the Mass of the Lord's Supper. Where the rite is kept, the priest sets aside his outer vestment, kneels, and washes the feet of people from the parish. The rite's old Latin name is the Mandatum, from Jesus's new commandment later that night, and that word is where the English name Maundy Thursday comes from.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: do one humble task for someone. Settle and breathe. Think of the jobs nobody wants in your home, your office, your group. Cleaning the bathroom. Taking out the recycling. Staying late to put the chairs away. Changing the diaper. Pick one you could do today for someone else, without being asked, and without mentioning it afterward. Picture yourself doing it slowly, with care, the way John describes the towel and the basin. If you pray, you can offer it for that person. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> The basin and towel. The night before he died, Jesus knelt and washed his friends' feet, and told them to do the same. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: as I have done to you. Today, do the humble task quietly, and let nobody thank you for it. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the story — tap the pairs."):

- the towel = tied around his waist
- Peter = thou shalt never wash my feet
- Judas = at the table, feet washed too
- Mandatum = Latin: the Holy Thursday foot washing

*True or myth?*

- "Jesus skips Judas when he washes their feet" - **myth**: John has told us Judas is at the table. Jesus washes the disciples' feet, Judas included.
- "Peter at first refuses to let Jesus wash his feet" - **true**: “Thou shalt never wash my feet.” then: not only my feet, but my hands and my head.
- "Maundy Thursday gets its name from the Latin mandatum" - **true**: from Jesus's new commandment, given that same night.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Peter. your teacher is kneeling in front of you with a basin and a towel, about to wash your feet. what do you say?

1. thank you, Lord.
2. you'll never wash my feet. **(right answer)**
3. let me wash yours first.

Reveal: “Thou shalt never wash my feet.” Jesus answers: “If I wash thee not, thou shalt have no part with me.”

*In the original language:* ὑπόδειγμα (say: hoo-PO-dayg-mah). Greek: an example, a pattern to copy. What Jesus says he has given them.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: Jesus washes feet to show he's humble.
- what it means: on his last night, loving them to the end, he does the servant's job for all of them, Judas too, and says: do this for each other.
- what a scholar hears: hypodeigma, a pattern to copy. the Holy Thursday rite is the Mandatum, the root of Maundy.

**How it's done (info card):** At the Mass of the Lord's Supper on Holy Thursday, the priest washes the feet of members of the congregation, a rite called the Mandatum, after the Gospel of John 13 is read.

**Sources cited:** John 13:1 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner)); John 13:8 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner)); John 13:8 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner)); John 13:15 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner)); John 13:2–7, 9–14, 34 (The Bible); 1 Samuel 25:41 (The Bible)

## Catholic #4: "From the rising of the sun (Psalm 112 [113])" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** From the rising of the sun
- **The promise on the card:** God stoops down to lift the poor from the dust
- **Line to carry:** he raises the needy
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Vespers, and a priest for ever like Melchisedech. Your line was a priest for ever. Did you stop at sunset? Today, a psalm that runs from sunrise to sunset. Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Right now, somewhere on earth, the sun is rising. Somewhere else it's setting. It's always coming up on someone's morning. Which means that at every single moment, someone somewhere is waking up, and someone is going to bed. A short psalm takes that fact and turns it into praise that never stops, a relay around the planet. And then, halfway through, it looks down from the sky, all the way down to the garbage heap at the edge of town.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Psalm 112 in the Douay, 113 in most Bibles. It opens: "Praise the Lord, ye children: praise ye the name of the Lord." Children here follows the Latin, pueri, a word that can also mean servants, and the Hebrew does say servants: you who serve the Lord. Then the sun: "From the rising of the sun unto the going down of the same, the name of the Lord is worthy of praise." East to west, morning to night. Then it climbs as high as it can go. God is high above all nations, his glory above the heavens. Who is like the Lord our God, who dwells on high? And then the great surprise. This God looks down, on the low things in heaven and in earth. And the psalm keeps going down: "Raising up the needy from the earth, and lifting up the poor out of the dunghill: That he may place him with princes, with the princes of his people." The dunghill was probably the town refuse heap, often explained as the place where the poorest people slept and begged. The God above the heavens stoops all the way down to the dump and seats someone from there at the royal table. Then one more: the barren woman is given a home, the joyful mother of children. Hannah sings nearly the same lines about the poor and the dunghill when her son is born, and Mary sings the same turning-over of the world in the Magnificat. This psalm begins the Hallel, the psalms of praise sung at Passover. Some readers think these were the hymn Jesus and his friends sang at the Last Supper before going out to the Mount of Olives.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: lift someone's spirits today. Settle, and breathe. Bring to mind one person who's low right now: discouraged, overlooked, stuck, sitting on their own small dunghill. Picture them. Now think of one small thing that might lift them a little: a message, a call, a compliment that's true, a coffee, an invitation to sit at your table. If you pray, you can pray for them in the psalm's words: raising up the needy. If you don't, simply hold them in mind, and decide on your one small thing. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> From the rising of the sun. Praise running around the world all day, to the God who stoops to lift the poor. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: he raises the needy. Today, do the one small thing you chose for that person, before the sun goes down. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the psalm — tap the pairs."):

- pueri = children, or servants
- the rising of the sun = to the going down of the same
- the dunghill = where God lifts the poor from
- the Hallel = psalms of praise sung at Passover

*True or myth?*

- "the psalm's God is too high to notice the poor" - **myth**: he looks down on the low things, and lifts the poor out of the dunghill.
- "Hannah's song uses almost the same lines about the poor and the dunghill" - **true**: and Mary's Magnificat sings the same turning-over of the world.
- "the Hebrew says ye children, as the Douay does" - **myth**: the Hebrew says servants; the Latin pueri can mean children or servants.

*The fork (what would you do?):* the psalm has just called God high above the heavens. where does it go next?

1. higher still, into the stars.
2. all the way down, lifting the poor from the dunghill to sit with princes. **(right answer)**
3. to the king's palace, to praise the king.

Reveal: raising up the needy from the earth, and lifting up the poor out of the dunghill, that he may place him with princes. the higher God is, the lower he stoops.

*In the original language:* Laudate, pueri (say: lau-DAH-teh POO-eh-ree). Latin: praise, O servants (or children), the psalm's first words and its name at Vespers.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: a general line of praise.
- what it means: praise running from sunrise to sunset, to a God who stoops from the heavens to the dump to lift the poor.
- what a scholar hears: the psalm opens the Hallel sung at Passover, and some readers think it was part of the hymn sung after the Last Supper.

**How it's done (info card):** Catholics pray Psalm 112 [113] at Evening Prayer on some Sundays and on feasts, and hear it at Mass; in Latin it is known as Laudate pueri, and composers have set it for Vespers.

**Sources cited:** Psalm 112:1 [113:1] (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner, 1752)); Psalm 112:3 [113:3] (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner, 1752)); Psalm 112:7–8 [113:7–8] (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner, 1752)); Psalm 112:4–6, 9 [113:4–6, 9] (The Bible); 1 Kings 2:8 in the Douay (1 Samuel 2:8); Luke 1:52–53 (The Bible); Matthew 26:30 (The Bible)

## Catholic #5: "Angels (CCC 328–336)" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** angel
- **The promise on the card:** spiritual creatures, messengers, and a guardian beside each person
- **Line to carry:** never alone
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, why evil: the hard question, and the Catechism's honest answer that no quick answer will do. Your line was no quick answer. Did you show up for someone? Today, day three hundred, and some company for the road. Day three hundred.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Angels are everywhere in our culture. Chubby babies with harps on greeting cards. Women in white robes on top of Christmas trees. People say someone who died has become an angel now. A sweet kid gets called an angel. Almost none of that is what the Catholic Church actually teaches about angels. What it does teach is stranger, and in a way more comforting: that you have never, for one moment of your life, been completely alone. Today: angels.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> First, the word. Angel comes from the Greek angelos, messenger. Augustine made a sharp point about it, and the Catechism repeats it: angel is the name of their job, not their nature. What they are is spirit. What they do is carry God's messages. Think of Gabriel at Nazareth, telling Mary she would bear a son. Second, what they are. The Catechism, from paragraph 328, says the existence of angels is a truth of faith. They are purely spiritual creatures, with intelligence and will, personal and immortal. And they're created. They are not God, and, despite the greeting cards, they're not people who died. When someone dies, Catholics pray they're with God, and the Church teaches they don't become angels. Third, the guardian. Jesus says, in Matthew, in the Douay-Rheims: "See that you despise not one of these little ones: for I say to you, that their angels in heaven always see the face of my Father who is in heaven." And a psalm promises: "For he hath given his angels charge over thee; to keep thee in all thy ways." The Catechism says that from infancy to death, human life is surrounded by the angels' watchful care, and quotes Saint Basil: beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd. Fourth, never alone. Catholics keep the feast of the Guardian Angels on October 2, and there's a short traditional prayer to the guardian angel, often taught to children. It's a small, steady comfort: on the dark nights, in the hospital hallway, on the drive home, Catholics believe someone God sent is keeping watch.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: the guardian angel prayer. It's short enough for a child to learn by heart. - **The promise on the card:** a great Franciscan preacher, now asked to find lost things
- **Line to carry:** finder of lost things
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, Barnabas, who walked Saul into a room that feared him. Your line was son of encouragement. Whose good did you say out loud? Day one thousand.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Keys. Glasses. A ring. A passport the night before a flight. Somewhere, right now, someone is turning a couch cushion over again and saying, half joking and half not, Saint Anthony, help. Day one thousand on this path, and the saint for it is one whose name gets said in kitchens and parking lots. But that's not how he started. He started as a voice that filled a square. Today, Anthony of Padua.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Anthony was born in Lisbon, in Portugal, and became a canon, then a Franciscan friar, drawn to the order of Francis of Assisi while Francis was still alive. He hoped to go to Morocco as a missionary. Illness sent him back, a storm blew his ship off course, and he ended up in Italy, an unknown friar. Then one day, the story goes, at an ordination, no one was ready to preach, and Anthony was told to say something. He opened his mouth, and the room discovered he knew the Scriptures deeply. From then on he preached across Italy and France, to crowds that filled churches and spilled into squares. The Church later named him a Doctor of the Church. So how does a great preacher become the saint of lost keys? The story tradition tells is small. Tradition says a young friar who was leaving the order took Anthony's book of psalms with him, a book Anthony had filled with notes for his preaching. Anthony prayed for it. The young man returned the book, and came back to the community too. Something precious was lost, and found, and a person along with it. That's an old pattern in the Gospels. Jesus tells of a woman who sweeps her whole house for one silver coin, and when she finds it she calls in the neighbors. In the Douay-Rheims: "Rejoice with me, because I have found the groat which I had lost." In Catholic teaching, asking Anthony's help isn't a lucky charm. It's asking a friend in heaven to pray with you, as you'd ask a friend at the table. And some pray to him for things bigger than keys: a lost faith, a lost child, a friendship gone quiet.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: pray for something lost. Settle, and breathe. Let one lost thing come to mind. Maybe it's small and annoying. Maybe it's not a thing at all: a habit of prayer, a person you've drifted from, a version of yourself you miss. Name it plainly, once. Don't search yet. Just hold it, the way the woman lit her candle before she swept. If you pray, you can ask Saint Anthony to pray with you for it. If you don't, simply let yourself admit you'd like it back. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Anthony of Padua. The Franciscan preacher from Lisbon, now asked by Catholics to help them find what's lost.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: finder of lost things. Today, help someone else find something, or reach out once to someone you've lost touch with. Day one thousand.

**Games after the talk**

*True or myth?*

- "Anthony was Italian by birth" - **myth**: he was born in Lisbon, in Portugal; Padua is where he's remembered by name.
- "he was first known as a famous preacher" - **myth**: he arrived in Italy unknown; the story says he was asked to preach without warning.
- "the lost-things story involves a book of psalms" - **true**: tradition says a departing friar took Anthony's annotated psalter and brought it back.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're a young friar leaving the order. you've taken Anthony's book of psalms, full of his notes. what does the story say happens?

1. you sell it and never look back.
2. you return the book, and come back yourself. **(right answer)**
3. Anthony writes a new one and forgets it.

Reveal: tradition says the young man brought the book back and returned to the community: both were found.

*In the original language:* εὗρον (say: HEW-ron). Greek: 'I found' — the woman's cry over her lost coin in Luke 15:9.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: the saint you call on for car keys.
- what it means: a great preacher whom Catholics ask to pray with them for anything lost.
- what a scholar hears: the lost-things devotion traces back to a tradition about his annotated psalter; the Church named him a Doctor of the Church.

**How it's done (info card):** The Church keeps the memorial of Saint Anthony of Padua on 13 June; Catholics ask his intercession when something is lost, and some parishes bless and hand out bread on his feast, called Saint Anthony's bread, for the poor.

**Sources cited:** Luke 15:9 (The Bible, Douay-Rheims (Challoner)); Luke 15:8 (The Bible); Early lives of Saint Anthony (Assidua); Roman Calendar, 13 June (Saints' lives and General Roman Calendar)
