# Sikhism: 10 sample lessons

For the review kit (see ../README.md and ../rubric.md). These are the lessons exactly as written in the app's lesson scripts, laid out to read. In the app the "spoken" parts are read aloud by a voice while short lines appear on screen, and the games come after the talk. Score each one as "Sikhism #N".

Sampled days: #1 = day 1, #2 = day 7, #3 = day 60, #4 = day 180, #5 = day 300, #6 = day 420, #7 = day 514, #8 = day 640, #9 = day 820, #10 = day 1000. Year 1 is days 1 to 331, year 2 is 332 to 696, year 3 is 697 to 1,061. The same day numbers are used for every tradition, so nobody picked the best ones.

Each lesson ends with a folded section "notes the writers left for a scholar". Learners never see these. They show what the writers were unsure about.

## Sikhism #1: "a kitchen in amritsar feeds a hundred thousand a day" (day 1, year 1)

- **Today's word:** seva
- **The promise on the card:** a kitchen in Amritsar feeds a hundred thousand a day for free; the word for that
- **Line to carry:** one quiet thing for someone
- **Length:** about 6:00 minutes

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> In Amritsar, in northern India, next to the Golden Temple, there's a kitchen that never closes. Anyone can walk into its dining hall and eat. No ticket. No question about who you are or what you believe. Lentils, bread, rice, a sweet pudding. People at the temple speak of tens of thousands of meals on an ordinary day, and a hundred thousand or more on festival days. And much of the work is done by volunteers. A banker chopping onions. A teenager scrubbing steel plates. A grandmother rolling bread she'll never see eaten. Nobody's keeping score. There's a word for what those hands are doing, and it's your first word on this path.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The word is seva. Selfless service. Work done for others with no reward in mind. It's a word Sikh children grow up hearing, and you'll see it everywhere once you know it: people cleaning the floors of a gurdwara, the Sikh house of worship, taking shoes at the door and handing them back polished, cooking, washing up, pouring water for strangers. Here's what makes seva different from being nice. It's done as part of the spiritual life, not beside it. In the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, the Gurus return to the idea again and again: service done with a humble heart is a way of drawing near to God. Not a warm-up for the real practice. The practice itself. Hands in the dishwater count. And seva has a condition that matters: it's meant to be done without ego. The hymns warn that service done to be seen, to be thanked or to be admired quietly turns into something else. That's why so much of it happens where nobody's watching. The person who scrubbed your plate at the Golden Temple doesn't know your name, and you'll never know theirs. That's not an accident. That's the point. Sikh teaching names three kinds of seva: with your body, like cooking or cleaning; with your mind, like teaching or listening; and with what you have, like giving money or food. So there's no one who can't do it. A kid can hand out water. Someone who can't stand for long can listen. And if you've seen Sikh volunteers turning up with hot meals after a flood or a fire, far from home, for people they'll never meet again, now you know the word they'd use for it.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Here's the practice for day one, and it's small on purpose. One breath, with the bell. Breathe in, and bring to mind one person who could use a hand today. Not a grand gesture. A dish, a text, a door held. Breathe out, and picture yourself doing it without telling anyone. That's all. One breath, one person, one quiet thing.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Seva. Selfless service, done for others with no reward in mind, and part of the spiritual life itself. First word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: one quiet thing for someone. Today, do one small act of service and don't mention it to anyone. Not online, not over dinner. Let it stay quiet. Day one.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the word — tap the pairs."):

- seva = selfless service
- with your body = cooking, cleaning, washing up
- with your mind = teaching, listening
- with what you have = giving money or food

*True or myth?*

- "the kitchen in Amritsar charges a small fee" - **myth**: it's free to anyone, whoever they are and whatever they believe.
- "much of the work in that kitchen is done by volunteers" - **true**: much of the cooking and washing up is seva: unpaid, and done by people from every walk of life.
- "seva counts most when people see you do it" - **myth**: the hymns warn that service done to be admired turns into something else. it's meant to be done without ego.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you've spent the morning scrubbing plates in a gurdwara kitchen. someone asks for your name so they can thank you publicly. what fits seva?

1. give your name and enjoy the thanks.
2. smile, say it was nothing, and keep washing. **(right answer)**
3. ask them to post about it.

Reveal: seva is meant to be done without ego. the whole kitchen runs on people nobody will ever name.

*In the original language:* ਸੇਵਾ (say: SAY-vaa). Punjabi, in Gurmukhi script: service, especially service offered freely.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: volunteering.
- what it means: selfless service done as part of the spiritual life itself, without ego, for anyone.
- what a scholar hears: the word is older than the Sikh path and neighbors use it too. in Sikh life it's woven into the gurdwara itself: the kitchen, the floor, the shoes at the door, the stranger.

**How it's done (info card):** Sikhs do seva in the gurdwara by cooking and serving langar, cleaning, washing dishes and looking after shoes, and outside it through relief work and community service, usually as unpaid volunteers.

**Sources cited:** Guru Granth Sahib, passim (seva and humble service as a way to God) (Guru Granth Sahib); Langar at Sri Harmandir Sahib (the Golden Temple), Amritsar (Sikh practice)

## Sikhism #2: "remembrance" (day 7, year 1)

- **Today's word:** simran
- **The promise on the card:** remembrance; the name on the breath
- **Line to carry:** remember on the inhale
- **Length:** about 6:15 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday: the Mool Mantar, line two. Nirbhau, nirvair. Without fear, without hate. Did one tense conversation go a little differently? Day seven, the end of your first week.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> You walk into a room and forget why you came in. You set your keys down and two minutes later they're gone. You meet someone at a party and their name falls out of your head before they've finished saying it. We forget all day long. Mostly it's harmless. But there's a deeper kind of forgetting too: whole days when you never once remember what matters to you, what you're grateful for, what you believe is real. You just get carried along. Sikhs have a word for the opposite of that, and a surprisingly physical way of doing it. Today, the word that ties this first week together.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The word is simran. Remembrance, calling to mind. In Sikh practice it means remembering God, and especially remembering the name, the naam you met on day five, the whole presence of what is real. How do you remember something all day long? You tie it to something you do all day long anyway. You breathe. So one of the simplest forms of simran is this: breathe in with Wahe, breathe out with guru. The word from day two, riding on the breath. Some Sikhs do it sitting still in the early morning, some with a string of prayer beads, some while they drive, cook or fall asleep. It can be sung aloud with a whole room, or said silently, with no one around you any the wiser. The fifth Guru, Guru Arjan, wrote a long hymn called Sukhmani, the jewel of peace, and it keeps circling back to simran: remember, and remember, and remember, and find peace; remembering the name, he says, pain and worry fall away. And notice how this first week fits together. Seva, serving with your hands. Waheguru, the name that is a cry of wonder. Nanak, who kept asking what things were for. Ik Onkar, there is one. The root verse: truth is the name, without fear, without hate. Simran is how all of that stays with you after the lesson ends. Not by thinking harder. By remembering, gently, again and again, on the breath.

**The practice** (spoken, 1 min)

> Three breaths, with the bell. If you'd like to try simran in one way Sikhs teach it: breathe in slowly and let the word Wahe ride in with it. Breathe out and let guru ride out. Three times. If that word isn't yours, just remember one thing on each inhale that you don't want to forget today.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Simran. Remembrance: calling God, the name, to mind, again and again, often on the breath. Seventh word. One week.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: remember on the inhale. Today, three times, at a red light, a kettle, a doorway, take one breath and remember. Next week: the kitchen, the work, the sharing. Day seven.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("this week's words — tap the pairs."):

- seva = serving with your hands
- Ik Onkar = there is one
- nirbhau, nirvair = without fear, without hate
- simran = remembrance

*True or myth?*

- "simran only happens sitting still in a gurdwara" - **myth**: it can be done while driving, cooking or falling asleep, silently or aloud.
- "a simple form of simran ties Waheguru to the breath" - **true**: wahe on the way in, guru on the way out.
- "Guru Arjan's Sukhmani keeps coming back to remembrance" - **true**: remember, and remember, and find peace.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you want to remember something all day long, without carrying a notebook. what do you tie it to?

1. an alarm on your phone every hour.
2. your breath, which goes with you everywhere. **(right answer)**
3. a note stuck to the fridge.

Reveal: simran rides on the breath: Wahe in, guru out. you breathe all day anyway.

*In the original language:* ਸਿਮਰਨ (say: SIM-run). Punjabi, in Gurmukhi script: remembrance, calling to mind.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: some kind of meditation.
- what it means: remembrance: calling God and the name to mind, again and again, often on the breath.
- what a scholar hears: simran is related to the Sanskrit smarana, remembering. Guru Arjan's Sukhmani, the jewel of peace, returns to it again and again.

**How it's done (info card):** Sikhs practice simran by repeating Waheguru, silently or aloud, often in the early morning, sometimes with prayer beads, and also by singing the scripture together in the gurdwara.

**Sources cited:** Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 262 onward (Sukhmani Sahib of Guru Arjan, on simran) (Guru Granth Sahib); Sikh practice of naam simran with Waheguru (Sikh practice)

<details><summary>Notes the writers left for a scholar (not shown to learners)</summary>

- Sukhmani Sahib paraphrased ('remember, and remember, and remember, and find peace'; 'pain and worry fall away'), from its opening ashtpadi at Ang 262. Confirm the paraphrase and Ang.
- Breath practice (Wahe on the in-breath, guru on the out-breath) is a common teaching; some teach other patterns. Confirm.
- Simran connected to Sanskrit smarana; confirm.
- Week-end look-back names seva, Waheguru, Nanak, Ik Onkar and the Mool Mantar.

## Sikhism #3: "The house with four doors" (day 60, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Harmandir Sahib
- **The promise on the card:** Guru Arjan builds the Harmandir Sahib low in the pool with doors on four sides, open to all
- **Line to carry:** open on every side
- **Length:** about 6:45 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, a jealous brother, and a Guru who answered with work, not revenge. Your line was pray for the difficult one. Did you wish them well, even once? Day sixty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Think about how important buildings are usually designed. They sit up high, on a hill or on a platform. There's a grand staircase going up. There's one main entrance, maybe with a guard. The meaning is clear: this place is above you, and you come in by permission, through the front. Now picture the pool at Amritsar, the one Guru Ram Das began. His son, Guru Arjan, finished it, and then he did something unexpected. In the middle of the water, he began to build a house of God. And almost every choice he made about that building turns the usual meaning upside down. Today, the house in the middle of the pool.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> It's called the Harmandir Sahib. Hari is a name for God, mandir means temple or house, and Sahib is a word of respect. The house of God. Many people know it as the Golden Temple, because of the gold that covers it; that came much later, in the nineteenth century. Here's what Guru Arjan did. First, instead of raising it high, he set it low. You walk down steps to reach it, so everyone who enters, rich or poor, comes down a little. Humility is built into the architecture. Second, instead of one grand entrance, he gave it doors on all four sides. In a world where people were sorted by caste and by creed, this house was open from every direction, to everyone. North, south, east, west. Remember Guru Nanak's feet toward the Kaaba, and the question: which way is God not? Here's that question turned into a building. And tradition adds one more detail. Many Sikhs say that the foundation stone was laid by a Muslim Sufi, Mian Mir, a friend of the Guru's from Lahore. Historians debate that story, but it has been told for a very long time, and it fits the house. The building was finished around 1604. It stands in the middle of the pool, reached by a causeway, and to this day the langar there feeds tens of thousands of people every day, of every faith and none. The house with four doors is still open on every side. What Guru Arjan placed at its center is the story of the next few days.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: open a door and leave it open. Settle first. Then, if you can, get up and open a door: your front door, a window, the door to your room. Stand in it for a moment. Feel the air come through. Think of who you'd welcome through it, and who you might have kept out without meaning to. Leave it open, for a while, if it's safe. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Harmandir Sahib. The house of God at Amritsar, set low in the pool with doors on four sides, open to all. Today's word.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: open on every side. Today, open one door you've kept shut: a conversation, an invitation, a seat at your table. Day sixty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the house — tap the pairs."):

- Harmandir = the house of God
- set low = you walk down to enter
- four doors = open from every direction
- Mian Mir = a Sufi, the foundation stone, tradition says

*True or myth?*

- "the Harmandir Sahib was covered in gold from the start" - **myth**: the gold came much later, in the nineteenth century.
- "it has doors on all four sides" - **true**: open from every direction, to everyone.
- "it was built on a hill so pilgrims climb up" - **myth**: it's set low; you walk down steps to enter.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you're Guru Arjan, building a house of God in the middle of a pool. how many doors does it get?

1. one grand door, facing east.
2. doors on all four sides. **(right answer)**
3. no doors: only priests may enter.

Reveal: Guru Arjan gave it four doors, one on each side, so it was open from every direction, to everyone.

*In the original language:* ਹਰਿਮੰਦਰ ਸਾਹਿਬ (say: HUR-mun-dur SAA-hib). Gurmukhi: Harmandir Sahib, hari, God, and mandir, house or temple; also called Darbar Sahib, the Golden Temple.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: the Golden Temple, a beautiful landmark.
- what it means: a house of God set low, so all come down to enter, with doors on every side, open to all.
- what a scholar hears: begun around 1588, finished around 1604; the gilding came under Maharaja Ranjit Singh; Mian Mir laying the foundation stone is tradition that historians debate.

**How it's done (info card):** The Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar is the holiest gurdwara for Sikhs; visitors of every faith cover their heads, walk down into the complex, circle the pool, and eat together in its langar, which serves people day and night.

**Sources cited:** Sikh tradition (the building of the Harmandir Sahib; Mian Mir and the foundation stone) (Sikh tradition)

<details><summary>Notes the writers left for a scholar (not shown to learners)</summary>

- Dates: construction begun about 1588 (some say 1589), completed about 1604. Confirm.
- Mian Mir laying the foundation stone: late tradition; many historians doubt it. Framed as 'many Sikhs say' and 'historians debate'. Confirm.
- Gilding under Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the early nineteenth century: confirm.
- Langar numbers ('tens of thousands every day'; often cited as 50,000–100,000): confirm before release.
- Hari etymology: 'Harmandir' is often explained as 'Hari's temple'; confirm.

## Sikhism #4: "Pauri 10: listening, truth and contentment" (day 180, year 1)

- **Today's word:** santokh
- **The promise on the card:** suniai: by listening come truth, contentment and wisdom, worth bathing at every shrine (Japji 10)
- **Line to carry:** enough, by listening
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, pauri nine: by listening, even the bad-mouthed turn to praise. Your line was the lowly speak praise. Did you hear something good from someone unexpected? Day one hundred eighty.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> There's a moment most of us know. You're scrolling through something you might buy, a jacket, a phone, a trip, and you already have one that works perfectly well. You know it. And still there's a little tug: but this one's better. The tug never quite goes away; there's always a next one. Now think of the rare moments when the tug stops. A meal with friends where nobody checks the time. A quiet evening when nothing is missing. Pauri ten has a name for that feeling, and says where it comes from.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Here is pauri ten in plain words, following Macauliffe. By listening come truth, contentment and divine knowledge. By listening, the bath at the sixty-eight places of pilgrimage. By listening, reading and reading, honor is won. By listening, the mind settles easily into meditation. Nanak, the devotees are always in bloom. By listening, pain and wrongdoing are wiped away. The first line is the heart of it: suniai sat santokh giaan. Sat is truth, the same root as sach. Giaan is knowledge, wisdom. And in the middle, today's word: santokh. Contentment. Enough. Not giving up, not pretending not to want anything. It's the settled feeling of having enough, the tug gone quiet. Sikh teaching often counts santokh among the great virtues, alongside truth, compassion, humility and love. Notice where the pauri says it comes from: listening. Not from getting the next thing, and not from grimly denying yourself. From hearing the Name until the restless wanting loosens its grip on its own. Then that second line: the sixty-eight places of pilgrimage. Indian tradition counted sixty-eight great bathing places, and people walked for months to reach them. Remember pauri six, the bath that's only worth it if it pleases Him. Here Guru Nanak says it plainly: listening is the bath at all sixty-eight at once. And the last line before the refrain: sahaj dhiaan. Sahaj is ease, naturalness. The meditation that comes without forcing. Contentment and ease, by listening. You don't have to wrestle the tug down. You listen, and it quiets.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Pick one thing in front of you, or in your life, exactly as it is. Your chair. Your hands. The light in the room. This day, as it's gone so far. Don't improve it in your head. Don't compare it to a better version. Just let it be enough, for the length of the bell. When the tug comes, and it will, notice it, and go back to listening: to your breath, to the room. If you pray, you can rest in Waheguru. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Santokh. Contentment, the settled sense of enough. Japji's tenth pauri says it comes by listening.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: enough, by listening. The next time you feel the tug to buy or add something today, wait one full minute and just listen to what's around you. Day one hundred eighty.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("what's inside the pauri — tap the pairs."):

- sat = truth
- santokh = contentment, enough
- giaan = knowledge, wisdom
- sahaj = ease, naturalness

*True or myth?*

- "santokh means giving up all wanting by force" - **myth**: it's the settled sense of enough, which comes by listening, not by grim denial.
- "the pauri says listening is worth a bath at sixty-eight shrines" - **true**: suniai athsath kaa isnaan.
- "santokh is counted among the great Sikh virtues" - **true**: with truth, compassion, humility and love.

*The fork (what would you do?):* you want contentment, the feeling of enough. where does pauri ten say it comes from?

1. from getting the next thing.
2. from listening. **(right answer)**
3. from a long pilgrimage.

Reveal: suniai sat santokh giaan: by listening come truth, contentment and wisdom, and listening is itself the bath at all sixty-eight shrines.

*In the original language:* ਸੰਤੋਖੁ (say: san-TOKH). Gurmukhi: santokh, contentment. from pauri 10: ਸੁਣਿਐ ਸਤੁ ਸੰਤੋਖੁ ਗਿਆਨੁ.

*The trapdoor:*

- what you thought: being satisfied.
- what it means: contentment, the settled sense of enough, which Japji says comes by listening.
- what a scholar hears: santokh comes from Sanskrit santosha; later in Japji, contentment appears beside dharma in the line about what keeps the earth in its place.

**How it's done (info card):** Sikhs recite the tenth pauri each morning; santokh, contentment, is often taught as one of five virtues, with sat (truth), daya (compassion), nimrata (humility) and pyaar (love).

**Sources cited:** Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3 (Japji, pauri 10) (Guru Granth Sahib); Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), vol. 1, the Japji, pauri 10 (The Sikh Religion)

## Sikhism #5: "Sikh soldiers in the World Wars" (day 300, year 1)

- **Today's word:** Sikh soldiers in the World Wars
- **The promise on the card:** tens of thousands of Sikhs served and many died in both world wars, far from Punjab
- **Line to carry:** far from punjab
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, the SGPC, and your line, peaceful protest worked. Did you speak up once, calmly? Today is day three hundred on this path. Thank you for walking it. And today, Sikhs who walked a very long way from home. Day three hundred.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Autumn, 1914. A young farmer from a village in Punjab steps off a ship in France. He has never seen the sea before this journey. He has never felt cold like this. Within days he's in a muddy trench in Flanders, in a war between European empires he'd barely heard of a year ago, wearing his turban under the gray sky. In his pocket, maybe, a small book of prayers. At home, his mother is waiting for a letter. Today: Sikh soldiers in the World Wars.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> When the First World War began, India was ruled by Britain, and the Indian Army was sent to fight. Sikhs made up a large part of it, far more than their share of India's population. They were among the first Indian troops to reach the Western Front in 1914, holding lines at Ypres and fighting at Neuve Chapelle. Others served at Gallipoli, in Mesopotamia, in East Africa. Tens of thousands of Sikhs served, and many never came home. Many of their letters home survive, because the army censors copied out and translated extracts. They talk about the cold, the guns, friends lost, and again and again about God, and about the village. Some ask that the family keep praying at the gurdwara. Twenty-five years later it happened again. In the Second World War Sikh soldiers fought in North Africa, in Italy, in Malaya, and in the jungles of Burma, where a Sikh soldier named Nand Singh won the Victoria Cross, Britain's highest award for bravery. Why did they go? Historians give many answers. Many were from farming families, and soldiering was steady pay and honored work. Many had a strong military tradition, and a sense of duty. Most were volunteers, though not all went freely. And it's a hard truth that they fought for an empire that did not let their own people rule themselves. For a long time, their part was mostly left out of the story of the wars. That's changing. Today there are memorials to Sikh soldiers in France, in Britain, and in Punjab. They served and died far from Punjab, and they're remembered.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Think of someone who has served: in the military, as a nurse, a firefighter, a caregiver, someone who went far from home to do a hard job for others. Maybe someone in your own family history. Picture them, and picture the people waiting for their letters. Silently, say thank you to them. If they're living and you can, plan to say it out loud. Then rest with the breath. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Sikh soldiers in the World Wars. Tens of thousands of Sikhs who served, and many who died, in both world wars, far from Punjab.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

> Your line: far from punjab. Today, thank one person who has served others far from home, in words or in a message. Day three hundred.

**Games after the talk**

*Match the pairs* ("the wars — tap the pairs."):

- 1914 = Sikhs reach the Western Front
- Neuve Chapelle = a 1915 battle in France
- Nand Singh = won the Victoria Cross in Burma
- the censors = copied the soldiers' letters home

*True or myth?*

## Sikhism #7: "Ashtpadi 20, salok: wandering, I came" (day 514, year 2)

- **Today's word:** Ashtpadi 20
- **The promise on the card:** phirat phirat: wandering and wandering, I have come to Your refuge (Sukhmani 20)
- **Line to carry:** I have come
- **Length:** about 7:00 minutes

**Looking back** (spoken, 20 sec)

> Yesterday, living ashtpadi 19, and your line: live light. Did the give pile from the drawer find a new home? Today a new ashtpadi begins, the twentieth, with a traveler who finally stops. Day five hundred fourteen.

**The hook** (spoken, 50 sec)

> Phirat phirat. Say it out loud and it sounds like footsteps: phirat, phirat, phirat. It's the first word of the twentieth ashtpadi, and it means wandering, turning, going round and round. A car circles a city at night, looking for an address. Left, then left again, back past the same gas station, the phone saying recalculating. And then, finally, the right door, the porch light on, and someone saying come in. Today's salok is that moment of arrival.

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> The salok has two lines. In paraphrase: wandering and wandering, O God, I have come, and fallen at Your sanctuary. This is Nanak's prayer, O God: attach me to Your devotion. Start with the repeated word. Gurbani often doubles a word to make it last: phirat phirat isn't one detour, it's a whole life of them. Some readers hear in it the long round of births and deaths, the soul turning through lifetime after lifetime. Others hear the ordinary wandering of a single life: job to job, want to want, one more thing that was supposed to finally make it feel settled. The text holds both. Then the verb: I have come, aaiaa. Not I have earned my way in, or I have found the answer. Just: I'm here. And then pariaa: I've fallen, or I've laid myself down, at Your saran, Your refuge. It's the posture of someone too tired to keep standing. The last line is the request. Notice what it doesn't ask for. It doesn't ask for the wandering to have been worth it, or for a prize at the end. It asks: attach me to Your bhagti, Your devotion. Keep me here. After nineteen ashtpadis of teaching, of lists and pictures and warnings, the twentieth opens with no argument at all. Just a traveler at the door, and a request to stay.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: stop and rest. Wherever you are, let yourself arrive. Feel the weight of your body settle into the chair or the floor, as if you'd just walked a long way. Let the shoulders drop. With each breath out, you can silently say: I have come. Nothing to fix for two minutes, nowhere else to get to. If thoughts start wandering, notice the footsteps, phirat phirat, and come back. If you pray, you can rest in Waheguru. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

> Ashtpadi 20. Its salok: after long wandering, phirat phirat, the seeker arrives at God's refuge and asks only to be kept in devotion.

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

**The teaching** (spoken, 2 min 15)

> Every full Ardas already asks for this. Near the end comes the line sarbat de kaaraj raas karne: make everyone's tasks succeed. Kaaraj is a task, a piece of work, an undertaking. Raas means set right, brought to a good end. It's a general request, for everybody. Before a journey, an exam, a wedding, a surgery or a new business, Sikhs make it specific: this trip, this test, this first day. Families sometimes offer karah prashad at the gurdwara so the Ardas can be said there, with the congregation joining in. There's a line in the Guru Granth Sahib that fits these moments. In the Ardas, effort and outcome come in an order: the work is done, the result is placed in the hands of the One, and then the traveler goes. There's a practical side, too. Saying a task out loud before starting means naming what you're actually walking into. A short Ardas makes it plain: this is the road, this is what I hope for, this is in Your hands.

**The practice** (spoken, 2 min 30)

> Here's the practice: a pause at the threshold. Think of your very next real task: a call, a drive, a meeting, a page of homework. Picture the doorway you'll cross to get to it, or the screen you'll open. On the in-breath, picture yourself doing the work honestly and well. On the out-breath, let go of how it turns out. Do that a few times. If you pray, you can place it before the One in your own words, or simply say Waheguru. Then, when the sit ends, begin that task. The bell holds the time.

**The word** (spoken, 25 sec)

**The line to carry** (spoken, 15 sec)

Third, the spirit for the road. Sikhs have a phrase for the mood they hope to carry through good years and terrible ones: chardi kala. It means rising spirits, an upward-climbing heart. It isn't pretending things are fine. It's staying turned toward the light when they're not. The Ardas, the standing prayer you met three days ago in a trench, closes by asking that through Nanak the Name may bring rising spirits, and that by God's will all may be well. Chardi kala and sarbat da bhala, side by side. Fourth, the next stretch. The story is about to go through the year of Partition, and it won't be easy to hear. Chardi kala is not a way of skipping the hard chapters. It's how Sikhs have walked through them. That's today's line: a thousand days. Not a finish line. A place to stand for a moment, glad, before the path goes on.
