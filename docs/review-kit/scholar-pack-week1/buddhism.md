# Buddhism: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Buddhism path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: The insult no one took

**Story:** A story from the Pali Canon

**Story source**

- Akkosa Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 7.2: the abusive brahmin Bharadvaja, the guests and the food, “it all belongs to you”; set in the Bamboo Grove, Squirrels’ Sanctuary, Rajagaha
- Nibbedhika Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 6.63: “intention is what I call kamma”
- Upajjhatthana Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.57: “owner of my actions, heir to my actions”
- Samuddaka Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 11.10: as the seed is sown, so is the fruit

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when someone says something unkind to you, what usually happens to it?

- "I say something back": then you’re in good company. this story is for you.
- "I replay it for days": oh, i know that one. listen to this.
- "I let it go, mostly": that’s a real gift. here’s where it comes from.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Near the city of Rajagaha, in a quiet bamboo grove, the Buddha was staying with his monks.
2. A man named Bharadvaja stormed in. Someone from his own clan had just become a monk, and he was furious.
3. He shouted insult after insult at the Buddha. The Buddha listened, calm, and waited.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): When the shouting stops, what does the Buddha do?
>
> A. He asks his monks to send the man away
> B. He stays silent until the man leaves
> C. He asks the man a question about his guests  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He answers anger with a question. It’s about food, and guests.

4. “Do friends and guests ever come to your house?” the Buddha asked. “Sometimes.” “And do you offer them food?” “Sometimes, yes.”

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): “And if your guests don’t eat it,” the Buddha asks, “whose food is it?” What does Bharadvaja say?
>
> A. It belongs to the guests
> B. It’s still ours  **(right answer)**
> C. No one’s, it gets thrown away
>
> **Why:** If no one takes it, it stays with the one who offered it.

5. “In the same way,” said the Buddha, “I don’t accept your insults. So they all belong to you.”

> **Question 3** (why?): Why did the Buddha ask about food instead of answering the insults?
>
> A. To show that an insult only becomes yours if you take it  **(right answer)**
> B. To change the subject and calm things down
> C. To shame the man in front of the monks
>
> **Why:** Bharadvaja’s words were his own action. The Buddha didn’t have to pick them up.


> **Question 4** (finish the line): “I don’t accept your insults, so they ____ to you.”
>
> A. return
> B. belong  **(right answer)**
> C. stick
>
> **Why:** Belong. What you say and do is yours. The Buddha simply didn’t take it.

6. They talked on, and Bharadvaja’s anger cooled. The old text says he soon asked to become a monk himself.

> **Question 5** (who's who): Match each one to its part in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Bharadvaja = shouted the insults
> - the Buddha = didn’t take them
> - the untouched food = a gift that stays with the giver
>
> **Why:** Same insults, two different choices. Each man owned his own.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - in buddhism, karma just means action. and the buddha said the heart of it is intention: what you mean as you act.
> - bharadvaja’s anger was his seed to grow, and the buddha’s calm was his. that’s why many buddhists chant: i am the owner of my actions, heir to my actions.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): He came to be remembered as Akkosaka Bharadvaja. From this story, what do you think Akkosaka means?
>
> A. The one who listens
> B. The one who changed
> C. The one who insults  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Akkosaka comes from akkosa, “insult” or “abuse.” The name kept his old habit, even after he changed.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, if a sharp word comes your way, picture it as a dish set in front of you. you don’t have to pick it up. and before you answer, notice what you mean.

- "I’ll try it today": that’s karma at its root: choosing what you mean.
- "That sounds hard": it is. the buddha’s calm took practice too. one try counts.

**What the learner keeps:** A seed of intention. What you mean is what you grow.

**Please confirm**

1. The sutta says a brahmin of the Bharadvaja clan had gone forth; the commentary makes him his brother. The lesson says “someone from his own clan.”

## Day 2: The monk who bowed to everyone

**Story:** From the Lotus Sutra

**Story source**

- Lotus Sutra, chapter 20, “The Bodhisattva Never Disparaging” (Sadaparibhuta), Kumarajiva’s Chinese version (e.g. Burton Watson’s translation): his words of respect, the sticks and stones, bowing from a distance, the name given by the arrogant, the Buddha’s “that was I myself”
- Saranagamana, the three refuges (Khuddakapatha 1): Buddham saranam gacchami
- Why Buddhists bow: articles in Lion’s Roar and Tricycle

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there someone you find it hard to respect right now?

- "Yes, someone close": that’s heavy to carry. this story is for you.
- "Maybe, a little": most of us have one. listen to this.
- "Not really": lovely. keep this one for the day you do.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, the Lotus Sutra says, there lived a monk who did something unusual. He bowed to everyone he met.
2. To each person he said: “I deeply respect you. I would never look down on you. You will all walk the way and become buddhas.”
3. Not everyone liked it. Some people got angry. “Who is this fool,” they said, “telling us we’ll be buddhas?”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Some of them throw sticks and stones at him. What does he do?
>
> A. He stops bowing to the angry ones
> B. He steps back, and calls out the same words from farther away  **(right answer)**
> C. He sits down and refuses to move
>
> **Why:** He keeps his distance, and keeps his respect.

4. When they threw sticks and stones, he stepped back out of reach, and called out the same words again.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why did he keep bowing to people who were rude to him?
>
> A. He was bowing to the buddha they could become, not to how they acted  **(right answer)**
> B. He hoped they would feel guilty and stop
> C. His teacher had ordered him to
>
> **Why:** The Lotus Sutra is a Mahayana scripture, loved in East Asia. Many there teach that anyone can wake up. He bowed to that.

5. He kept bowing for years. Later, the Buddha told this story and said: that monk was me, in a life long ago.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “You will all walk the way and become ____ .”
>
> A. monks
> B. kings
> C. buddhas  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Buddhas: people who wake up. That’s what he saw in every face.

6. Today, in temples around the world, many Buddhists bow three times: to the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (three jewels): Match each bow to what it’s for.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the Buddha = the one who woke up
> - the Dharma = the teaching
> - the Sangha = the community who walks it
>
> **Why:** Together they’re called the three jewels. Theravada and Mahayana Buddhists alike bow to them. The statue is a reminder.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - when buddhists bow three times, it’s to the three jewels: the buddha, the dharma and the sangha. the statue is a reminder, like a photo of someone you love.
> - and like the monk in the story, many teachers say the bow is to the possibility of waking up, in the buddha and in anyone.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The people who mocked him gave him a nickname, from the words he always said. What do you think it was?
>
> A. Always Patient
> B. The Smiling Monk
> C. Never Disparaging  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Never Disparaging: the one who never looks down on anyone. They meant it as mockery. It became his name.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, when someone tests your patience, try his bow on the inside. quietly think: you could wake up too. and if you’d like to try three real bows, at home or at a temple, that’s yours to choose.

- "I’ll try the inside bow": that’s the bow he made every day.
- "I’d like to try three bows": palms together, three times. the three jewels.

**What the learner keeps:** A lotus from the Lotus Sutra. Bow to what could wake up, in anyone.

**Please confirm**

1. The lesson leaves out the sutra’s ending (he hears the sutra near death, his life is extended, his mockers become his followers).
2. Caption 2 shortens the monk’s words of respect from the sutra. Confirm the paraphrase “I deeply respect you. I would never look down on you. You will all walk the way and become buddhas.”

## Day 3: A bowl full of oil

**Story:** A story the Buddha told

**Story source**

- Janapadakalyani Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 47.20 (at Sedaka, among the Sumbhas): the crowd, the most beautiful girl of the land dancing and singing, the bowl of oil filled to the brim, the man with a drawn sword behind; the bowl as mindfulness
- Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11: right mindfulness as one of the eight factors of the path
- Sati related to Sanskrit smrti, memory; “mindfulness” as T. W. Rhys Davids’ rendering
- ehipassiko, “come and see,” in the recollection of the Dhamma (e.g. Anguttara Nikaya 11.12)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? how often does your phone pull you away from what you’re doing?

- "All the time": same here, honestly. this one’s for us.
- "Now and then": then you know the tug. listen.
- "Hardly ever": that’s rare. here’s an old picture of what you’re doing.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. The Buddha once told his monks a story. A huge crowd gathers to watch the most beautiful girl in the land dance and sing. _(label: a story the Buddha told)_
2. Then a man is handed a bowl of oil, filled to the very brim. He must carry it right between the crowd and the dancer. _(label: a story the Buddha told)_
3. Behind him walks a guard with a drawn sword. If he spills even one drop, it will cost him his life. _(label: a story the Buddha told)_

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Music, a crowd, the famous dancer. What does the man do?
>
> A. He keeps his attention on the bowl, every step  **(right answer)**
> B. He sneaks one quick look at the dancer
> C. He walks as fast as he can to get it over with
>
> **Why:** Every step, the bowl. That’s the whole point of the story.

4. Would he look up at the dancer? No, said the monks. He would keep his mind on the bowl, one step after another. _(label: a story the Buddha told)_

> **Question 2** (why?): Why does the bowl have to be full to the brim?
>
> A. Oil was costly in those days
> B. So the guard can see it clearly
> C. So that every single step needs his attention  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Half full, he could relax. Brim full, he has to keep coming back to it, step after step.

5. “The bowl full of oil,” the Buddha said, “stands for mindfulness of the body.” His word was sati, from an old word for memory.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): Sati is usually translated mindfulness, but it’s closer to ____ to notice.
>
> A. trying
> B. remembering  **(right answer)**
> C. learning
>
> **Why:** Remembering. Not just noticing, but remembering to notice, again and again.

6. Today, many people sit with meditation apps. Much of what they teach grew out of practices like this one. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (what's what): Match each one to its meaning.
>
> Right pairs:
> - sati = remembering to notice
> - the bowl of oil = the Buddha’s picture of mindfulness
> - the eight-part path = where mindfulness belongs, one part of eight
>
> **Why:** In the Buddha’s teaching, mindfulness sits beside kind speech, honest work and wise action.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - so, is meditation buddhist? much of it grew from buddhist practice. but in the buddha’s teaching, mindfulness is one part of an eight-part path, next to how you speak, act and earn a living.
> - and no one has to become a buddhist to sit. the tradition’s own word for its teaching is an invitation: come and see.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Why does “remembering” fit the man with the bowl better than just “paying attention”?
>
> A. He has to memorize the path through the crowd
> B. He keeps bringing his attention back, again and again, with every step  **(right answer)**
> C. He keeps remembering the guard’s warning
>
> **Why:** Attention slips. Sati is noticing that it slipped, and coming back. That’s the practice.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> pick one ordinary thing you’ll carry today: a cup of tea, a bag, the walk to your car. carry it the whole way like the bowl of oil. when your mind wanders, remember, and come back.

- "I’ll try it today": one walk with the bowl. that’s sati.
- "What if I forget?": then you remember again. that’s the practice, not a failure.

**What the learner keeps:** The bowl of oil. Remember to notice, every step.

**Please confirm**

1. The guide’s teaching, the matching question and the today step speak of mindfulness in general; the sutta’s simile is specifically mindfulness directed to the body (the caption says so).
2. The guide says of meditation, “much of it grew from buddhist practice.” Other traditions, Hindu yoga among them, have their own old meditation practices. Confirm the wording is fair.

## Day 4: Five friends in a deer park

**Story:** From the Buddha’s first teaching

**Story source**

- Vinaya, Mahavagga I.6: the journey to the deer park at Isipatana near Benares; the five monks’ agreement not to rise or greet him; one takes his bowl and robe, one prepares a seat, one sets out water for his feet
- Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11: the four noble truths; Kondanna’s vision “whatever is subject to arising is subject to cessation”; “Kondanna has understood,” hence Añña Kondanna
- Visuddhimagga XVI: the truths compared to a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when something hurts, what do you want first?

- "To know why": then you’ll like this teacher. listen.
- "To know how to fix it": then you’ll like him too. listen.
- "Someone to sit with me": i’m here. and this story begins with old friends.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. After he woke up under the Bodhi tree, the Buddha walked a long way to a deer park near Varanasi, to find five old friends.
2. Years before, these five had stayed by his side through his hardest fasting. When he chose to eat again, they walked away from him.
3. Now they saw him coming. “Here comes Gotama, who gave up the striving,” they said. “We won’t stand. We won’t greet him.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): They’ve agreed to snub him. What happens as he walks up?
>
> A. They keep their word and look away
> B. They get up and leave the park
> C. They can’t help it, and rise to welcome him  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Something about him had changed. Their plan fell apart.

4. But as he came close, they couldn’t keep their pact. One took his bowl, one set out a seat, one brought water for his feet.
5. Then he taught them four truths. There is dukkha, the ache in life. It has a cause. It can end. And there is a path.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why isn’t the first truth a gloomy one?
>
> A. Because it says life is mostly happy
> B. Because it names what’s hard for everyone, so it can be treated  **(right answer)**
> C. Because dukkha only comes to people who do wrong
>
> **Why:** It’s a diagnosis, not a verdict. Loss, change, things not fitting: everyone meets them. Naming it is step one.

6. One friend, Kondanna, saw it first: whatever begins, also ends. “Kondanna has understood!” the Buddha said.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): Kondanna saw it: whatever begins, also ____ .
>
> A. ends  **(right answer)**
> B. returns
> C. grows
>
> **Why:** Ends. Hard things too. That’s why the third truth, “it can end,” is good news.


> **Question 4** (like a doctor): Later teachers compared the four truths to a doctor’s visit. Match them.
>
> Right pairs:
> - there is dukkha = the diagnosis
> - craving = the cause
> - the eight-part path = the treatment
>
> **Why:** The cause is craving: a thirst for things to be other than they are. Its ending is called nirvana.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - the four noble truths are the heart of the buddha’s first teaching. there is dukkha. it has a cause, craving. it can end. and there’s a path, with eight parts.
> - and the first truth isn’t a verdict. it doesn’t say life is bad, or that you’re doing it wrong. it says: this is hard, for everyone. then it gets to work.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): After that day, he was called Añña Kondanna. From the story, what do you think Añña means?
>
> A. The one who understood  **(right answer)**
> B. The first friend
> C. The one who stood up
>
> **Why:** Añña: “understood,” or “knowing.” He was remembered as the first to understand.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, when something’s hard, try the doctor’s first two steps. name it plainly: this is dukkha. then ask, gently: what am i clutching at?

- "I’ll try naming it": naming it is the first truth. that’s real practice.
- "What if I can’t find the cause?": that’s okay. the first step already helps. it’s not just you.

**What the learner keeps:** The wheel of the teaching. A diagnosis, not a verdict.

**Please confirm**

1. The lesson leaves out the first part of the first teaching, the middle way between hard self-denial and indulgence, which speaks directly to why the five had left him. Confirm that is acceptable.
2. Caption 5 renders dukkha as “the ache in life,” and question 2 gives “loss, change, things not fitting” as examples. Confirm.
3. Question 4 pairs the truths with a doctor’s visit (diagnosis, cause, treatment), credited to “later teachers.” Confirm.
4. Caption 4 lists three of the five friends’ services (bowl, seat, water for his feet). Confirm.

## Day 5: The prince with a bowl

**Story:** From the Buddha’s life story

**Story source**

- Nidanakatha (introduction to the Jataka commentary): the return to Kapilavatthu, the alms round through the city, Rahula’s mother at the window, the king’s objection, “this is the custom of our lineage,” the king taking the bowl and leading the monks to the palace
- Dhammapada commentary on verses 168–169 (the same episode)
- Vinaya, Pacittiya 37: not eating at the wrong time (after midday)
- The gradual talk (anupubbi-katha), e.g. Vinaya Mahavagga I.7: teaching begins with giving

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is it easier for you to give, or to receive?

- "To give": that’s a kind heart. this story might surprise you.
- "To receive": that’s honest. receiving is part of this story too.
- "Both feel awkward": oh, same. listen to this one.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Years after he left home, the Buddha came back to his father’s city, Kapilavastu, with his monks.
2. The next morning, he walked the streets with his bowl, house to house, quietly, like any monk.
3. From a palace window, Yasodhara, his wife before he left home, saw him and told the king.
4. King Suddhodana hurried out to the street. “Why shame us? No one in our royal family has ever begged.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The king calls it begging. What does the Buddha answer?
>
> A. He says his monks need the food
> B. He says this is the custom of his lineage, the buddhas  **(right answer)**
> C. He says he will stop if it upsets the king
>
> **Why:** His family now is the line of buddhas, and that line lives on what is given.

5. “That royal line is yours,” the Buddha said. “Mine is the line of the buddhas. This is our custom.”

> **Question 2** (why?): In Buddhist teaching, why isn’t the alms round begging?
>
> A. Because it’s an exchange: the monk receives a meal, the giver practices giving  **(right answer)**
> B. Because the monks pay for the food later
> C. Because only rich families are asked
>
> **Why:** Monks don’t ask; people offer. Many Buddhists understand giving as making merit: good karma, and a lighter heart.

6. The king listened. Then he took his son’s bowl himself, and led the monks to the palace to eat.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): The word for what the givers practice is dana. It means ____ .
>
> A. fasting
> B. giving  **(right answer)**
> C. bowing
>
> **Why:** Dana, giving. When the Buddha met new people, the texts say, he often began with giving, before anything else.

7. Today, at dawn in towns in Thailand and Laos, monks walk barefoot with bowls, and people kneel to offer rice. Often the givers bow. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (what's what): Match each one to its meaning.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the bowl = a meal that comes from giving
> - dana = giving, the practice that comes first
> - the line of the buddhas = the family the Buddha now belonged to
>
> **Why:** Traditionally a monk owns almost nothing: robes, a bowl, a razor, a needle. So the bowl is filled only by what people choose to give.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - in buddhism, giving is called dana, and it comes first. the monk lives on what’s offered, and the giver practices an open hand.
> - in the theravada tradition, monks don’t eat after midday, so the morning bowl matters. that’s a rule for monks and nuns, not something everyone keeps.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): At the end, the king carries the bowl himself. What does that show?
>
> A. He wanted the town to see who was in charge
> B. He was still embarrassed and wanted it over
> C. He had come to see giving to the monks as an honor, not a shame  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The same bowl that shamed him that morning, he carried with his own hands.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> give first today: offer something before anyone asks. a meal, a coffee, a full listening ear. and if there’s a buddhist temple near you, many welcome visitors who bring food for the monks.

- "I’ll give first today": that’s dana. the open hand comes first.
- "I’d like to visit a temple": lovely. you can just watch the first time.

**What the learner keeps:** A bowl of rice. Give first. The open hand comes before everything.

**Please confirm**

1. In the Pali sources she is usually “Rahula’s mother”; Yasodhara is the later, widely used name the lesson uses.
2. The lesson mixes Pali and Sanskrit forms across the week: Kapilavastu here (Pali Kapilavatthu), Rajagaha and Kondanna elsewhere, dukkha but nirvana. Tell us if you prefer one form throughout.
3. Question 4’s “why” lists a monk’s things as “robes, a bowl, a razor, a needle.” The traditional list of requisites is a little longer (for example a belt and a water strainer). Confirm.
4. The guide says not eating after midday is a rule “in the theravada tradition.” The rule comes from the Vinaya and is also kept by many monks and nuns in other traditions. Confirm the wording.

## Day 6: Two streams of water

**Story:** As the old texts tell it

**Story source**

- Acchariya-abbhuta Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 123: the mother gives birth standing; the newborn stands, takes seven steps, declares “this is my last birth”; two streams of water from the sky, one cool and one warm
- Nidanakatha (Jataka commentary introduction): the journey toward Devadaha, the Lumbini grove of sal trees, the queen holding a sal branch
- Dona Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 4.36: “remember me as awake”
- Vesak (full moon of Vesakha in Theravada countries); Hanamatsuri, 8 April (Japan); bathing the baby Buddha in East Asian Buddhist practice

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? on your birthday, do you like a big celebration, or a quiet one?

- "Big, with everyone": then you’ll love this festival. listen.
- "Quiet, just a few people": this one has quiet mornings too. listen.
- "I’d rather skip it": fair. this birthday is about more than a birthday.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, a queen named Maya was traveling to her parents’ home when she stopped to rest in a garden called Lumbini.
2. There, standing beneath a flowering sal tree, holding one of its branches, she gave birth to a son.
3. The old texts say the newborn stood, took seven steps, and spoke: this is my last birth. _(label: as the old texts tell it)_

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Today people pour water over statues of the baby. What does the story say happened next?
>
> A. His mother bathed him in a river
> B. Two streams of water fell from the sky to bathe him  **(right answer)**
> C. It rained for seven days
>
> **Why:** One cool stream and one warm, the texts say, falling from the sky.

4. Then two streams of water fell from the sky, one cool and one warm, to bathe him. _(label: as the old texts tell it)_
5. Today, in many East Asian temples, people pour water over a small statue of the baby Buddha, one hand pointing up. _(label: today)_
6. And in Sri Lanka, Thailand and Myanmar, the full moon of Vesak marks three things at once: his birth, his awakening and his death. _(label: today)_

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would a festival for his birth also honor his awakening?
>
> A. Because Buddha is a title: waking up is what made him the Buddha  **(right answer)**
> B. Because no one knew which day he was born
> C. Because they happened in the same year
>
> **Why:** Buddha isn’t a name. It means “awakened one.” The baby became the Buddha years later, under the Bodhi tree.

## Day 7: One lamp lights another

**Story:** From the Questions of King Milinda

**Story source**

- Milindapanha II.2.1: “neither the same nor another”; the lamp burning through the watches of the night
- Milindapanha III.5.5: rebirth without anything passing over, like a lamp lit from a lamp
- Kesamutti (Kalama) Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65: the four assurances, good either way
- Forty-nine-day rites after a death (Tibetan bardo teachings; East Asian memorial practice); offering to monks and sharing merit with the dead (e.g. Tirokudda Sutta, Khuddakapatha 7)

**Opening question from the guide**

**The lesson, in order**

1. Long ago, in the city of Sagala, ruled a king of Greek descent named Milinda. He loved hard questions, and few teachers could answer him.
2. Then he met a monk named Nagasena, and they began to talk.
3. “When someone dies and is reborn,” the king asked, “is it the same person, or someone else?”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Same person, or someone else? What do you think Nagasena says?
>

> B. A completely different person
> C. Neither the same, nor someone else  **(right answer)**

4. “Neither,” said Nagasena. “If a man lights a lamp at dusk, is the flame at midnight the same flame?” “No,” said the king.
5. “Then is it a different lamp?” “No. The same lamp burned all night.” “Just so,” said Nagasena. “Neither the same, nor another.”
6. Another time, he put it this way: light one lamp from another. Nothing moves across. But the new flame came from the old.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why do many Buddhist teachers say “rebirth” rather than “reincarnation”?

> C. outlasts

7. Today, when someone dies, many Buddhist families offer food to monks and dedicate the good of it to the one who has died. _(label: today)_
