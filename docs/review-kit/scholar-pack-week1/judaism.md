# Judaism: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Judaism path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: The pearl and the doorpost

**Story:** From the Jerusalem Talmud

**Story source**

- Jerusalem Talmud, Peah 1:1 (Artaban sends Rabbi a priceless gem or pearl; Rabbi sends a mezuzah; ‘you sent me something I must guard, I sent you something that guards you while you sleep’)
- Proverbs 6:22 (cited by Rabbi in the story: ‘when you lie down it shall watch over you’)
- Deuteronomy 6:4–9 (the Shema; write them on the doorposts of your house, 6:9)
- Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam on the mezuzah’s position (Tosafot, Menachot 33a); Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De’ah 289:6, with the Rema (the slant)
- Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Mezuzah 5:4 (not an amulet)
- Babylonian Talmud, Menachot 33b; Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De’ah 289:2 (the right side, as you enter)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? what’s the most precious thing anyone has ever given you?

- "Something that cost a lot": then this story might surprise you. listen.
- "Something small, but meaningful": then you already know the secret of this one. listen.
- "I can’t pick just one": that’s a lovely problem. keep a few in mind. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, a great king named Artaban owned a pearl so fine that people said no one could put a price on it.
2. He sent it to Rabbi Yehuda, the great sage of the Jewish people, with a message: “Send me something just as precious.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): What do you think the rabbi sends back?
>
> A. A pearl just as fine
> B. A small scroll of holy words  **(right answer)**
> C. A blessing for his kingdom, in the rabbi’s own hand
>
> **Why:** A mezuzah: a tiny handwritten scroll, worth almost nothing in coins.

3. The rabbi sent back a mezuzah: a tiny scroll, written by hand, made to be fixed to the doorpost of a home.
4. Inside are words from the Torah, beginning: “Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one.” The same passage says, write them on your doorposts.
5. The king was offended. “I sent you something priceless, and you send me something worth a coin?”

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would the little scroll feel like an insult to the king?
>
> A. It came without a royal messenger
> B. It was written in a language he didn’t speak
> C. Next to the pearl, it was worth almost nothing  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He was measuring the way kings measure: by price. By that measure, the pearl wins easily.

6. The rabbi answered: “You sent me something I must guard. I sent you something that guards you, even while you sleep.”

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “You sent me something I must guard. I sent you something that ____ you.”
>
> A. guards  **(right answer)**
> B. impresses
> C. pays
>
> **Why:** Guards. A pearl needs a lock and a watchman. The words on the doorpost watch over the home instead.

7. Today, many Jewish homes have a mezuzah on the right doorpost as you walk in, often slanted. Many people touch it as they pass. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (what’s what): Match each one to what it is.
>
> Right pairs:
> - mezuzah = Hebrew for “doorpost”
> - the Shema = the words inside
> - the slant = a meeting point of two teachers
>
> **Why:** One medieval teacher said hang it upright, another said lay it flat. Many Ashkenazi Jews slant it, a little of both. Many Sephardi Jews hang it upright.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the rabbi’s gift was worth almost nothing in coins, and it was the one that kept watch.
> - in jewish tradition the mezuzah isn’t a lucky charm. the great teacher maimonides said so himself. it’s the words, and what they remind you of, every time you cross the door.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The king measured gifts by price. By the rabbi’s measure, which gift was worth more?
>
> A. The pearl, because no one could price it
> B. The scroll, because it looks after the one who keeps it  **(right answer)**
> C. Both the same, since each was given freely
>
> **Why:** The scroll. One treasure you lock away. The other you live with, every time you walk through your door.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, as you leave home, rest your fingers on the doorframe for one second, the way people touch a mezuzah, and carry one good word out with you.

- "I’ll try it today": that little touch is the whole idea. take the words with you.
- "I’ll just picture it": that works too. your door will still be there tomorrow.

**What the learner keeps:** The mezuzah. Words that keep watch on the door.

**Please confirm**

1. Artaban’s gift: the Hebrew margalit is often rendered ‘pearl’, sometimes ‘precious stone’; the lesson says pearl.
2. The king’s complaint paraphrased as ‘worth a coin’ (the Yerushalmi names a small coin, a follar); Rabbi’s reply paraphrased.
3. Artaban described only as ‘a great king’ (usually identified as a Parthian king, Artabanus); parallel in Genesis Rabbah 35:3 to confirm.
4. Caption 4 names only the Shema as the words inside. A mezuzah holds Deuteronomy 6:4–9 and 11:13–21. Confirm the shortening.

## Day 2: Two angels at the door

**Story:** From the Talmud

**Story source**

- Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 119b (two ministering angels, one good and one evil, accompany a person home from synagogue on Friday night; lamp lit, table set, bed made)
- Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 23b (if one can afford only one, the Shabbat lamp comes before the Hanukkah lamp, for peace in the home)
- Exodus 20:8; Deuteronomy 5:12 (remember; observe)
- Mishnah Shabbat 7:2 (kindling among the kinds of work)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? at the end of a long week, what tells you it’s finally over?

- "Nothing, really": then this one is for you. listen.
- "A meal with people I love": then you already know half of this story. listen.
- "Collapsing on the couch": that counts. listen to how one tradition does it.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Friday, just before sunset. In a Jewish home, someone lights the lamps. When the sun goes down, Shabbat, the day of rest, begins.
2. The Talmud tells that on Friday night, two angels walk each person home from the synagogue. One good angel, and one bad.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The angels look inside the home. What do you think they’re checking?
>
> A. How long the person prayed
> B. Whether the lamp is lit and the home is ready  **(right answer)**
> C. Whether there are guests at the table
>
> **Why:** The lamp lit, the table set, the bed made. A home ready for rest.

3. If they find the lamp lit, the table set and the bed made, the good angel says, “May it be like this next Shabbat too.”
4. And the bad angel, against his will, has to answer, “Amen.”

> **Question 2** (why?): Why is it a little funny that the bad angel has to say amen?
>
> A. He’s agreeing to a blessing he’d rather stop  **(right answer)**
> B. Angels always end a sentence with amen
> C. He’s too tired to argue
>
> **Why:** Amen means “so be it.” A peaceful home is so strong that even the one against it has to agree.

5. If the home is dark and nothing is ready, it goes the other way. The bad angel speaks the wish, and the good angel must say amen.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “May it be like this ____ too.”
>
> A. every day
> B. next Shabbat  **(right answer)**
> C. for all your neighbors
>
> **Why:** Next. Whatever the angels find this week, they wish it again for next week. A home tends to become what it repeats.

6. Today, many people light two candles before sunset, often eighteen minutes early. Many then cover their eyes, say a blessing and look up. Shabbat is here. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (what each one means): Match each one to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the lamp, the table, the bed = what the angels look for
> - “amen” = “so be it”
> - hands over the eyes = bless first, then see the light
>
> **Why:** Once the blessing is said, Shabbat has begun, so people light, cover, bless, then look. And two candles, many say, for the Torah’s two words for the rest day: “remember” and “observe.”


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the angels don’t ask how holy you were this week. they look at the lamp, the table, the bed.
> - in jewish tradition the shabbat light is for shalom bayit, peace in the home: so the people you love can see each other’s faces at dinner. customs differ from family to family, and the light is the same.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The rabbis said: if you can afford only one, a Shabbat lamp or a Hanukkah lamp, choose Shabbat. From this lesson, why?
>
> A. It’s the older custom
> B. It’s commanded in the Torah itself
> C. It brings peace to your own home  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The Hanukkah lights are for showing the world. The Shabbat light is for the people at your table. Peace at home comes first.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> this friday, before the sun sets, light a candle on your table, and at dinner really look at the faces across from you. learn the blessing, or just watch the flame. your choice.

- "I’ll light one this friday": light, then rest. that’s the whole thing.
- "I eat alone most nights": then light it for you. the light was always for whoever’s at the table.

**What the learner keeps:** The Shabbat candles. Light, then rest.

**Please confirm**

1. Wording of the angels’ blessing paraphrased (‘may it be so next Shabbat’; the Talmud says ‘another Shabbat’); calling the evil angel ‘bad’ for a light audience.
2. Caption 6 and question 4 describe lighting, covering the eyes, blessing, then looking. That is the common Ashkenazi custom; many Sephardi families say the blessing first and then light. Confirm the wording covers both.

## Day 3: It could always be worse

**Story:** A Yiddish folk tale

**Story source**

- Traditional Jewish/Yiddish folk tale ‘It Could Always Be Worse’ (retold as a picture book by Margot Zemach, 1976)
- Mishnah Avot 2:5 (Hillel: in a place where there are no men, strive to be a man)
- Mishnah Avot 5:7 (the wise person does not interrupt another’s words)
- Max Weinreich, History of the Yiddish Language (origins and components of Yiddish)
- Uriel Weinreich, Modern English-Yiddish Yiddish-English Dictionary (kvetshn, shlepn, mentsh; oy vey iz mir)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when life feels too crowded, what do you do first?

- "Kvetch to someone": good news: you already speak a little yiddish. listen.
- "Keep it to myself": then let this old story keep you company. listen.
- "Try to fix everything": then this rabbi’s advice will surprise you. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In a little village, a poor man lived in one tiny room with his wife, his mother and a houseful of children. It was loud. Very loud. _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_
2. So he went to the rabbi to kvetch, which is Yiddish for complain, at length. “Rabbi, it’s so crowded. What do I do?” _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The rabbi strokes his beard and thinks. What do you think he tells him?
>
> A. Build another room
> B. Bring your chickens into the house  **(right answer)**
> C. Send some children to their aunt
>
> **Why:** Yes, really. Bring the chickens inside.

3. “Do you have chickens?” asked the rabbi. “Bring them inside.” So the man did. Now it was louder, and full of feathers. _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_
4. He schlepped back, dragging his feet, to kvetch again. “Bring in the goat,” said the rabbi. Then, “Bring in the cow.” Oy vey. _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_

> **Question 2** (why?): The rabbi keeps making it worse. What do you think he’s up to?
>
> A. He’s testing whether the man will obey
> B. He doesn’t really understand the problem
> C. He wants the man to see his home differently  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He can’t make the house any bigger. He can change how it feels to come home.

5. At last the rabbi said, “Now take them all out.” Out went the chickens, the goat and the cow. _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_
6. That night the family ate supper in the same little room. It felt so quiet, so roomy. “Rabbi,” said the man, “it’s wonderful.” _(label: a Yiddish folk tale)_

> **Question 3** (finish the line): Every time, the man went to the rabbi to ____ , which is Yiddish for complain, at length.
>
> A. schlep
> B. kvetch  **(right answer)**
> C. kibitz
>
> **Why:** Kvetch. A good kvetch is almost a Yiddish art form. And the rabbi let him do it, every single time.


> **Question 4** (say it in yiddish): Match each one to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - schlep = drag yourself along
> - oy vey = oh, woe is me
> - it could always be worse = said with a shrug and a smile
>
> **Why:** Yiddish was the everyday language of Ashkenazi Jews in central and eastern Europe, built mostly on medieval German, with Hebrew and Slavic words mixed in. English kept a few.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the rabbi never lectured. he listened, every single time, and found a way to make the man laugh at himself.
> - that’s a mensch: yiddish for a real human being, decent and kind. long before yiddish, the sage hillel taught: where no one is acting like a person should, try to be that person.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): By the end, the house is exactly the same size. So what actually changed?
>
> A. The man saw what he already had  **(right answer)**
> B. The rabbi paid to fix it
> C. Some of the family moved out
>
> **Why:** Same room, new eyes. That’s why “it could always be worse” comes with a smile, not a sigh.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> be a mensch in one small way today. an old jewish teaching says the wise don’t interrupt. when someone kvetches to you, listen all the way through, the way the rabbi did, before you offer any advice.

- "I’ll listen today": that’s a mensch. the rabbi would be proud.
- "I’m usually the kvetcher": ha. then thank whoever listens to you. that counts too.

**What the learner keeps:** The little house. Same house. New eyes.

**Please confirm**

1. Folk tale details vary by version (which animals, who lives in the house, a rabbi or a wise man); confirm the lesson’s version is a fair telling.
2. The Hillel teaching in the guide’s line is Mishnah Avot 2:5 (2:6 in some editions). Confirm the paraphrase, “where no one is acting like a person should, try to be that person.”
3. Spellings kvetch, schlep, mensch and oy vey follow common English use rather than YIVO spelling. Confirm.

## Day 4: The bread with no time to rise

**Story:** From the book of Exodus

**Story source**

- Exodus 1:13–14 (bitter lives, with mortar and brick)
- Exodus 12:31–34, 39 (sent out that night; dough taken before it was leavened, kneading bowls bound in their cloaks on their shoulders; baked into unleavened cakes)
- Exodus 13:8 (you shall tell your child on that day)
- Mishnah Pesachim 10:4–5 (the child asks; bitter herbs for the bitterness of slavery; in every generation a person must see himself as if he came out of Egypt)
- Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 116a (charoset in memory of the mortar)
- The Passover Haggadah (the four questions; avadim hayinu, we were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there a family story you’ve heard so many times it feels like your own memory?

- "Yes, more than one": then you’re ready for this night. listen.
- "Not really": then here’s a good one to borrow. listen.
- "I wish there were": then hold that wish. this story was made to be handed on.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In the book of Exodus, the Israelites are slaves in Egypt. Their lives are made bitter with hard work, with mortar and bricks.
2. After ten plagues, Pharaoh finally lets them go. And they leave in a rush, that very night.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): They’re leaving so fast. What happens to the bread they were making?
>
> A. They leave it behind
> B. They take the dough just as it is  **(right answer)**
> C. They stay up to bake it first
>
> **Why:** They take the dough as it is, kneading bowls and all. No time to wait for it to rise.

3. They carry their dough before it can rise, the kneading bowls wrapped in their cloaks, up on their shoulders.
4. On the road, they bake it into flat cakes. There had been no time to wait. That flat bread is matzah.

> **Question 2** (why?): So why do Jews eat flat matzah on Passover instead of bread?
>
> A. To taste the hurry of that night  **(right answer)**
> B. Bread was too costly in Egypt
> C. It keeps longer on a long trip
>
> **Why:** Matzah is the story you can bite: the bread of people who left before it could rise.

5. Every spring, at the Passover seder, Jewish families tell it again. Seder means order, and the plate lays the story out as food. _(label: today)_

> **Question 3** (what’s on the plate): Match each food to what it recalls.
>
> Right pairs:
> - bitter herbs = how bitter slavery was
> - charoset, a sweet paste = the mortar for the bricks
> - parsley in salt water = tears
>
> **Why:** You taste the story before anyone explains it. Plates differ a little from family to family.

6. Then the youngest stands up and asks: “Why is this night different from all other nights?” _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (guess the answer): The answer doesn’t begin “once upon a time.” It begins: “ ____ were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt.”
>
> A. We  **(right answer)**
> B. They
> C. The Israelites
>
> **Why:** We. Not they. An old teaching says that in every generation, each person should see themselves as if they walked out of Egypt.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the first passover wasn’t calm or tidy. it was dough on your shoulder and no time to wait.
> - in jewish tradition, the seder isn’t a history lesson. it’s a memory you’re handed, so each person feels they left egypt too. families tell it in their own ways, with their own questions.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The Torah says, “tell your child on that day.” So why is the telling built around a child’s question?
>
> A. Children get restless otherwise
> B. So the grown-ups can test what children know
> C. A story sticks best when someone asks for it  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The night is built to make the young ask, and the old answer. That’s how a memory gets handed down.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> this week, ask someone older one question about your own family’s story, and let them tell it. that’s the seder’s whole idea: memory is handed down by asking.

- "I know who to ask": then ask. the telling is the gift.
- "There’s no one left to ask": then tell it yourself, to someone younger. that’s how it’s carried.

**What the learner keeps:** A piece of matzah. The bread that had no time to rise.

**Please confirm**

1. Salt water as tears: a widespread explanation, not a Talmudic one; seder plate items vary by community (e.g. romaine or horseradish)
2. Question 2 gives one reason for matzah: the hurry of that night. The Torah also calls it the bread of affliction (Deuteronomy 16:3). Confirm the lesson may give only the first.
3. Caption 6 says “the youngest stands up and asks.” Confirm this fits most families’ custom.

## Day 5: Why the glass breaks

**Story:** From the Talmud

**Story source**

- Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 30b–31a (Mar son of Ravina, at his son’s wedding, sees the rabbis very merry and breaks a costly cup before them; Rav Ashi does the same with a cup of white glass)
- Psalms 137:5–6 (if I forget you, Jerusalem…; Jerusalem above my chief joy)
- Tosafot to Berakhot 31a (the custom of breaking a glass at weddings); Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 560:2, with the Rema (remembering Jerusalem at a wedding)
- Family explanations of the custom (fragility of love, an irreversible promise, warding off bad luck), as families explain it (for example 18Doors, Modern Tribe, Chabad.org)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? on your happiest days, does someone you miss ever come to mind?

- "Yes, always": then this custom was made for you. listen.
- "Sometimes": then you know the feeling this story keeps. listen.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for the day it happens.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. The Talmud tells of a rabbi named Mar, son of Ravina, who made a wedding feast for his son, long ago in Babylon.
2. The guests were great rabbis. And as the night went on, the celebrating got louder, and wilder.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Mar sees the party getting out of hand. What do you think he does?
>
> A. Ends the feast early
> B. Breaks a very costly cup  **(right answer)**
> C. Gives a long, stern speech
>
> **Why:** He brings out a very costly cup, and breaks it right in front of them.

3. So Mar brought out a precious cup, worth a fortune, and held it up for everyone to see.
4. And in front of all his guests, he broke it.
5. The room went still. The guests grew serious. The joy was still there, but now it remembered.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would breaking something help at a wedding?
>
> A. It warned the guests to be careful with the dishes
> B. It showed off how rich the host was
> C. It turned wild joy into joy that remembers  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The shock didn’t end the feast. It reminded everyone that joy and seriousness can sit at the same table.

6. Later teachers tied the custom to Jerusalem and its Temple, destroyed long ago. An old psalm says: keep Jerusalem above your highest joy.
7. Today, at the end of many Jewish weddings, under the wedding canopy, a glass wrapped in cloth is broken underfoot. Then everyone shouts, “Mazel tov!” _(label: today)_

> **Question 3** (finish the line): At the happiest moment, something breaks: even in joy, ____ .
>
> A. remember  **(right answer)**
> B. be careful
> C. celebrate
>
> **Why:** Remember. The crunch comes right before the cheering, never instead of it.


> **Question 4** (what the glass can mean): Families tell it different ways. Match each reading.
>
> Right pairs:
> - remembering Jerusalem = something in the world is still broken
> - a glass that can’t be unbroken = a promise meant to last
> - the loud crunch = an old way to scare off bad luck
>
> **Why:** A Jewish wedding is happy to hold more than one meaning at once.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. nobody cancels the party. the cheering comes right after the crunch.
> - in jewish tradition, even the happiest day keeps a little room for what’s missing. traditionally the groom breaks the glass; at many weddings today either partner does, or both together.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Mar’s cup was worth a fortune. Most weddings today use a simple glass. From the story, does that change the point?
>
> A. Yes, it only works if it hurts to lose
> B. No, the point is the pause, not the price  **(right answer)**
> C. Yes, today it’s only for good luck
>
> **Why:** Mar wanted a moment of seriousness in the middle of the joy. Any glass can give you that.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> next time you celebrate something, even something small, take one quiet second for what’s still broken in the world, or for someone who isn’t there. then cheer.

- "I’ll do that": even in joy, remember. then mazel tov.
- "Someone came to mind already": then they’re part of today too. hold them gently.

**What the learner keeps:** The wedding glass. Even in joy, remember.

**Please confirm**

1. ‘Scare off bad luck’ offered as one folk reading in the match (not a rabbinic one); keep or cut?
2. Caption 3 says Mar’s cup was “worth a fortune” (the Talmud gives four hundred zuz) and leaves out Rav Ashi breaking a white glass cup in the same passage. Confirm.
3. Caption 6 paraphrases Psalm 137:6 as “keep Jerusalem above your highest joy.” Confirm.

## Day 6: The girl who read first

**Story:** From Jewish history

**Story source**

- Judith Kaplan Eisenstein’s bat mitzvah, 18 March 1922, Society for the Advancement of Judaism, New York (Jewish Women’s Archive)
- Mishnah Avot 5:21 (thirteen for the commandments)
- Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 45b (the ages of adulthood: twelve for a girl, thirteen for a boy)
- Genesis Rabbah 63:10 (a father’s responsibility until thirteen)
- Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 39b–40a (visiting the sick)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when did you first feel like a grown-up?

- "On a big day I remember": then you’ll love this one. listen.
- "Slowly, without noticing": that’s how it often goes. this story has both. listen.
- "I’m still waiting": ha. you might be closer than you think. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. New York, 1922. A twelve-year-old named Judith Kaplan wakes up on a Saturday morning, a little nervous.
2. Boys became bar mitzvah, “son of the commandment,” and marked it by reading from the Torah in synagogue. In most synagogues, girls had no ceremony like it.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Judith’s father is the rabbi there. What do you think he has planned?
>
> A. A party just for her
> B. She’ll stand up and read in front of everyone  **(right answer)**
> C. A private blessing at home
>
> **Why:** He calls her up to say the blessings and read, in front of the whole congregation.

3. Her father, Rabbi Mordecai Kaplan, called her up. She said the blessings and read the week’s passage aloud, from a printed book, a little apart from the scroll.

## Day 7: An argument for the sake of heaven

**Story:** From the Talmud

**Story source**

- Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b (three years of dispute; a heavenly voice: these and these are the words of the living God, and the law follows Beit Hillel; because they were kind and humble and taught Beit Shammai’s words before their own)
- Mishnah Avot 5:17 (an argument for the sake of heaven will endure; Hillel and Shammai)
- Mishnah Eduyot 1:5–6 (why minority opinions are recorded)
- Mishnah Yevamot 1:4; Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 14b (the schools did not refrain from marrying into each other’s families)
- Tosafot, Menachot 33a; Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De’ah 289:6, with the Rema (the slanted mezuzah)

> **Why:** An old teaching says an argument for the sake of heaven will last, and its example is Hillel and Shammai.

6. Today, many Jews study in pairs, arguing over a text across a table, out loud. It isn’t a lack of faith. It’s how people learn. _(label: today)_

> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the side that lost wasn’t erased. shammai’s opinions are still written down and studied, two thousand years later.
> - in jewish tradition, a good argument is one for the sake of heaven: to find what’s true, not to beat someone. it’s part of why jews from different movements can disagree and still study the same pages.
