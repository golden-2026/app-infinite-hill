# Sikhism: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Sikhism path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: The lamps of the sky

**Story:** A sakhi, a traditional story of Guru Nanak

**Story source**

- Guru Granth Sahib, Raag Dhanasari, Mahala 1, “Gagan mai thaal” (ang 13, within Kirtan Sohila; also ang 663)
- Janamsakhi tradition of Guru Nanak and Mardana at Jagannath Puri, singing “Gagan mai thaal” at the evening aarti
- Waheguru as a name for God: Bhatt Gayand’s swayyas, Guru Granth Sahib ang 1402–1403

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when did something last make you stop and just say “wow”?

- "This week": then you already know today’s word. listen.
- "It’s been a while": then this story is for you. listen.
- "I can’t remember": that’s okay. this one might help. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, Guru Nanak and his friend Mardana, a Muslim musician, walked to Puri, a temple town by the sea in eastern India. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. At dusk the priests lifted golden trays of lamps, flowers and incense, and waved them in worship. It’s called aarti.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Everyone joins the aarti. What do you think Guru Nanak does?
>
> A. Joins the priests at the front
> B. Stays outside the temple  **(right answer)**
> C. Leaves the town that night
>
> **Why:** He stayed outside. Not to be rude. He had something even bigger to show them.

3. Guru Nanak didn’t join in. The puzzled priests came out and asked him why.

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): The priests ask why. Where do you think he points?
>
> A. At the temple’s golden roof
> B. At the sea
> C. At the sky  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The sky. His whole answer is a song about it.

4. He pointed up and sang. The sky is the tray. The sun and moon are the lamps. The stars are the pearls.

> **Question 3** (why?): Why didn’t Guru Nanak need a tray of lamps?
>
> A. He thought lamps were wasteful
> B. He saw the whole sky already shining in worship  **(right answer)**
> C. He had forgotten his own
>
> **Why:** To him the whole world was already offering its light. Nothing needed adding.

5. Mardana played, and Guru Nanak kept singing: sandalwood on the wind is the incense, the forests are the flowers. The whole world is already doing aarti.

> **Question 4** (finish the line): In his song, the stars are the ____ on the sky’s tray.
>
> A. lamps
> B. flowers
> C. pearls  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Pearls. The sun and moon are the lamps, and the stars are pearls scattered across the tray.

6. Sikhs still sing that song every night. And when something is bigger than words, they say Waheguru: wow, the teacher. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (what’s what): Match each one to its part in Guru Nanak’s song.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the sky = the tray
> - the sun and moon = the lamps
> - the forests = the flowers
>
> **Why:** In his song, the world is already the offering. All you have to do is look up.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. guru nanak didn’t look away from worship. he looked up, and saw the whole sky joining in.
> - that wonder is inside the name sikhs say for god. waheguru: wahe, wow; guru, the teacher. when words run out, wow is the answer.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Sikhs say Waheguru at good news and at hard news. From this story, why might one word fit both?
>
> A. It points past the feeling, to something bigger  **(right answer)**
> B. It’s a polite way to fill a silence
> C. It simply means “thank you”
>
> **Why:** It isn’t a report on your mood. Like the song under the sky, it’s wonder at what is bigger than you.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> tonight, step outside or to a window for one minute and look up. if you like, let one word go out with your breath: waheguru. or simply: wow.

- "I’ll look up tonight": the sun, the moon, the stars. the whole tray. enjoy it.
- "Maybe tomorrow": that’s fine. the sky will still be there, lamps lit.

**What the learner keeps:** The lamps of the sky. The whole sky is already lit. Wow.

**Please confirm**

2. The lesson explains Waheguru as “wahe, wow; guru, the teacher.” Confirm this simple explanation, or suggest a better one.

## Day 2: The true bargain

**Story:** A sakhi, a traditional story of Guru Nanak

**Story source**

- Janamsakhi tradition: the Sacha Sauda (“true bargain”) of the twenty rupees; Gurdwara Sacha Sauda, Chuharkana (now Farooqabad, Pakistan)
- Sikh tradition of the emperor Akbar eating in the langar at Goindwal before meeting Guru Amar Das (“pehle pangat, pichhe sangat”)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? if someone handed you some money and said “spend it well,” what would you do?

- "Save it": careful with it. i like that. listen to what one boy did.
- "Treat someone": then you might like this boy. listen.
- "Spend it on me": honest. listen to what one boy did with his.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. When Guru Nanak was young, his father gave him twenty rupees and sent him to market. Make a good deal, he said. A true bargain. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. On the road, Nanak met a group of holy men who had gone without food for days.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Nanak has twenty rupees and a job to do. What do you think he does?
>
> A. Spends it all on food for them  **(right answer)**
> B. Gives them one rupee and goes on
> C. Takes them home to his father
>
> **Why:** He spent every rupee feeding them.

3. He spent every rupee on flour, lentils and ghee, and fed them all.
4. He came home with empty hands. His father was furious. Where’s the deal?
5. Nanak said feeding the hungry is the truest deal there is. Sikhs remember the place as Sacha Sauda.

> **Question 2** (finish the line): Sacha Sauda means the true ____ .
>
> A. gift
> B. bargain  **(right answer)**
> C. journey
>
> **Why:** Bargain. His father wanted profit. Nanak found a deal that feeds people, and it’s still remembered.


> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): Decades later, an emperor comes to meet the third Guru. Where is he asked to sit first?
>
> A. On a throne beside the Guru
> B. On the floor, in the row with everyone  **(right answer)**
> C. In a private tent
>
> **Why:** On the floor, with everyone, to eat the same meal.

6. Sikh tradition says that when the emperor Akbar came to see the third Guru, he first sat on the floor and ate in the row with everyone. _(label: a traditional story)_

> **Question 4** (why?): Why would an emperor be asked to sit on the floor first?
>
> A. To test his patience
> B. Because there were no chairs that day
> C. Because in the langar, nobody sits higher  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** In the langar everyone sits on the same floor. Not even an emperor sits higher.

7. Today gurdwaras everywhere keep a langar, a free kitchen. Simple vegetarian food, everyone in one row on the floor, the pangat. Any faith, or none. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (what’s what): Match each word to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Sacha Sauda = the true bargain
> - langar = the free kitchen
> - pangat = the row everyone sits in
>
> **Why:** Three words, one idea: feed whoever comes, side by side.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. his father asked for a good deal, and nanak found the best one: hungry people, fed.
> - sikhs carry that into every langar. the food is free, the floor is shared, and nobody sits higher.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Langar food is simple and vegetarian. From what you’ve seen, why might that be?
>
> A. So anyone, of any faith or diet, can eat it  **(right answer)**
> B. Because meat cost too much
> C. So the cooks can work faster
>
> **Why:** So nobody has to ask what’s in it. Any guest can sit down and eat.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> any gurdwara’s langar is open to you, no questions asked. this week, find your nearest one, or share one simple meal with someone you’d never usually sit beside.

- "I’ll find one": shoes off, head covered, sit in the row, hold out your hands. you’ll be fed.
- "I’ll share a meal": that’s the true bargain, right there.

**What the learner keeps:** A handful of langar. Twenty rupees, spent on strangers. The truest bargain.

**Please confirm**

1. Sacha Sauda details vary by telling (twenty rupees; what he bought; Bhai Bala along in some tellings); confirm.
2. Akbar’s visit to Guru Amar Das at Goindwal and sitting in the pangat is tradition; caption says “Sikh tradition says”.
3. The last question gives the reason langar food is vegetarian: so anyone, of any faith or diet, can eat it. Confirm this is a fair reason to give.

## Day 3: A crown you tie yourself

**Story:** A sakhi from Guru Gobind Singh’s childhood

**Story source**

- Sakhi of the child Gobind Rai throwing his gold bangle(s) into the Ganges at Patna, remembered at Gurdwara Kangan Ghat, Patna Sahib
- Guru Gobind Singh, born at Patna, 1666

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? do you wear anything that tells people who you are?

- "Yes, every day": then you know the questions people ask. listen.
- "Once in a while": then you know the feeling. listen.
- "Not really": then this one might surprise you. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago in Patna, by the river Ganges, a little boy played on the riverbank. He would grow up to be the tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. One day he slipped off his gold bangle and tossed it into the river.
3. His mother hurried down to the water. Where did it fall? Show me.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The boy wants to show her where it fell. How do you think he does it?
>
> A. Points at the spot
> B. Throws the other bangle in too  **(right answer)**
> C. Wades in to look for it
>
> **Why:** He threw the second one in. Right there.

4. So he tossed in the other one too. Right there, he said.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why do you think Sikhs still tell this little story?
>
> A. It shows gold didn’t hold him, even as a child  **(right answer)**
> B. It shows he was a careless child
> C. It explains where the gold went
>
> **Why:** Gold didn’t hold him, even at five. Sikhs see in it the Guru he would become.


> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): He grows up to be the tenth Guru. What do you think he gives farmers and barbers alike?
>
> A. Gold bangles, like his own
> B. A turban, worn like a king’s  **(right answer)**
> C. A seat at his court
>
> **Why:** A turban, worn like a crown. Not gold this time: dignity.

5. In his day, fine turbans marked kings and nobles. The Guru asked every Sikh to keep their hair and cover it. A crown for every family.
6. Many Sikhs keep their hair uncut, called kesh, and tie a turban, the dastaar, fresh each morning. On the wrist, a kara: plain steel. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (finish the line): The kara is made of plain ____ , not gold.
>
> A. silver
> B. wood
> C. steel  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Steel. It isn’t jewelry. It’s a circle with no end, like the one God.


> **Question 5** (what’s what): Match each word to what it is.
>
> Right pairs:
> - kesh = hair left uncut
> - dastaar = the turban
> - kara = the steel bracelet
>
> **Why:** Hair left as it grew, a crown tied over it, and plain steel on the wrist.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. a boy who tossed his gold into a river grew up to hand every sikh a crown.
> - that’s the turban: not a hat, not wealth. a crown you tie yourself. and if you’ve cut your hair, you’re every bit as welcome here.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): A boy who threw his gold away later gave every Sikh a crown. So what kind of crown is the turban?
>
> A. A sign of wealth
> B. A dignity anyone can wear, from any family  **(right answer)**
> C. A prize for the best Sikhs
>
> **Why:** Not gold, not rank. A crown you tie yourself, open to every family.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> many sikhs say the kara reminds the hand to do only good. today, before one choice, touch your wrist and ask: is this good?

- "I’ll try it": one touch, one question. that’s the kara.
- "I already wear one": then today, let it ask out loud.

**What the learner keeps:** A crown you tie yourself. Not a hat. Not gold. A crown for every family.

**Please confirm**

1. Bangle sakhi: confirm details (his mother Mata Gujri asking, versus an attendant or a visitor in some tellings; one bangle, then the second).
2. “In his day, fine turbans marked kings and nobles” follows Sikh tradition’s memory; confirm framing.
3. “The Guru asked every Sikh to keep their hair and cover it”: confirm wording (kesh as one of the five Ks; the dastaar in the Rehat).
4. Question 2’s “why” says “even at five.” The caption gives no age. Confirm his age in the story, or remove it.
5. The guide says “if you’ve cut your hair, you’re every bit as welcome here.” Confirm this line is respectful to Sikhs who keep kesh and to those who don’t.

## Day 4: Five who said yes

**Story:** The history and tradition of Vaisakhi, 1699

**Story source**

- Vaisakhi 1699 at Anandpur Sahib: Guru Gobind Singh’s call for a head and the founding of the Khalsa (traditional accounts; Sikh Rehat Maryada on Amrit)
- The Panj Pyare: Bhai Daya Singh (Lahore), Bhai Dharam Singh (Hastinapur, near Delhi), Bhai Himmat Singh (Jagannath Puri), Bhai Mohkam Singh (Dwarka), Bhai Sahib Singh (Bidar)
- The Guru receiving amrit from the five, “aape gur chela” (the Guru is himself the disciple), Bhai Gurdas II, Vaar 41

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you ever been the first to stand up when nobody else would?

- "Yes, once": then you’ll understand daya ram. listen.
- "I wanted to": so many people have. listen.
- "Not yet": that’s okay. this one might help. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Vaisakhi, 1699. A huge crowd gathered at Anandpur for the spring harvest festival. Guru Gobind Singh raised a sword and asked: who will give their head for the faith?

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The crowd goes completely silent. What do you think happens?
>
> A. Everyone stands at once
> B. One man slowly stands up  **(right answer)**
> C. The crowd quietly leaves
>
> **Why:** One man stood. Just one, at first.

2. Silence. Then one man stood: Daya Ram, a shopkeeper from Lahore.
3. The Guru led him into a tent and came out alone. He asked again. What happened inside? The tellings differ.
4. Four more stood, one at a time: a farmer, a water-carrier, a cloth printer and a barber, from far corners of India.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why does it matter who the five were?
>
> A. They came from castes that wouldn’t share a meal, and became one family  **(right answer)**
> B. They were all from one rich family
> C. They were the Guru’s bodyguards
>
> **Why:** A shopkeeper, a farmer, a water-carrier, a printer, a barber. Their castes were kept apart. Now they stood together.


> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): The crowd waits outside the tent. What do you think happens?
>
> A. The five are sent home
> B. The Guru comes out alone again
> C. All five walk out alive, dressed alike  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** All five walked out, alive and dressed alike, beside the Guru.

5. Then all five walked out alive, dressed alike, beside the Guru. Sikhs call them the Panj Pyare: the five beloved.

> **Question 4** (finish the line): Panj Pyare means the five ____ .
>
> A. brave
> B. beloved  **(right answer)**
> C. brothers
>
> **Why:** Beloved. Panj is five, pyare is beloved.

6. They drank amrit, water and sugar stirred with a double-edged sword in one steel bowl. Then the Guru asked the five to give him amrit too.
7. Today, at Vaisakhi parades, five Sikhs dressed alike walk at the front. They stand in for those five. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (who's who): Match each one to its part in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Vaisakhi = the harvest festival
> - Daya Ram = the first to stand
> - Panj Pyare = the five beloved
>
> **Why:** Sikhs remember the five at every Vaisakhi, and in every Ardas.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. five strangers stood up when nobody else would, and walked out as one family.
> - sikhs call that family the khalsa. men take the name singh, lion, and women kaur, princess. names in place of caste.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): After the five, the Guru asked them to give him amrit too. What does that show?
>
> A. The Guru had forgotten the words
> B. Even the Guru was one of the family, not above it  **(right answer)**
> C. The five were now in charge
>
> **Why:** The teacher became the student. Sikh tradition puts it simply: the Guru became the disciple.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> the five came from homes that never shared a meal, and drank from one bowl. today, share food or a drink with someone outside your usual circle, like family.

- "I know who": then that’s your yes for today.
- "I’ll look for someone": good. the five were strangers too.

**What the learner keeps:** The steel bowl. Five strangers, one bowl, one family.

**Please confirm**

1. Occupations vary between tellings (e.g., Mohkam Chand as a cloth printer or a washerman; Dharam Das as a farmer); confirm.
2. “What happened inside? The tellings differ”: kept neutral on purpose; confirm.
3. Caption 1 renders the Guru’s call as “who will give their head for the faith?” Confirm the wording.
4. Caption 6 leaves out the tradition that the Guru’s wife added the sugar (patashas) to the amrit. Confirm leaving it out is fine.

## Day 5: Water for both sides

**Story:** A sakhi of Bhai Ghanaiya, a traditional story

**Story source**

- Sakhi of Bhai Ghanaiya (Kanhaiya) serving water to wounded soldiers of both sides at Anandpur, and Guru Gobind Singh giving him balm (marham); Bhai Ghanaiya is remembered as the founder of the Sevapanthi tradition
- Ardas closing words: “Nanak naam chardi kala, tere bhaane sarbat da bhala” (Sikh Rehat Maryada, text of Ardas)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you ever helped someone you didn’t really like?

- "Yes": then you’ve already started today’s lesson. listen.
- "I tried": trying counts. listen.
- "Not yet": that’s honest. listen to one old man.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In Guru Gobind Singh’s time, during the battles at Anandpur, wounded and thirsty soldiers lay in the dust on both sides. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. An old Sikh named Bhai Ghanaiya walked among them with a leather water bag, giving each one a drink.
3. He gave water to everyone. Sikh soldiers, and the enemy’s soldiers too.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Some Sikhs see him giving water to the enemy. What do you think they do?
>
> A. Thank him for his kindness
> B. Bring him to the Guru, angry  **(right answer)**
> C. Help him carry the water
>
> **Why:** They were angry, and took him straight to the Guru.

4. Some Sikhs were angry. They brought him to Guru Gobind Singh. He’s helping the enemy!

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): What do you think Ghanaiya tells the Guru?
>
> A. He saw no enemy, only the Guru in every face  **(right answer)**
> B. He couldn’t tell who was who
> C. He was only following orders
>
> **Why:** He saw no friend or enemy. Only the Guru’s light in every face.

5. Ghanaiya answered: I don’t see friend or enemy. I see you in every face.
6. The Guru was pleased. He gave Ghanaiya a soothing balm. Keep giving water, he said, and ease their wounds too.

> **Question 3** (why?): Why did the Guru give him a balm instead of stopping him?
>
> A. To keep him out of the fighting
> B. To reward him with a gift
> C. To show care belongs to everyone, friend or enemy  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The Guru didn’t just allow it. He asked for more of it. Care has no sides.

7. Today, Sikhs end their standing prayer, the Ardas, with three words: sarbat da bhala. The good of all. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (finish the line): Sarbat da bhala means the good of ____ .
>
> A. Sikhs
> B. all  **(right answer)**
> C. the family
>
> **Why:** All. Not just Sikhs, not just this room. Everyone.


> **Question 5** (what’s what): Match each one to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Ardas = the standing prayer
> - sarbat da bhala = the good of all
> - Bhai Ghanaiya = water for both sides
>
> **Why:** A prayer and a man who lived it.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. ghanaiya didn’t check sides before he poured. he saw a thirsty person.
> - sikhs carry that in the ardas. after remembering the gurus and the five beloved, the prayer ends with sarbat da bhala, the good of all. as if to say, “not just us.”


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Ghanaiya saw the Guru in every face. If he’s right, who is left out of “the good of all”?
>
> A. Nobody, not even an enemy  **(right answer)**
> B. Anyone who isn’t a Sikh
> C. Only those who fight against you
>
> **Why:** Nobody. If the Guru’s light is in every face, nobody falls outside “all.” That’s why he could pour for both sides.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> if you like, try a tiny piece of it with me: stand for a breath, palms together, and wish one person well, then someone you find hard to wish well. watch, learn, or join in. it’s your choice.

- "I’ll try it": the good of all. that’s the whole prayer, really.
- "I’ll just think about it": that counts too. ghanaiya started with one cup.

**What the learner keeps:** A drop from the water bag. Water for everyone, on both sides.

**Please confirm**

1. Ghanaiya’s reply is paraphrased (“I see you in every face”); tellings vary; confirm wording.
2. Caption 7 says the Ardas ends “with three words: sarbat da bhala.” The closing line is longer (“Nanak naam chardi kala, tere bhaane sarbat da bhala”). Confirm the wording.

## Day 6: Out of the river

**Story:** A sakhi, a traditional story of Guru Nanak

**Story source**

- Janamsakhi tradition: Guru Nanak at the Vein (Bein) river, Sultanpur Lodhi, gone three days, returning with “na ko hindu na musalman” (there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim)
- Sakhi of Guru Nanak at prayer with Nawab Daulat Khan and the qazi (the qazi’s mind on his newborn foal)
- “Sikh”: Punjabi for learner or disciple

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? has anyone ever guessed wrong about who you are?

- "All the time": then you’ll love this one. listen.
- "Sometimes": then you know the feeling. listen.
- "Not really": then listen to what sikhs hear a lot.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Guru Nanak, a young man working in the town of Sultanpur, went to bathe in the river Bein one morning, as he always did. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. He didn’t come out. The town searched the banks for three days.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): On the third day he comes back. What do you think his first words are?
>
> A. I’m sorry I worried you
> B. There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim  **(right answer)**
> C. I saw a vision of heaven
>
> **Why:** His first words: there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim.

3. On the third day he walked out of the water and said: there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim.

> **Question 2** (why?): Sikhs hear a lot in that line. Which fits it best?
>
> A. Beneath the labels, everyone is made by the one God  **(right answer)**
> B. Hindus and Muslims should merge into one new religion
> C. He was giving up faith altogether
>
> **Why:** Many hear: under the labels, everyone is made by the one. Some also hear a challenge: few truly live up to the name they carry.

4. The town’s governor and its judge, the qazi, heard about it. Then come and pray with us, they said.

> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): In the mosque, everyone bows in prayer. What do you think Nanak does?
>
> A. Bows with them
> B. Leaves before the prayer starts
> C. Stands still, and doesn’t bow  **(right answer)**
>

## Day 7: Two swords, one circle

**Story:** A sakhi of Guru Hargobind, a traditional story

**Story source**

- Sikh tradition of Guru Hargobind wearing two swords, miri and piri, at his installation as Guru (1606)
- Meeting of Guru Hargobind and Samarth Ramdas, as recorded in the Pothi Panjah Sakhian; the reply “batan faqiri, zahir amiri, shastar garib ki rakhya, jarwane ki bhakhya” (inwardly a hermit, outwardly a prince; arms to protect the poor and resist the tyrant)
