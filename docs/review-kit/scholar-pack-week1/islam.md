# Islam: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Islam path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: Before every beginning

**Story:** The first revelation

**Story source**

- Sahih al-Bukhari 3 (the beginning of revelation in the cave of Hira: “Read!”, “I am not a reader”, the three embraces, “cover me”, Khadijah’s reassurance)
- Qur’an 96:1–5 (“Read in the name of your Lord who created … who taught by the pen”), paraphrased
- Qur’an 1:1 (bismillah ar-rahman ar-rahim)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 5376; Sahih Muslim 2022 (say bismillah before eating)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when something big is about to start, what do you do first?

- "Take a breath": that’s a beginning before the beginning. hold onto it. listen.
- "Say a little prayer": then you already know part of this story. listen.
- "Just dive in": fair. stay with me, this one starts with a very sudden beginning.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Near Mecca, high on a bare mountain, is a small cave called Hira. Before his calling, Muhammad ﷺ often went there alone to pray.
2. Then, in the cave, the angel Jibril came to him with one word: “Read!”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): He is told, “Read!” He has never learned to read. What do you think he answers?
>
> A. “I am not a reader.”  **(right answer)**
> B. “What should I read?”
> C. Nothing. He runs out of the cave.
>
> **Why:** “I am not a reader,” he said. Three times the command came, and three times he said it.

3. “I am not a reader,” he said. Three times the command came. Then the first words: “Read in the name of your Lord, who created.”

> **Question 2** (finish the line): The very first words began: “Read in the ____ of your Lord.”
>
> A. light
> B. power
> C. name  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** In the name. Before he did anything, the first verse put a name in front of it.

4. He hurried home, shaking, and said to his wife Khadijah, “Cover me, cover me.” She wrapped him up and stayed beside him.
5. “God will never disgrace you,” she told him. “You are good to your family, you carry the weak, you welcome guests.” She stood by him from the very start.

> **Question 3** (why?): Why might the first verse say “read in the name of your Lord,” and not simply “read”?
>
> A. To show reading is only for prayer
> B. So he would not be starting alone  **(right answer)**
> C. To give the book its title
>
> **Why:** Starting in God’s name means the work isn’t done alone. In Muslim teaching, the beginning is carried.

6. Today, many Muslims say “bismillah,” in the name of God, before a meal, a drive, an exam. Nothing starts alone. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (what’s inside the word): Bismillah is three small pieces. Match each one to its meaning.
>
> Right pairs:
> - bi = in, with
> - ism = name
> - Allah = the Arabic word for God
>
> **Why:** Bi, ism, Allah: in the name of God. Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews say Allah for God too.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the very first word was “read,” and right behind it came “in the name of your Lord.”
> - that’s the spirit of bismillah. in islam, you don’t begin alone. even the smallest start, a bite of food, is begun in God’s name.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): He said three times that he couldn’t read, and the command still came. What does that suggest about beginnings?
>
> A. Only people who are ready get called
> B. You can begin before you feel ready, and you don’t begin alone  **(right answer)**
> C. If you repeat something, it comes true
>
> **Why:** He didn’t feel ready, and he began anyway, in God’s name. That’s the heart of bismillah.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> before the next thing you start today, a meal, a message, the car, say “bismillah,” out loud or under your breath. or just give it one full pause.

- "I’ll try it today": lovely. that one small word starts so many beginnings, every single day.
- "I’ll start with the pause": that counts. the pause is where the name goes.

**What the learner keeps:** The first word. Read, in the name of your Lord. Nothing starts alone.

**Please confirm**

1. Khadijah’s words are paraphrased and shortened from Bukhari 3 (she lists more: speaking the truth, helping those in hardship). Confirm the shortening.
2. Linking the first revelation’s “in the name of your Lord” (iqra’ bismi rabbik) to the everyday bismillah is a teaching link (the lesson says “the spirit of bismillah”), not a claim that one comes from the other. Confirm a Keeper is comfortable with it.
3. The last question reads the Prophet’s ﷺ “I am not a reader” as “you can begin before you feel ready.” Confirm this reading is respectful and fair.

## Day 2: What the prayer says

**Story:** A saying of the Prophet ﷺ

**Story source**

- Sahih al-Bukhari 4474 (Abu Sa‘id ibn al-Mu‘alla: called while praying, the promise to teach the greatest surah before leaving the mosque, “al-hamdu lillahi rabbil-‘alamin … the seven oft-repeated”)
- Qur’an 15:87 (the seven oft-repeated)
- Qur’an 1:1–7, paraphrased in plain English
- Sahih Muslim 395 (hadith qudsi: the prayer divided between God and His servant, “My servant has praised Me”)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 756; Sahih Muslim 394 (no prayer without al-Fatiha)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 446 (the Prophet’s mosque: mud brick, a roof of palm leaves, pillars of palm trunks)
- Qur’an 96:1–5 (the first verses revealed; a wrong answer in the last question)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there something you’ve said a hundred times without ever stopping to think what it means?

- "Yes, definitely": then you’ll love this one. listen.
- "A prayer, maybe": then this story is very close to home. listen.
- "Not that I know of": keep listening. you might find one by the end.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In the simple mosque in Medina, roofed with palm branches, a man named Abu Sa‘id was praying when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ called his name.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): He’s in the middle of his prayer, and the Prophet ﷺ calls him. What do you think he does?
>
> A. Stops and answers right away
> B. Finishes his prayer first  **(right answer)**
> C. Pretends not to hear
>
> **Why:** He finished his prayer first, then came over, a little late.

2. He finished his prayer, then came over. The Prophet ﷺ told him, “Before you leave the mosque, I will teach you the greatest surah, the greatest chapter, of the Qur’an.”
3. They walked toward the door together. Just before they reached it, Abu Sa‘id spoke up: “You said you would teach me.”

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): The greatest surah. Which one do you think it is?
>
> A. The longest one in the book
> B. The very last one
> C. The one said in every prayer  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The one said in every prayer: al-Fatiha, the opening.

4. “Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds,” he answered. “It is the seven oft-repeated verses.” Al-Fatiha: the opening.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): Its first line of praise: “Praise be to God, Lord of the ____ .”
>
> A. heavens
> B. worlds  **(right answer)**
> C. people
>
> **Why:** Worlds, all of them. Then, in the middle, it turns to speak to God directly: “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.”

5. In another saying, the Prophet ﷺ said God answers as you pray. When someone says, “Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds,” God says, “My servant has praised Me.”

> **Question 4** (why?): If God answers as you pray it, what changes when you know what its lines mean?
>
> A. You can follow your own side of it  **(right answer)**
> B. Nothing, the sound is enough
> C. It becomes a different prayer
>
> **Why:** The words stay the same. Knowing them means you know what you’re saying, and that an answer comes back.


> **Question 5** (match it up): Match each one to its part in the prayer.
>
> Right pairs:
> - al-Fatiha = the opening
> - the one request = show us the straight path
> - “My servant has praised Me” = God’s answer
>
> **Why:** Seven short verses: a name, praise and mercy, “You alone,” then one request. And God answers as you go.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the greatest surah is one of the shortest. seven lines, and every muslim who prays says it many times a day.
> - in islam, there’s no prayer without it. it’s mostly praise, it asks for one thing, the straight path, and God answers as you pray.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Fatiha comes from a root meaning “to open.” From the lesson, why is that a fitting name?
>
> A. It was the first surah ever revealed
> B. It opens the Qur’an, and it opens every prayer  **(right answer)**
> C. It was taught at a door, so it was named after it
>
> **Why:** It’s the first surah of the Qur’an, and it opens every unit of every prayer. A key in Arabic, miftah, shares the same root.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> pick one line of al-Fatiha and learn what it means: “show us the straight path.” it’s your choice: just read it, listen for it when someone prays, or say it yourself.

- "I’ll read it today": one line, known by heart in meaning. that’s how the whole prayer opens up.
- "I’ll listen for it": beautiful. next time you hear it, you’ll know what’s being asked.

**What the learner keeps:** The open door. Seven lines that open every prayer, and an answer as you pray.

**Please confirm**

1. In Bukhari 4474 the Prophet ﷺ also reminds him of the verse about answering when called (8:24); the lesson leaves that out and only says he came “a little late”. Confirm that’s fair.
2. Muslim 395 lists God’s answers from “Praise be to God” onward (not the bismillah), so the lesson says “God answers as you pray”, not “every line”. Confirm.
3. The verse count follows the common numbering with the bismillah as verse 1.
4. “There’s no prayer without it”: the Hanafi school treats al-Fatiha as wajib rather than a pillar. The guide’s line reports the hadith only.

## Day 3: The water before the words

**Story:** A saying of the Prophet ﷺ

**Story source**

- Sahih al-Bukhari 1149; Sahih Muslim 2458 (Bilal’s footsteps in paradise, asked after the dawn prayer; he prays after every purification)
- Qur’an 5:6 (face, arms to the elbows, head, feet), paraphrased
- Sahih Muslim 244 (the wrongs leave with the water)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6464; Sahih Muslim 783 (the most beloved deeds to God are the most regular, even if few)
- Bilal as the caller to prayer: Sunan Abi Dawud 499 (the dream of the adhan; “teach it to Bilal, for his voice is stronger”)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3754 (Bilal freed by Abu Bakr: “Abu Bakr is our master, and he freed our master”)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? do you have a small thing you do to switch from one part of your day to the next?

- "Yes, I do": then you already get half of this one. listen.
- "I wish I did": then this story might hand you one. listen.
- "Not really": that’s okay. this one is about the smallest habit there is.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In Medina lived a man named Bilal. He had once been enslaved. Freed, he became the one who called people to prayer, his voice carrying over the roofs at dawn.
2. One morning after the dawn prayer, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ turned to him: “Bilal, I heard your footsteps ahead of me in paradise. What have you been doing?”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): What do you think Bilal’s secret was?
>
> A. He fasted every single day
> B. He gave away everything he owned
> C. Something small he did after every washing  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Something small: after every washing, he prayed.

3. Bilal answered, “The deed I hope in most is this: whenever I make wudu, by day or by night, I pray with it.” Wudu is the washing before prayer.

> **Question 2** (finish the line): “Whenever I make wudu, by day or by night, I ____ with it.”
>
> A. rest
> B. pray  **(right answer)**
> C. sing
>
> **Why:** Pray. He never let a washing go to waste. Every wudu led straight into a prayer.

4. The washing itself comes from one verse of the Qur’an: wash the face, and the arms up to the elbows, wipe the head, then the feet.
5. And a saying of the Prophet ﷺ pictures it: as the water runs off the face, the hands and the feet, the wrongs they did run off with it.

> **Question 3** (why?): People often make wudu when their hands are already clean. So why picture wrongs running off with the water?
>
> A. Because wudu washes the heart as well as the hands  **(right answer)**
> B. Because water was scarce in Medina
> C. Because hands are never clean enough
>
> **Why:** Wudu isn’t really about dirt. It lets the day run off, so you step into the prayer new.

6. Today, at a row of taps by a mosque door, or at an ordinary kitchen sink, Muslims still stop for the water before the prayer. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (match it up): Match each one to its part in the lesson.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Bilal = called people to prayer
> - the face = washed first in the verse
> - the arms = washed up to the elbows
>
> **Why:** Face, arms, head, feet: the order comes straight from the verse. Bilal’s voice called the prayer the water leads into.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. bilal’s secret wasn’t grand. he washed, and then he prayed. every time.
> - in islam, wudu is the step between the street and the prayer. the water readies you inside and out, every single time.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Bilal didn’t name a great fast or a great gift. Why might his footsteps still have been heard in paradise?
>
> A. Because he washed more often than anyone
> B. Because his voice was the loudest in Medina
> C. Because a small deed, kept up every time, is beloved to God  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Not a grand deed: a small one, kept up every time. That’s the kind the Prophet ﷺ said God loves most.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> learn the order of wudu from the verse today: face, arms to the elbows, a wipe over the head, then the feet. those are the steps the verse names; a teacher can show you the whole of wudu, which begins with the hands, mouth and nose. it’s your choice: just learn it, watch someone make wudu, or try it yourself, slowly.

- "I’ll learn the order": lovely. face, arms, head, feet. that’s the whole verse in four steps.
- "I’ll try it slowly": beautiful. one slow wash, then a quiet moment. that’s how bilal’s small habit went.

**What the learner keeps:** A drop of wudu water. Wash, then pray. Every time, like Bilal.

**Please confirm**

1. Caption 3 shortens Bukhari 1149 (“I have done no deed I hope in more than that whenever I purify myself, by day or night, I pray with it what was written for me”). Confirm.
2. “Then the feet” is kept neutral on the Sunni and Shia difference (washed or wiped). Confirm.
3. Two pictures show only the hands of ordinary people today, not anyone from the story. Confirm that’s acceptable alongside a caption about the hadith.

_Anything else you would change, in your own words:_

## Day 4: Inshallah or mashallah?

**Story:** A parable in the Qur’an

**Story source**

- Qur’an 18:32–44 (the parable of the two gardens: grapes, date palms, a stream, 18:32–33; “I think not that all this will ever perish”, 18:35; the friend’s question, “ma sha’ Allah, la quwwata illa billah”, 18:39; the fruit destroyed, the vines fallen on their trellises, the owner wringing his hands, 18:42), paraphrased
- Qur’an 18:23–24 (“And say not of anything: Lo! I shall do that tomorrow, except if Allah will”), paraphrased; the exact words in sha’ Allah appear in the same surah at 18:69

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there something good in your life right now that you’d hate to take for granted?

- "Yes, a few things": then keep them in mind. this story is about exactly that.
- "I think I already do": most of us do sometimes. listen to this one.
- "Not sure": that’s honest. the story might help you spot one.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. The Qur’an tells of a man with two gardens, heavy with grapes, ringed with date palms, a stream running between them.
2. He walked through them with a friend and said, “I don’t think this will ever end.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): His friend has a question for him. What do you think he asks?
>
> A. “Will you share the harvest with me?”
> B. “How much did all this cost?”
> C. “Why didn’t you say, ‘what God wills’?”  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He asked why the man hadn’t named where the garden came from.

3. His friend asked: when you came into your garden, why didn’t you say, “Ma sha’ Allah,” what God has willed? There is no strength except in God.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would the friend want him to say “what God has willed” about his own garden?
>
> A. To admit the garden was a gift, not only his doing  **(right answer)**
> B. To make the garden grow faster
> C. Because the garden really belonged to the friend
>
> **Why:** Mashallah says: this good thing is a gift. The man thought it was all his, and that it would last forever.

4. Then the harvest was struck down. The vines fell onto their trellises, and the man wrung his hands over everything he had spent.
5. In the same chapter, the Qur’an says: never say of anything, “I will do it tomorrow,” without adding, “if God wills.” In sha’ Allah.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “I will do it tomorrow, if God ____ .”
>
> A. wills  **(right answer)**
> B. allows
> C. helps
>
> **Why:** Wills. In sha’ Allah: if God wills. A real plan, with honesty that tomorrow isn’t yours to promise.

6. So today: inshallah, if God wills, for plans still ahead. Mashallah, what God has willed, for good that’s already here. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (which is which): Match each one to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - inshallah = if God wills: looking ahead
> - mashallah = what God has willed: already here
> - the two gardens = a gift taken for granted
>
> **Why:** Both phrases come from the same chapter of the Qur’an, the Cave. One looks ahead, one looks at what’s here.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the friend didn’t tell him to stop enjoying his garden. he told him to name where it came from.
> - in islam, that’s the difference. inshallah: make your plan, and hold tomorrow with an open hand. mashallah: see the good that’s here, and call it a gift.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The man also said his garden would never end. Which word was he missing for the future, too?
>
> A. Mashallah
> B. Inshallah  **(right answer)**
> C. Bismillah
>
> **Why:** He talked about tomorrow as if it were his. Inshallah would have held it with an open hand.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, when you talk about a plan, add “if God wills,” even quietly. and when you see something good, say “mashallah,” or just name it as a gift.

- "I’ll try both today": lovely. one for tomorrow, one for today.
- "I’ll start with the gift": that’s a beautiful place to start. good things look different when you name them.

**What the learner keeps:** Grapes from the garden. Plans for tomorrow, thanks for today.

**Please confirm**

1. Caption 3 merges the friend’s words in 18:39 into one line and leaves out the friend’s warning and the man’s boast of wealth and children (18:34, 18:37–41). Confirm the shortening.
2. The pictures show only the gardens, a gate and objects, no people, since commentators differ on who the two men were. Confirm.
3. The last question says the man with the gardens was “missing” inshallah for the future. The Qur’an gives the friend’s rebuke about mashallah; the missing inshallah is the lesson’s own reading. Confirm.
4. Question 4’s “why” says both phrases come from the same chapter, the Cave. In sha’ Allah also appears in other surahs (for example 2:70, 12:99, 37:102, 48:27). Confirm the wording doesn’t suggest it appears only there.

## Day 5: Is doubt okay?

**Story:** A story in the Qur’an

**Story source**

- Qur’an 6:75–79 (Ibrahim and the star, the moon and the sun: “I love not things that set”; “I have turned my face toward Him who created the heavens and the earth”), paraphrased
- Qur’an 2:260 (“Show me how Thou givest life to the dead … Yea, but (I ask) in order that my heart may be at ease”; the four birds on the hills, coming in haste)
- Sahih Muslim 132 (the companions’ troubling thoughts: “that is clear faith”)
- Qur’an 48:4 (faith added to faith)

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you ever had a question about faith you didn’t feel you could ask out loud?

- "Yes, I have": then this one is for you. you’re in good company. listen.
- "I have one right now": thank you for saying so. hold onto it while you listen.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for a friend who might.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. The Qur’an tells how Ibrahim, Abraham, looked up one night and saw a bright star. “This is my Lord,” he said. Then the star set.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The star is gone. What do you think Ibrahim says?
>
> A. “I don’t love things that set.”  **(right answer)**
> B. “I’ll wait for it to come back.”
> C. “The stars must be angry.”
>
> **Why:** “I don’t love things that set.” Whatever disappears can’t be the answer.

2. “I don’t love things that set,” he said. Then the moon rose, shining. “This is my Lord.” But the moon set too.
3. Then the sun came up, bigger than all of them. “This is greater.” And it set. So he turned to the One who made the heavens and the earth.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why does Ibrahim keep looking, instead of stopping at the first star?
>
> A. He was counting the stars
> B. Each answer he tried came to an end, so he kept asking  **(right answer)**
> C. He was waiting for someone to tell him
>
> **Why:** Each one set. He didn’t stop asking until he reached the One who made them all.

4. Later, the Qur’an says, he asked God: “Show me how You give life to the dead.” “Don’t you believe?” “Yes, but so that my heart may be at ease.”

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “Yes, but so that my ____ may be at ease.”
>
> A. mind
> B. soul
> C. heart  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Heart. He wasn’t asking to argue. He was asking to find rest.

5. He wasn’t scolded. He was shown: four birds, set on far hills, came flying back to him in haste when he called.
6. Centuries later, some companions told the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ they had thoughts too troubling to say aloud. “That,” he told them, “is clear faith.” _(label: a saying of the Prophet ﷺ)_

> **Question 4** (match it up): Match each one to its part in the lesson.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the star, the moon, the sun = they all set
> - Ibrahim’s question = show me, so my heart may be at ease
> - “That is clear faith” = the Prophet’s ﷺ answer
>
> **Why:** The star, the moon and the sun all set. Ibrahim asked, and was shown. And the Prophet ﷺ met troubled hearts with reassurance.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. ibrahim asked, and he wasn’t sent away. he was shown.
> - many muslim scholars teach that iman, faith, can grow and dip and grow again. a question you bring to God, looking for rest, has been part of the path since ibrahim.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): The companions were ashamed of their thoughts. Why would the Prophet ﷺ call that “clear faith”?
>
> A. Because being troubled showed how much they cared  **(right answer)**
> B. Because the thoughts themselves were good
> C. Because he wanted them to stop talking about it
>
> **Why:** Not the thought itself: being troubled by it. Their worry showed their faith was alive.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> if there’s a question you’ve been carrying, write it down tonight, or bring it to an imam or someone you trust. and if a night question ever comes with real darkness, please reach out: 988 in the US, or local emergency help.

- "I’ll write mine down": good. a question on paper is a question you can carry, like ibrahim did.
- "I’ll talk to someone": beautiful. the companions did exactly that.

**What the learner keeps:** Ibrahim’s star. Keep asking, so the heart can rest.

**Please confirm**

1. Qur’an 6:75–79: commentators differ on whether Ibrahim was truly searching or arguing with his star-worshipping people step by step. The lesson tells it as the Qur’an does and doesn’t decide. Confirm, since this is the lesson’s main story on doubt.
2. Caption 1 says he looked up “one night” and the lesson moves from the stars (6:76) to the later request (2:260) with “later”. The Qur’an doesn’t give the order in time. Confirm “later”.
3. The birds (2:260): the verse says to take four birds, train them, place a part of them on each hill and call them. The lesson softens this to “four birds, set on far hills”. Confirm.
4. The guide’s second teaching line says “many muslim scholars teach” that faith grows and dips (48:4); Hanafi/Maturidi scholars hold that faith itself doesn’t increase or decrease. Confirm the wording.

## Day 6: One day of Ramadan

**Story:** From the hadith

**Story source**

- Sahih al-Bukhari 1915 (al-Bara’ ibn ‘Azib: the early rule about sleeping before eating; Qays ibn Sirma al-Ansari, the palm grove, falling asleep before his wife returned, fainting at midday; the revelation of Qur’an 2:187)

## Day 7: Two Eids, not one

**Story:** A saying of the Prophet ﷺ

**Story source**
