# My own religion: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the My own religion path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: The farmer’s horse

**Story:** From the Huainanzi

**Story source**

- Huainanzi, chapter 18 (“Renjian xun”, Among Men): the old man near the frontier (sai weng) whose horse runs off to the Hu lands, returns with Hu horses, the son breaks his leg (thigh) riding, a year later the Hu invade, nine of ten men near the frontier die, father and son survive; closing line that fortune and misfortune turn into each other without end.
- English: John S. Major et al., The Huainanzi (Columbia University Press, 2010), chapter 18.
- Proverb 塞翁失马，焉知非福 (“the old man of the frontier lost his horse; how do you know it isn’t a blessing?”) derives from this passage.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? has something happened this year that you still can’t explain?

- "Yes, something big": then hold it gently while we watch. this one is old, and kind.
- "A few small things": keep them in mind. this story has a lot of small turns.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for the day you need it.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Over two thousand years ago, a Chinese book called the Huainanzi told of an old man who lived near the northern frontier, with his son and their horses.
2. One day his horse ran off across the border. The neighbors came to comfort him. What bad luck!

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The neighbors say it’s bad luck. What do you think the old man says?
>
> A. “Yes, it’s a disaster”
> B. “Who knows? This could be good luck”  **(right answer)**
> C. “I’ll go and get it back”
>
> **Why:** “How do you know this won’t turn out to be a blessing?” He doesn’t argue. He just won’t decide yet.

3. Months later, the horse came home, bringing fine horses from beyond the border. What good luck! the neighbors said. Who knows, said the old man, this could be bad luck.

> **Question 2** (why?): Things just got better. Why would the old man still say it could be bad luck?
>
> A. He’s a grump who hates good news
> B. He knows one moment can’t show you how the whole story turns  **(right answer)**
> C. He wanted the horse to stay away
>
> **Why:** He isn’t gloomy. He’s honest. From inside one moment, you can’t see where it leads.

4. His son loved riding the new horses. He fell, and broke his leg, and was left with a limp. What bad luck! Who knows, said the old man.

> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): A year later, an army crosses the border and the young men are called to fight. What happens to the son?
>
> A. He goes and becomes a hero
> B. He runs away into the hills
> C. His injured leg keeps him home, safe  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Nine in ten of the frontier men died. The son, with his limp, stayed home, and lived.

5. A year later, raiders invaded. The young men took up bows to fight, and nine in ten frontier men died. The son, with his limp, stayed home.

> **Question 4** (finish the line): A Chinese saying still used today: “The old man lost his horse. Who knows it isn’t a ____ ?”
>
> A. blessing  **(right answer)**
> B. lesson
> C. mistake
>
> **Why:** Blessing. The saying comes from this very story. People say it when a loss might turn out well.

6. Today, people retell it as the farmer who only ever says “maybe.” It’s an old answer to “everything happens for a reason.” _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (match them): Match each turn of the story to what it led to.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the horse runs away = fine horses come home
> - the fine horses = the son’s fall
> - the son’s fall = he stays home from war
>
> **Why:** Each turn led to the next. From inside one moment, nobody could see where the chain would go.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the old man never says why. he says, who knows? the book, which draws deeply on daoist thought, ends with its lesson: good fortune turns into bad, and bad into good, without end.
> - so you don’t need the reason to make your next move. not why, but what now. that question always has an answer.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): A friend loses a job and says, “everything happens for a reason, right?” What would the old man’s way sound like?
>
> A. “Don’t worry, it’ll turn into good luck. It always does”
> B. “Who knows yet. What’s one thing you want to do next?”  **(right answer)**
> C. “No, it was just bad luck”
>
> **Why:** He never promised the bad would turn good, either. He left the story open, and turned to what comes next.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> when something goes wrong today, even something small, try the old man’s words before you judge it: who knows? then ask, what now?

- "I’ll try it today": lovely. one “who knows” at a time.
- "I’ll save it for something bigger": that counts. the old man waited too.

**What the learner keeps:** The farmer’s horse. Who knows? Then, what now.

**Please confirm**

1. One picture shows “a herd of six wild horses”; the text says he brought back fine horses of the Hu (nomads). The caption says “fine horses from beyond the border.” Confirm the picture is acceptable.
2. The Huainanzi describes the old man as skilled in reading signs; the lesson leaves this out and presents him simply as wise. Confirm.

## Day 2: Tight, loose, tuned

**Story:** From the Buddhist suttas

**Story source**

- Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, Sona Sutta (Sona Kolivisa at Rajagaha, in the Cool Wood): Sona considers returning to lay life; the Buddha appears before him and asks about the lute strings too tight, too loose, and tuned; energy should be balanced. Sona later becomes an arahant. Trans. Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Numerical Discourses of the Buddha (2012).
- Parallel: Vinaya, Mahavagga V.1.
- Breath meditation: Anapanasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 118.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you ever tried meditating and felt like you were bad at it?

- "Yes, I gave up": then this story is for you. a monk almost gave up too.
- "I keep trying": then you’ll love what the buddha tells him.
- "Never tried": perfect. you’ll start with the right idea.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In ancient India, a young Buddhist monk named Sona practiced in a quiet forest grove. He pushed himself harder than anyone, day and night.
2. But his mind would not settle. Alone, he thought: I try harder than everyone, and it isn’t working. Maybe I should go home.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The Buddha comes to Sona. What do you think he does?
>
> A. Tells him to try even harder
> B. Tells him to go home
> C. Asks him a question about music  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** A question about music. Before he became a monk, Sona played the lute.

3. The Buddha came and sat with him. Before you were a monk, he asked, you played the lute, didn’t you? Yes, said Sona.
4. When the strings were too tight, did it sound good? No, said Sona. And when they were too loose? No.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why ask a struggling monk about lute strings?
>
> A. Because effort is like a string: it needs the right tension  **(right answer)**
> B. To cheer him up with memories of home
> C. To hint that he’d be happier back home with his music
>
> **Why:** Sona was straining like an over-tight string. The fix wasn’t to quit. It was better tuning.

5. And neither too tight nor too loose? Beautiful. Effort is the same, said the Buddha. Too much makes you restless. Too little makes you lazy. Keep it balanced.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “Too much effort leads to restlessness. Too little leads to ____ .”
>
> A. sadness
> B. laziness  **(right answer)**
> C. doubt
>
> **Why:** Laziness. The Buddha’s answer to both was balance. Many Buddhist teachers link it to the middle way: not straining, not slacking.

6. Sona tuned his effort, and in time he woke up fully. Today, meditation teachers still use his lute to explain how hard to try. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (match them): Match each string to what it looks like when you meditate.
>
> Right pairs:
> - too tight = forcing your mind to go blank
> - too loose = drifting off and never coming back
> - tuned = wander, notice, come back
>
> **Why:** The noticing isn’t failure. It’s the tuned string. Every time you come back is one more note.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the buddha didn’t tell sona to want less. he told him to tune.
> - in buddhist teaching, you rest your attention on the breath. the mind wanders. you notice, and come back, without forcing. no blank mind required.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): A friend says, “I meditated for one minute and my mind wandered ten times. I’m terrible at this.” What would the Buddha’s lute lesson say?
>
> A. They should quit and try something else
> B. They should squeeze harder until the thoughts stop
> C. Coming back ten times is the practice, gently tuned  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Ten wanders means ten returns. Forcing the thoughts out is the too-tight string.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> try one tuned breath today. breathe in, let it out a little longer. if a thought shows up, notice it and finish the breath.

- "I’ll do it now": that’s one note on a tuned string.
- "Later today": that counts. the string will wait for you.

**What the learner keeps:** Sona’s tuned string. Not too tight, not too loose.

**Please confirm**

1. The guide’s teaching and the today step apply the lute lesson to breath meditation; the sutta is about effort in general (Sona was doing walking meditation). Confirm the bridge is fair.
2. Question 4 matches the strings to meditation (too tight = forcing your mind to go blank; too loose = drifting off; tuned = wander, notice, come back). This is a modern application, not the sutta’s words. Confirm.

## Day 3: The emperor’s notebook

**Story:** From the notebook of Marcus Aurelius

**Story source**

- Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.17, trans. George Long (1862): “Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good.”
- Meditations 6.24: Alexander the Macedonian and his mule driver were brought to the same state by death.
- Book 1 closes “among the Quadi, on the Gran”; Book 2 is headed “at Carnuntum”: written during the Marcomannic Wars on the Danube frontier (c. 170s CE).
- Written in Greek; the Greek title is usually given as “To Himself” (Ta eis heauton).

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there something you keep saving for next month? a call, a trip, a sorry.

- "Yes, more than one": then this one is for you. an emperor had the same habit.
- "Maybe one thing": hold it in mind. you’ll know what to do with it by the end.
- "Not really": good. keep this one anyway. it’s warmer than it sounds.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. About eighteen centuries ago, Marcus Aurelius ruled Rome. He spent many of his last years in cold army camps on the far frontier, by the Danube river.
2. At night, by lamplight, he wrote notes to himself, in Greek. Not speeches. Reminders. Their old title is simply “To Himself.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The most powerful man in the world keeps coming back to one subject in his notes. What is it?
>
> A. His victories in battle
> B. His own death  **(right answer)**
> C. His enemies in Rome
>
> **Why:** His own death. Not to frighten himself. You’ll see where it lands.

3. He reminded himself that he would die, like everyone. Alexander the Great and his mule driver, he wrote, ended up in the same place.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would an emperor remind himself that a great king and a mule driver end up the same?
>
> A. To comfort himself that his enemies would die too
> B. Because power and fame don’t follow anyone past death, so they aren’t what matters most  **(right answer)**
> C. To remind himself that kings are better remembered
>
> **Why:** Death evens everyone out. For a man with every kind of power, that kept his eyes on what he could still do well.

4. He wrote: “Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good.” _(label: an old translation)_

> **Question 3** (finish the line): “While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be ____ .”
>
> A. remembered
> B. brave
> C. good  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Be good. The sentence starts with death and ends on kindness, right now, while you can.

5. His notes survived him, copied by hand for centuries. Today we call them the Meditations, and people still read them. _(label: later)_
6. Today, a skull, an hourglass, two Latin words: memento mori, “remember that you will die.” It’s the same idea, and it’s really about today. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (match them): Match each one to what it is.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Marcus Aurelius = an emperor who wrote to himself
> - memento mori = remember that you will die
> - “be good” = where his thought about death lands
>
> **Why:** Marcus wrote in Greek. The Latin phrase memento mori became famous later, in art and on gravestones.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. marcus was a stoic, and stoics taught that death is natural, nothing to fear, and worth remembering.
> - not because life is grim. because a life you remember is short gets clearer. the good thing you keep postponing comes closer.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Someone gets “memento mori” tattooed and says it means “nothing matters, so who cares.” From Marcus’s note, what would he say?
>
> A. Because time is short, how you act today matters more  **(right answer)**
> B. He’d agree that nothing matters
> C. He’d say forget death and enjoy yourself
>
> **Why:** For Marcus, remembering death pointed straight back at today: while it is in your power, be good.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> pick the one thing you keep saving for next month: the call, the thank-you, the sorry. do the first small step of it today.

- "I’ll do it today": while it is in your power. that’s exactly it.
- "I’ve named it, not today": that counts. you’ve brought it closer already.

**What the learner keeps:** The emperor’s hourglass. While you can, be good.

**Please confirm**

1. One picture shows Marcus helping a soldier lift grain at dawn: an illustration of “be good,” not an event from the sources. Confirm that’s acceptable.
2. Caption 2 says he wrote “at night, by lamplight.” The sources don’t say when he wrote. Confirm or remove.
3. Question 1 says death is the one subject he keeps coming back to. It is a strong theme in the Meditations, among others. Confirm the framing.

_Anything else you would change, in your own words:_

## Day 4: The bow at the river

**Story:** A traditional Hindu story

**Story source**

- Traditional account of Adi Shankara and the chandala at Kashi (Varanasi), told in the hagiographies (e.g. Madhava-Vidyaranya’s Shankara Digvijaya, canto 6) and as the setting of the hymn Manisha Panchakam, attributed to Shankara.
- Manisha Panchakam, opening verse: the question whether Shankara wants the body (made of food) to move from the body, or consciousness from consciousness; the sun reflected the same in the Ganga and in water in an outcaste’s pot; refrain that whoever has this vision is his guru, be he chandala or Brahmin.
- namaste: Sanskrit namas (bow, reverence) + te (to you). Monier-Williams, Sanskrit-English Dictionary, s.v. namas.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? who do you walk past every day without really seeing them?

- "Someone comes to mind": keep their face in mind while we watch.
- "Probably lots of people": same here. this story is about exactly that.
- "I’m not sure": that’s honest. the story might show you.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago in Varanasi, at dawn, the young Hindu teacher Shankara walked down to bathe in the holy river Ganga, his students behind him. _(label: a traditional story)_
2. A man came toward them with four dogs. The harsh caste rules of that time pushed his people to the edges, and told others not to touch them.
3. Move aside, Shankara told him. Get out of the way.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The man doesn’t move. He asks Shankara a question. What do you think he asks?
>
> A. “Why are you so rude to me?”
> B. “What should move, my body or my self? Isn’t the self in us the same?”  **(right answer)**
> C. “Can I come with you to the river?”
>
> **Why:** He takes Shankara’s own teaching and hands it back: if the self in every person is one, who is moving away from whom?

4. Move what? he asked. My body from yours, or the self in me from the self in you? The sun shines the same in the Ganga and my clay pot.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why did the question stop Shankara in his tracks?
>
> A. He wanted to win the argument in front of his students
> B. He realized he had been rude in front of his students
> C. The man’s words were Shankara’s own teaching, and Shankara had just failed to live it  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Shankara taught that the deepest self is one in all. The man caught him not living it, and Shankara knew it.

5. Shankara bowed low before the man. Whoever truly sees this, he said, outcaste or priest, is my teacher. In many tellings, the man was the god Shiva.

> **Question 3** (finish the line): Namaste is two Sanskrit words. Namas means a bow. Te means ____ .
>
> A. to you  **(right answer)**
> B. the light
> C. goodbye
>
> **Why:** To you. A bow to you. “The light in me honors the light in you” is a modern reading, not a translation.

6. Today, across India and Nepal, palms together and namaste is how people greet an aunt, a neighbor, a guest at the door. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (match them): Match each one to its part in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Shankara = the teacher who bowed
> - the stranger = asked the question
> - namaste = a bow to you
>
> **Why:** In this story the great teacher becomes the student. That’s the bow.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. shankara taught that one sacred self lives in every person. so a bow isn’t to someone’s job, caste or mood. it’s to that.
> - and the story shows how easy it is to know this and forget it on a busy morning. namaste, said and meant, is how you remember.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): From this story, when does a namaste really mean what it says?
>
> A. When you say it slowly, with your palms together
> B. When you bow to someone you’d usually walk past, and mean it  **(right answer)**
> C. When you say it to your elders and teachers
>
> **Why:** The gesture is simple. The story’s test is who you bow to, and whether the bow is real.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> today, really greet one person you usually walk past: the cleaner, the driver, the neighbor. a namaste, a nod, a real hello. mean the bow.

- "I know who": lovely. that’s shankara’s lesson, carried out the door.
- "I’ll look for someone": that counts. looking is half of it.

**What the learner keeps:** The bow at the river. A bow to you. Mean it.

**Please confirm**

1. Caste wording: “The harsh caste rules of that time pushed his people to the edges, and told others not to touch them.” The texts call him a chandala. Confirm this is accurate and respectful, and that “outcaste or priest” (for the hymn’s chandala or Brahmin) is right.
2. Please check the Shankara pictures are respectful to both men.
3. The picture of Shankara: a young monk in saffron with a shaved head, bowing with palms together. Confirm this is a respectful depiction.
4. Question 2 says Shankara “had just failed to live” his own teaching. In tellings where the man is Shiva, it is a test from God. Confirm the wording is respectful to those who revere Shankara.
5. The hymn Manisha Panchakam is attributed to Shankara by tradition. Confirm the lesson’s use of it.

## Day 5: Shoulder to the wheel

**Story:** A fable told under Aesop’s name

**Story source**

- “Hercules and the Wagoner”, Perry Index 291; Babrius, Fables 20: the oxdriver’s wagon falls into a ravine; he stands idle and prays to Heracles; Heracles tells him to take hold of the wheels and goad the oxen, and to pray when he is doing something himself, or pray in vain.
- Later English tellings (e.g. George Fyler Townsend, 1867) add the phrase “put your shoulder to the wheel.”
- New Thought movement, 19th-century America; Rhonda Byrne, The Secret (2006).
- Gabriele Oettingen, Rethinking Positive Thinking (2014) and studies on mental contrasting and implementation intentions (WOOP).

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there something you really want this year, that you mostly just picture?

- "Yes, honestly": me too, sometimes. this fable is very old and very kind about it.
- "I’m working on it": then you’ll like what the fable says.
- "Not right now": keep this one for when a wish shows up.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. An old Greek fable, told under the name of Aesop: a farmer was driving his ox cart home along a country road after the rain.
2. The wheel sank deep into a muddy ditch. The oxen strained. The cart would not move.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): The cart is stuck. What do you think the farmer does?
>
> A. Stands there and begs the god Hercules to lift it out  **(right answer)**
> B. Pushes the wheel himself
> C. Leaves the cart and walks home
>
> **Why:** He stands with idle hands and calls for help from above.

3. He stood by the road, hands idle, and begged Hercules, the mighty hero-god he honored most, to come and lift the cart for him.
4. Hercules came, and said: take hold of the wheel, and drive your oxen. Call on the gods when you are working, or you call in vain.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why doesn’t Hercules just lift the cart?
>
> A. He’s too busy
> B. He wants to punish the farmer
> C. Help comes when your own hands are on the wheel  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The fable doesn’t mock the wish. It says the wish works with your effort, not instead of it.


> **Question 3** (finish the line): “Call on the gods when you are ____ , or you call in vain.”
>
> A. resting
> B. working  **(right answer)**
> C. dreaming
>
> **Why:** Working. In later English tellings it became the saying: put your shoulder to the wheel.

5. So he put his shoulder to the wheel, the oxen pulled, and the cart rolled free. _(label: one popular telling)_
6. Today, the vision board. “Manifesting” grew from an American movement of the 1800s. Research finds the dream works better with a look at the mud. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (match them): Match each part of the fable to its part in a real plan.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the wish for help = picture what you want
> - the mud = the wall in the way
> - shoulder to the wheel = your plan to push through
>
> **Why:** The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen found that people who only daydream often try less. Picture the wish, then the wall, then plan.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the fable never says wishing is silly. the farmer is allowed to call for help. he just has to push while he calls.
> - that’s the honest part of manifesting. a thought alone doesn’t pull a cart out of the mud. naming the wish, and the mud, and then pushing, does.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Which of these is closest to what the fable would tell someone manifesting a new job?
>
> A. Put the dream job on your board and picture it every morning until it comes
> B. Picture the job, name the fear of applying, and plan: when it hits, I’ll send one application anyway  **(right answer)**
> C. Skip the picturing and just send a hundred applications today
>
> **Why:** That’s the wish, the mud and the shoulder to the wheel, all in one plan.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> pick one wish for this year. say what’s in the way, honestly. then make one small plan: when that happens, i’ll…

- "I’ve got one": lovely. that’s your shoulder on the wheel.
- "I’ll think it through tonight": that counts. naming the mud is a real step.

**What the learner keeps:** The cartwheel. Wish, then push.

**Please confirm**

1. Oettingen summary (“people who only daydream often try less”): confirm it fairly states her findings.
2. “Manifesting grew from an American movement of the 1800s” (New Thought): confirm this is a fair one-line history.
3. The picture and caption have a muddy ditch after rain (later English tellings); Babrius has a ravine. Minor; noted for you.
4. Caption 3 says Hercules was the hero-god the farmer “honored most.” Confirm this follows Babrius.

_Anything else you would change, in your own words:_

## Day 6: The giant in the fog

**Story:** From the life of Carl Jung

**Story source**

- C. G. Jung, Memories, Dreams, Reflections (1962; English trans. Richard and Clara Winston, 1963), chapter “Student Years”: the dream of walking at night against a mighty wind in dense fog, hands cupped around a tiny light; a gigantic black figure following; on waking he realized it was a “specter of the Brocken,” his own shadow on the swirling mists, cast by the little light, and that the light was his consciousness.
- Jung on the shadow and projection: e.g. Aion (1951), chapter 2, “The Shadow”; Collected Works vol. 9ii.
- The shadow is not wholly bad and holds good qualities too: e.g. Psychology and Religion (1938), Collected Works vol. 11, §134.
- Right after the dream, the memoir reads it through his personalities No. 1 (the bearer of the light) and No. 2 (following like a shadow).

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there someone who gets under your skin more than they probably should?

- "Oh, yes": keep them in mind. no blame, just curiosity.
- "Now and then": that’s most of us. watch what jung noticed.
- "Not really": lucky you. keep this one for when it happens.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In Switzerland, a young man named Carl Jung, who would become a famous psychiatrist, had a dream he never forgot.
2. It was night. He was pushing against a mighty wind through thick fog, his hands cupped around a tiny light that might go out at any moment.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): He feels something coming up behind him. What do you think he sees when he turns?

**Story source**
