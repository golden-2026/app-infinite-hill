# Hinduism: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Hinduism path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: Ganesha comes first

**Story:** A traditional story

**Story source**

- A traditional story told in many versions. The lesson follows the popular telling with a fruit as the prize, common in South India and linked with Murugan (Kartikeya) and the hill shrine at Palani.
- A related version in the Shiva Purana (Rudra Samhita, Kumara Khanda) has the race decide which son marries first; Ganesha circles his parents and wins.
- Pradakshina (circling a sacred place or person, keeping it on the right), behind question 3
- The mantra “om gam ganapataye namah,” offered in the today step

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? ever notice how so many hindu families start everything with ganesha? weddings, new homes, the first day of school.

- "Yes, I’ve noticed": hold onto that. this story is the reason.
- "Not really": then this one’s a nice surprise. listen.
- "I do it myself": then you’ll love where it comes from.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. On Mount Kailash, Shiva and Parvati held up one perfect fruit for their two sons, Ganesha and Kartikeya.
2. “Whoever goes around the whole world first,” they said, “wins it.”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Kartikeya rides a peacock. Ganesha rides a tiny mouse. Who wins the race around the world?
>
> A. Kartikeya, easily
> B. Ganesha, somehow  **(right answer)**
> C. Nobody. It’s a tie.
>
> **Why:** Ganesha. But not by being faster. Watch.

3. Kartikeya leapt onto his peacock and shot off across the sky.
4. Ganesha looked at his ride, a tiny mouse. Then he stood, and slowly walked around his parents, three times.

> **Question 2** (guess his words): “You two are my whole ____ . I’ve been around it.”
>
> A. heart
> B. world  **(right answer)**
> C. family
>
> **Why:** “World.” For Ganesha, going around his parents was going around everything.

5. “You two are my whole world,” he said. “I’ve been around it.” His parents laughed, and gave him the fruit.

> **Question 3** (did you know?): Walking around something sacred, keeping it on your right, has a name in Hindu worship. What is it?
>
> A. Arti
> B. Pradakshina  **(right answer)**
> C. Puja
>
> **Why:** Pradakshina: circling a temple, a shrine or a holy person clockwise, keeping it to your right. Many Hindus still do it at temples today.

6. And so, before weddings, new homes and first days, Hindus begin with Ganesha. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (who's who): Match each one to who they are in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Shiva and Parvati = the parents
> - Kartikeya = the brother on the peacock
> - Mount Kailash = their home
>
> **Why:** Mount Kailash, in the Himalayas, is honored as Shiva’s home.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. ganesha won without running at all. he just knew what his world was.
> - that’s why so many hindus begin with him: before the big plans, remember what matters most.


> **Question 5** (the hard one): Many Hindus say a short prayer to Ganesha before starting anything. After this story, what is it really asking for?
>
> A. Speed, to beat everyone else
> B. To start with what matters most  **(right answer)**
> C. Luck, like a charm
>
> **Why:** Ganesha is honored as the remover of obstacles. Beginning with him is a way of starting in the right place.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> before you start something today, a meeting, a workout, a hard conversation, pause for five seconds and ask: what matters most here? you can say om gam ganapataye namah, if you’d like. it’s your choice.

- "I’ll try it today": five seconds. that’s all ganesha needed.
- "Maybe tomorrow": that’s okay. the question will keep.

## Day 2: The broken tusk

**Story:** A traditional story

**Story source**

- Mahabharata, Adi Parva, opening chapters: Vyasa asks Ganesha to write as he recites; Ganesha’s condition that the pen not stop, and Vyasa’s that Ganesha understand every verse. This episode is found in some versions of the text; the Critical Edition (Pune) places it in an appendix.
- The broken tusk used as a pen: a popular telling (the lesson labels it “one popular telling”). Other stories explain the broken tusk differently, for example a fight with Parashurama.
- Ekadanta, “one tusk,” as a name of Ganesha

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you put something meaningful on hold because things aren’t quite right yet?

- "Yes, right now": then this one is for you. listen.
- "I used to": then you’ll know this feeling. listen.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for when you do.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, the sage Vyasa carried a whole epic in his mind: the Mahabharata, one of the longest poems ever composed.
2. He needed someone to write it down as he spoke. So he asked Ganesha.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Ganesha agrees to write, with one condition. What do you think it is?
>
> A. Vyasa brings him sweets every day
> B. Vyasa can never stop reciting  **(right answer)**
> C. They rest every night
>
> **Why:** Once Vyasa begins, he can’t stop. Not even for a moment.

3. Ganesha agreed, on one condition: once Vyasa began, he must not stop.
4. Vyasa added a condition of his own: Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it.
5. So it began, day and night. Whenever Vyasa needed time to think, he recited a hard verse, and Ganesha paused to understand it.

> **Question 2** (why?): Why would Vyasa sometimes recite an especially hard verse?
>
> A. To show off
> B. To buy himself time to compose the next part  **(right answer)**
> C. To trick Ganesha into stopping
>
> **Why:** Ganesha had to understand each verse before writing it. A hard one gave Vyasa a moment to think.

6. In one popular telling, in the middle of it all, the pen breaks. _(label: one popular telling)_

> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): The pen breaks, and the words can’t stop. What does Ganesha do?
>
> A. Asks Vyasa to wait
> B. Goes to find a new pen
> C. Breaks off his own tusk  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He uses what he has: a piece of himself.

7. Ganesha broke off a piece of his own tusk, and kept writing. _(label: one popular telling)_
8. Verse by verse, the great story was written down, and understood.

> **Question 4** (finish the line): Ganesha had to ____ every verse before he wrote it.
>
> A. memorize
> B. understand  **(right answer)**
> C. repeat
>
> **Why:** Understand. Speed alone wasn’t enough. That was Vyasa’s condition, and it’s the heart of the story.

9. That’s why so many images of Ganesha show one broken tusk, often with the broken piece in his hand. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (who's who): Match each one to their part in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Vyasa = spoke the epic aloud
> - Ganesha = wrote it down
> - the broken tusk = a pen made from himself
>
> **Why:** Vyasa is honored as the sage behind the Mahabharata. Ganesha, the one who wrote it down.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. when the pen broke, ganesha didn’t wait for a better one. he used what he had.
> - and he never wrote a line he didn’t understand. both matter: keep going, and know why.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): One of Ganesha’s names is Ekadanta. From this story, what do you think it means?
>
> A. The one with one tusk  **(right answer)**
> B. The fast writer
> C. The one who never stops
>
> **Why:** Ekadanta: “one tusk.” It’s one of Ganesha’s best-loved names.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> choose one meaningful thing you’ve been delaying. give it five minutes today, with whatever’s in reach.

- "I’ll start today": use what you have. that’s the whole lesson.
- "I’ll think about it": that counts. ganesha paused to understand too.

## Day 3: The robber who became a poet

**Story:** As tradition tells it

**Story source**

- Popular tradition of Valmiki’s early life as the robber Ratnakara, his meeting with the sage Narada, the chant “mara, mara” and the anthill (valmika). This story is not in the Valmiki Ramayana itself; it appears in later tellings, and some (for example the Adhyatma Ramayana) have the seven sages teach him rather than Narada.
- Valmiki as adi kavi, “the first poet,” and the Ramayana as adi kavya, “the first poem”

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? ever feel like your past is too much to start over? a mistake, a reputation, a version of you you’re not proud of.

- "Yes, sometimes": then this story is yours. listen.
- "Someone I love does": keep them in mind while you hear this one.
- "Not really": good. you might meet someone who needs it.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. As tradition tells it, a robber named Ratnakara lived in the forest. He stopped travelers and took everything they had, to feed his family.
2. One day he stopped a wandering sage, Narada, who asked him one quiet question: “Will your family share the weight of what you’re doing?”

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Ratnakara runs home and asks his family: will you share the weight of what I’ve done? What do they say?
>
> A. Yes, we share it
> B. No, it’s yours alone  **(right answer)**
> C. They don’t answer
>
> **Why:** They say no. And that answer changes everything.

3. Ratnakara ran home and asked. And they said no. “You chose this. It’s yours.”

> **Question 2** (why?): Why does that answer shake him so badly?
>
> A. He’ll lose the money
> B. He sees the choice was his alone  **(right answer)**
> C. His family throws him out
>
> **Why:** He’d told himself he did it for them. Now he saw the choice, and its weight, were his. That’s where change starts.

4. He came back shaken. Narada gave him a name of God to repeat, and Ratnakara sat down to repeat it.

> **Question 3** (did you know?): A popular telling says he couldn’t even say the name of Rama at first. So Narada had him say it backwards. What did he repeat?
>
> A. “Mara, mara”  **(right answer)**
> B. “Om, om”
> C. “Hari, hari”
>
> **Why:** “Mara, mara, mara…” Said over and over, it turns into “Rama, Rama.” He started exactly where he was.

5. He sat so still, for so long, that ants built a hill over him.

> **Question 4** (guess the name): When he rose, people called him Valmiki: “the one from the ____ .”
>
> A. forest
> B. anthill  **(right answer)**
> C. river
>
> **Why:** The anthill. The name he’s remembered by comes from the stillness that changed him.

6. When he rose, people called him Valmiki, “the one from the anthill.” And Valmiki went on to write the Ramayana.

> **Question 5** (who's who): Match each name to who they are.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Ratnakara = the robber in the forest
> - Narada = the sage with one question
> - Valmiki = the poet of the Ramayana
>
> **Why:** Same man, two names. The second one is the one the world remembers.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. the man who gave us the ramayana started out as a robber.
> - in hindu tradition, who you were isn’t who you have to be. valmiki is the answer to “people don’t change.”


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Valmiki is honored as the adi kavi. What does that mean?
>
> A. The first poet  **(right answer)**
> B. The holy robber
> C. The silent sage
>
> **Why:** Adi kavi: the first poet. The Ramayana is often called the first poem in Sanskrit.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> think of one thing from your past you’ve let define you. today, do one small thing the new you would do.

- "I know what it is": then do the small thing. that’s how valmiki started too.
- "I need to think": that’s honest. sitting still is how he started.

**Please confirm**

1. Some Valmiki (Balmiki) communities reject the robber story as disrespectful to their revered sage. Please tell us whether and how this story should be told.
2. Which teacher to name: Narada (as in the lesson) or the seven sages, as in some older tellings. Confirm.
3. The last question says the Ramayana is “often called the first poem in Sanskrit.” Vedic hymns are older Sanskrit poetry; the usual claim is the first kavya, or classical poem. Confirm the wording.
4. Caption 3 has his family answer “You chose this. It’s yours.” Confirm this fits the tradition.

## Day 4: No effort is ever lost

**Story:** From the Bhagavad Gita

**Story source**

- Bhagavad Gita 6.37–6.45 (Arjuna asks about the one who has faith but falls away from yoga; Krishna’s answer), with the on-screen labels 6.37, 6.38 and 6.40
- Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva (the Gita’s place in the epic), behind question 1

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? tried something before and quit? the gym, a diet, a meditation app.

- "More than once": same here. this one’s for us. listen.
- "Once or twice": then listen to what krishna says about it.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for later.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, the warrior Arjuna turned to his friend and charioteer, Krishna, with a very human question.

> **Question 1** (think back): The Bhagavad Gita is one small part of a much longer epic. Which one?
>
> A. The Ramayana
> B. The Mahabharata  **(right answer)**
> C. The Vedas
>
> **Why:** The Mahabharata: the same epic Ganesha wrote down with his tusk.

2. “What about someone who starts on the path, really tries, and then falls away?” _(label: Gita 6.37)_
3. “Have they lost everything, like a cloud torn apart in the wind?” _(label: Gita 6.38)_

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): Is the person who tries and falls away lost forever, like a torn cloud?
>
> A. Yes, they lose it all
> B. Only if they never come back
> C. No good effort is ever lost  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Nothing is lost. Listen to how he says it.


> **Question 3** (guess his words): “No one who does good ever comes to a ____ end.”
>
> A. bad  **(right answer)**
> B. sudden
> C. lonely
>
> **Why:** “A bad end.” Listen for it now.

4. Krishna answered. “No one who does good ever comes to a bad end.” _(label: Gita 6.40)_
5. That person, Krishna says, picks up right where they left off. Nothing they did is wasted.

> **Question 4** (think about it): You meditated every day for a month, then stopped for a year. By Krishna’s logic, where do you start?
>
> A. From zero
> B. From where you left off  **(right answer)**
> C. You can’t start again
>
> **Why:** From where you left off. What you did is still there.

6. So if you stopped, you don’t start from zero. You start from where you left off. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (who's who): Match each one to their part in the story.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Arjuna = the warrior with the question
> - Krishna = his charioteer and friend
> - a torn cloud = the fear of being lost
>
> **Why:** A great warrior asking the most human question there is.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. arjuna’s question is so human: what if i try, and fail?
> - krishna’s answer: nothing good you did is wasted. starting again is never starting from zero.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Karma means action, and what it carries forward. How does Krishna’s answer fit that?
>
> A. Only bad actions carry forward
> B. Good effort carries forward too  **(right answer)**
> C. Karma resets every time you quit
>
> **Why:** In the Gita’s view, your good effort carries forward, even past a fall.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> restart one thing you quit. not from the beginning: from where you stopped.

- "I’ll pick it back up": from where you left off. nothing was lost.
- "Not yet": that’s okay. it’s waiting right where you left it.

**Please confirm**

## Day 5: The universe starts over too

**Story:** From the Puranas

**Story source**

- Puranic accounts of creation: Vishnu resting on the serpent Shesha (Ananta) on the cosmic ocean between cycles; a lotus rising from his navel; Brahma seated in the lotus begins creation (for example Bhagavata Purana, Book 3, and Vishnu Purana)
- The cycles of creation and dissolution in the Puranas
- Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva as creator, preserver and the one who ends and renews (the trimurti), behind question 4

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? has something ended that you didn’t want to end? a job, a friendship, a chapter.

- "Yes, recently": then watch this one slowly. it’s for you.
- "A while ago": then you may already see what grew after.
- "Not right now": keep this one for when it does.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Hindu tradition thinks about time in huge circles. Worlds are made, they last for ages, they dissolve, and then they begin again.

> **Question 1** (think about it): Hindu tradition pictures time as huge circles, not a straight line. In a circle, what comes after an ending?
>
> A. Nothing at all
> B. A new beginning  **(right answer)**
> C. The same moment, frozen
>
> **Why:** A beginning. That’s the shape of the whole story you’re about to see.

2. Between the cycles, the stories say, Vishnu rests on a great coiled serpent, floating on a dark ocean. Everything is quiet.

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): Everything is quiet, floating on a dark ocean. What happens next?
>
> A. The world ends forever
> B. Something begins to grow  **(right answer)**
> C. A great flood
>
> **Why:** Something grows. Watch where it comes from.

3. Then a lotus grows from Vishnu’s navel, rising through the dark.

> **Question 3** (guess who's inside): The lotus opens, and inside sits Brahma, the ____ .
>
> A. destroyer
> B. creator  **(right answer)**
> C. protector
>
> **Why:** Brahma, the creator. Now watch him begin.

4. It opens, and inside sits Brahma, the creator.
5. And Brahma begins to make a brand-new world.

> **Question 4** (three gods, three jobs): Many Hindus speak of three great roles in the cycle. Match them.
>
> Right pairs:
> - Brahma = creates
> - Vishnu = preserves
> - Shiva = ends and renews
>
> **Why:** Creation, keeping, and ending so it can begin again. The whole circle.


> **Question 5** (did you know?): Why a lotus? It grows somewhere surprising.
>
> A. In clear mountain water
> B. Up out of the mud at the bottom of a pond  **(right answer)**
> C. In desert sand
>
> **Why:** The lotus rises clean out of muddy water. That’s why it’s a sign of beauty growing out of hard places.

6. The ending was never the end. It was a pause before the next beginning. _(label: today)_

> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what i love. in these stories, even the universe gets a fresh start.
> - whatever ended in your life may be the quiet before something new. feel the ending, then let the lotus open.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): If endings are part of the circle, why do Hindus honor Shiva, the one who ends things?
>
> A. Because endings are bad
> B. Because endings make room for what’s next  **(right answer)**
> C. Because he’s the strongest
>
> **Why:** Shiva ends what has run its course, so something new can begin. Ending is part of creating.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> name one ending in your life. then name one thing it made room for.

- "I can name one": then you’ve seen a lotus open. hold onto it.
- "I’m still in the quiet": that’s okay. the quiet comes first, every time.

**Please confirm**

2. The last question asks why Hindus honor Shiva, “the one who ends things.” For many Shaivas, Shiva is the supreme God who creates, keeps and ends. Confirm the question is respectful, or suggest wording.
3. Question 4 hedges with “many Hindus speak of three great roles.” Confirm the matches (Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva ends and renews).
4. Question 5 explains the lotus as rising clean out of muddy water, a sign of beauty growing out of hard places. Confirm.

## Day 6: The first letters

**Story:** Vidyarambham

**Story source**

- Vidyarambham, the Kerala custom of beginning learning, usually on Vijayadashami at the end of Navaratri: a child, guided by a teacher, priest or elder, traces first letters in a plate of rice
- Saraswati as goddess of learning, music and the arts
- Vijayadashami, “the tenth day of victory”

_(This lesson came without its own source notes, so we have listed the sources the story draws on. Please correct them if needed.)_
