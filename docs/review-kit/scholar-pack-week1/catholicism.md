# Catholicism: week one, for our Keeper

Thank you for looking at this. Infinite Hill is a phone app for a few minutes of religious and spiritual practice each day, across eight paths. Each day is a short story told in pictures and captions, a few questions along the way, a word from the guide, and one small thing to try that day.

These are the seven lessons a newcomer meets in their first week on the Catholicism path. We would be grateful if you would:

- **Confirm or correct the facts:** names, dates, quotes, references and practices.
- **Flag anything disrespectful,** or anything that would feel wrong to people of this tradition, even if it is technically accurate.
- **Suggest better wording** wherever a line could be truer, kinder or clearer.

Each lesson ends with a short "Please confirm" list: the points we most want your eyes on. Please mark each one as fine, or tell us what it should say. You are welcome to write straight into this document, or reply however is easiest for you.

Captions are short on purpose, so a simpler telling is fine as long as it is true to the tradition. A few items mention the pictures that go with a scene; those pictures come with this document.

## Day 1: The smallest prayer

**Story:** A story of Saint Benedict

**Story source**

- Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II (Life of Saint Benedict), chapters 1 and 3: Benedict’s cave at Subiaco; the monks of a nearby monastery (Vicovaro) beg him to be their abbot; he warns them his ways and theirs will not agree; they poison his wine; when the glass is offered to him to bless, as was the custom, he makes the sign of the cross and it breaks as if struck by a stone; he rises calmly, asks God’s mercy on them and returns to his solitude.
- Tertullian, De Corona 3 (c. 200, Carthage): Christians trace the sign on the forehead at every going out and coming in, at dressing, at the table, at lighting the lamps, at lying down and sitting.
- Matthew 28:19 (baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit).
- Catechism of the Catholic Church 1235, 1668 (the sign of the cross and holy water as sacramentals recalling baptism); Western Catholic practice left shoulder then right; Eastern Orthodox right then left.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? when something big or scary happens, what does your body do first?

- "I freeze": that’s so human. this story is about a man who didn’t freeze. listen.
- "I reach for my phone": me too, honestly. this one is about reaching for something smaller.
- "I say a quick prayer": then your hands may already know today’s story. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago in Italy, a young man named Benedict left the noisy city behind to pray alone in a mountain cave.
2. Monks from a nearby monastery begged him to lead them. He warned them his ways were strict. They insisted.
3. They soon resented his strict rule, so they poisoned his wine. At supper, as the custom was, they handed him the cup to bless.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Benedict traces a small cross over the cup, the way he always does. What happens?
>
> A. He drinks and falls ill
> B. The cup shatters  **(right answer)**
> C. He drinks it, unharmed
>
> **Why:** The cup breaks in his hands. Benedict’s first move was the smallest prayer there is.

4. He made the sign of the cross over it, and the cup shattered, as if a stone had struck it.

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): Benedict knows exactly what they tried to do. What does he do?
>
> A. Calmly asks God’s mercy on them and goes back to his cave  **(right answer)**
> B. Has them thrown out of the monastery
> C. Stays and makes the rule even stricter
>
> **Why:** No shouting, no revenge. He blesses them and leaves.

5. He stood up calmly. “God have mercy on you, brothers,” he said. Then he went back to his cave to pray.
6. The habit was already old. Around the year 200, a writer in North Africa described Christians tracing a little cross on their foreheads all day: dressing, eating, lighting a lamp. _(label: history)_

> **Question 3** (why?): Why was a little cross over a cup such an ordinary thing at Benedict’s supper?
>
> A. It was only done when someone feared poison
> B. Christians had long made the sign at everyday moments, like eating  **(right answer)**
> C. It was a rule only for monks
>
> **Why:** For centuries Christians had signed themselves at meals, at the door, at bedtime. Benedict wasn’t doing magic. He was doing what he always did.

7. Today a pitcher does it before the first throw. A grandmother does it when her bus passes a church. Two seconds: head, heart, shoulders. _(label: today)_

> **Question 4** (finish the line): “In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy ____ .”
>
> A. Church
> B. Cross
> C. Spirit  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Spirit. Older Bibles say Holy Ghost. One God: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Catholics call this the Trinity.


> **Question 5** (words and hands): The hand moves head, heart, shoulders while the words are said. Match each place to the words said there.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the forehead = “In the name of the Father”
> - the chest = “and of the Son”
> - across the shoulders = “and of the Holy Spirit”
>
> **Why:** Words and hand keep the same order. The cross is traced over all of you, head, heart and shoulders, with nothing left out.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what the hand is saying. it traces the cross, where jesus died, over your whole self. and the words name who catholics believe God is: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, the words jesus gave for baptism at the end of matthew’s gospel.
> - benedict didn’t panic. he made the smallest prayer and put the moment in God’s hands. western catholics touch the left shoulder first; orthodox christians go right. same sign, two old habits.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): Many Catholics dip their fingers in holy water at the church door, then make the sign. From this lesson, why put those two together?
>
> A. The water cleans the hand for prayer
> B. The same words were said over them, with water, at their baptism  **(right answer)**
> C. It blesses the church building
>
> **Why:** Water, and “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.” That’s baptism. At the door, it’s a small reminder of it.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> at your next meal today, before the first bite, you could make the sign slowly, like benedict over his cup. or just pause and notice head, heart, shoulders. your choice.

- "I’ll try it at my next meal": two seconds, and nothing left out. that’s the whole prayer.
- "I’ll just watch for it": that counts. once you start looking, you’ll see it everywhere.

**What the learner keeps:** The sign of the cross. Head, heart, shoulders. All of you.

**Please confirm**

1. Tertullian’s De Corona is usually dated about 211; the caption says “around the year 200.” Confirm.
2. Question 1’s “why” says the cup breaks “in his hands.” As Gregory tells it, Benedict blessed it with the sign of the cross and the glass, held some way off, broke. Confirm the wording.
3. The match question pairs forehead / chest / shoulders with Father / Son / Holy Spirit. Confirm this is the usual way it is taught.

## Day 2: Stay with us

**Story:** From Luke’s Gospel

**Story source**

- Luke 24:13-35 (the road to Emmaus: two disciples, one named Cleopas, walk from Jerusalem; the risen Jesus joins them unrecognized; he opens the scriptures; “Stay with us, because it is towards evening”; at table he takes bread, blesses, breaks and gives it; their eyes are opened; he vanishes; “Was not our heart burning within us?”; they return to Jerusalem that same hour; he was known to them in the breaking of bread).
- Luke 22:19 (at the Last Supper: took bread, gave thanks, broke it: “This is my body, which is given for you”).
- Eucharist from Greek eucharistia, thanksgiving.
- Catechism of the Catholic Church 1373-1381 (the real presence; transubstantiation; the tabernacle), 1418 (genuflection), and the sanctuary lamp (General Instruction of the Roman Missal 316; Code of Canon Law 940).
- Comparative note: Orthodox and Lutheran churches also confess a real presence; many other Protestant traditions understand the Lord’s Supper as a memorial and sign.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? has someone ever just stayed with you when things were bad, without trying to fix anything?

- "Yes, I remember it": hold onto that face. today’s story is about someone who stayed.
- "I wish someone had": i’m sorry. i hope this story feels like company. listen.
- "I’ve been that person": then you already know what this story is about. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. On the third day after Jesus died, two of his followers walked away from Jerusalem, toward a village called Emmaus. Their hope was gone.
2. A stranger fell into step beside them and asked what they were talking about. They stopped, their faces sad.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Who do you think the stranger is?
>
> A. A Roman soldier keeping watch
> B. Jesus, risen, and they don’t know him  **(right answer)**
> C. Another grieving follower
>
> **Why:** It’s Jesus. They’ve walked miles with him and haven’t recognized him.

3. It was Jesus, risen, but they didn’t know him. Mile after mile, he opened the scriptures to them.
4. At the village he made as if to walk on. They begged him: “Stay with us, for it is toward evening.” So he stayed.

> **Question 2** (finish the line): “Stay with us, for it is toward ____ .”
>
> A. evening  **(right answer)**
> B. the Sabbath
> C. Jerusalem
>
> **Why:** Evening. The day was nearly gone, and so was their hope. They didn’t want to be alone with it.


> **Question 3** (guess before the story continues): At supper, the guest does something, and suddenly they know him. What is it?
>
> A. He shows them his wounds
> B. He calls them each by name
> C. He takes the bread, blesses it and breaks it  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** The bread. Four small actions: he took it, blessed it, broke it, gave it.

5. At the table he took the bread, blessed it, broke it and gave it to them. And their eyes were opened.
6. They knew him, and he vanished from their sight. “Weren’t our hearts burning as he talked with us?” They hurried back to Jerusalem that same hour.

> **Question 4** (why?): Luke tells this moment with the same four actions he used for Jesus’s last supper. Why does that matter?
>
> A. It shows this was just an ordinary meal
> B. It ties this table to the meal where he said, “This is my body”  **(right answer)**
> C. It proves these two had been at the Last Supper
>
> **Why:** At his last supper he took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said, “This is my body.” Gave thanks, in Greek, is eucharistia. That’s where the word Eucharist comes from.

7. Today, in Catholic churches, a lamp, usually red, burns beside the tabernacle, the small box where the blessed bread is kept. Catholics believe he is there. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (what’s what): Match each one to what it means.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the Eucharist = thanksgiving: the bread at the heart of Mass
> - the tabernacle = where the blessed bread is kept
> - the red lamp = a sign that he is present
>
> **Why:** That’s why many Catholics bend a knee when they walk into an empty church. They believe he’s there.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s what catholics believe. at every mass, when the priest says jesus’s words, “this is my body,” the bread and wine truly become his body and blood. they still look and taste like bread and wine. underneath, it’s him. the church calls it the real presence.
> - christians differ here. orthodox christians and lutherans also speak of a real presence; many other protestants see the meal as a memorial. for catholics, emmaus is the promise: he stayed.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): They walked for hours beside him and didn’t know him, then knew him at the table. What were Luke’s first readers, who never met Jesus, meant to take from that?
>
> A. He can still be known in the breaking of the bread  **(right answer)**
> B. Only people who met him in person could know him
> C. Strangers are never who they seem
>
> **Why:** Luke ends the story with them telling how they knew him “in the breaking of the bread.” For Catholics, that happens at every Mass.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> if you pass a catholic church this week, you could step in for one minute. find the red lamp, and just sit. you don’t have to pray. you can just notice who catholics believe stayed.

- "I’ll look for one": one minute is plenty. the lamp will be on.
- "Not this week, but I’d like to": that counts. the door will still be open.

**What the learner keeps:** The bread broken at Emmaus. They knew him in the breaking of the bread.

**Please confirm**

1. The fill-in line follows the RSV (“Stay with us, for it is toward evening”); Douay-Rheims reads “because it is towards evening.” Confirm the preferred translation.
2. Comparative line on Orthodox, Lutheran and other Protestant views of the Eucharist is simplified; confirm wording.
3. Question 4 says Luke uses “the same four actions” at Emmaus as at the Last Supper. At the Last Supper Luke says “gave thanks,” at Emmaus “blessed.” Confirm “the same four actions” is fair.

## Day 3: The priest who listened

**Story:** A story of Saint John Vianney

**Story source**

- Saint Jean-Marie Vianney (1786-1859), the Curé of Ars: struggled with his Latin studies before ordination (1815); sent in 1818 to Ars-sur-Formans, a small farming village near Lyon, where he served until his death in 1859; in his later decades pilgrims came from across France and beyond, and he spent most of each day hearing confessions. Canonized by Pius XI in 1925; named patron of parish priests in 1929.
- Pope Benedict XVI, Letter Proclaiming a Year for Priests (16 June 2009), on the Curé of Ars: the vicar general’s words, “There is little love of God in that parish; you will be the one to put it there”; his long hours in the confessional; his “recipe”: a small penance for the penitent, “the remainder I do in their place.”
- Abbé Francis Trochu, The Curé d’Ars (1925): the arrival in February 1818; the young shepherd Antoine Givre who showed him the way (“You have shown me the way to Ars; I will show you the way to heaven”), now marked by a monument outside the village; the crowds and the hours in the confessional.
- John 20:22-23 (Easter night: “Receive ye the Holy Ghost. Whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them”).
- Catechism of the Catholic Church 1424 (names of the sacrament), 1442, 1450-1460 (confession, penance/satisfaction), 1449 (absolution formula), 1467 (the sacramental seal); Code of Canon Law 983.
- Comparative note: Orthodox Christians also confess to a priest; many Protestants confess directly to God, and some (e.g. Lutherans, Anglicans) keep private confession as an option.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? is there something you’ve carried for a long time and never said out loud?

- "Yes, one thing": thank you for being honest. you don’t have to say it here. just listen.
- "A few things": most of us have a few. this story is gentle with that.
- "Not really": good. keep this one for a friend who does.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In 1818, a young French priest who had nearly failed his studies was sent to Ars, a tiny farm village. “There is little love of God there,” he was told.
2. Lost in the fog, he asked a shepherd boy the way. When they reached the village, he said: “You showed me the way to Ars. I’ll show you the way to heaven.” _(label: as the story is told)_

> **Question 1** (finish the line): “You showed me the way to Ars. I’ll show you the way to ____ .”
>
> A. heaven  **(right answer)**
> B. Lyon
> C. the church
>
> **Why:** Heaven. His name was John Vianney, and that was the whole job, as he saw it.

3. At first, the little church was nearly empty. He prayed there for hours before dawn, visited the farmhouses one by one, and ate very little.

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): Years later, people were coming to Ars from all over France. What do you think drew them?
>
> A. His grand, clever sermons
> B. A new shrine with a healing spring
> C. Confession: he listened, and people left lighter  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** He wasn’t a great scholar or a great speaker. He was a great listener.

4. They came to confess. Rich and poor, the curious and the lost waited in line outside his church, some all through the night.

> **Question 3** (why?): Why would people wait all night to tell a stranger their worst mistakes?
>
> A. A new law made them confess
> B. They hoped to hear, out loud, that they were forgiven  **(right answer)**
> C. They wanted to meet someone famous
>
> **Why:** Some came out of curiosity, sure. But the line kept growing because people walked out forgiven, and they told their friends.


> **Question 4** (guess before the story continues): In his later years, how much of his day do you think he spent in the confessional?
>
> A. Often more than twelve hours  **(right answer)**
> B. About an hour after morning Mass
> C. Saturday afternoons only
>
> **Why:** Most of every day. Cold in winter, stifling in summer, for decades.

5. So he sat in a cramped wooden box, often more than twelve hours a day, freezing in winter, sweltering in summer. Year after year, he listened.
6. He was gentle. He once said his secret was to give people a small penance, and quietly do the rest of the praying for them himself.
7. Today Saint John Vianney is the patron of parish priests. And in confession, Catholics still say it plainly, out loud, and hear out loud that they are forgiven. _(label: today)_

> **Question 5** (how confession goes): Match each part of confession to what happens.
>
> Right pairs:
> - confessing = you name what you did, plainly
> - the penance = a prayer or small act to make it right
> - absolution = the priest’s words: you’re forgiven
>
> **Why:** You can kneel behind a screen or sit face to face. Catholics believe God does the forgiving; the priest speaks for him.


> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s why catholics tell a person. on easter night, the risen jesus told his friends, “whose sins you forgive, they are forgiven.” so forgiveness is spoken out loud, through a priest, who may never reveal what he hears, to anyone, for any reason. that’s the seal.
> - orthodox christians also confess to a priest; many protestants confess straight to God. and if it’s been years, you can start with “it’s been a long time.” vianney sat waiting in that box for decades. catholics believe God waits like that too.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): A man confesses to Father Vianney. Later the man’s worried wife comes and asks what her husband said. From this lesson, what must the priest do?
>
> A. Tell her, since she’s family
> B. Say nothing at all about it, ever  **(right answer)**
> C. Tell her only the parts that would help her
>
> **Why:** The seal. Not to family, not to police, not even a hint. That’s why people dared to stand in that line.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> name one thing you’ve been carrying in one plain sentence, out loud or on a scrap of paper you can tear up after. if you’re catholic and it feels right, you could look up when your parish hears confessions. or just learn how it goes. your choice.

- "I’ll name it today": once it’s said, it has edges. that’s already smaller.
- "I’m not ready yet": that’s okay. the curé waited in that box for years. you can take your time.

**What the learner keeps:** The confessional door. Say it out loud, then it’s smaller.

5. Caption 2 says he was “lost in the fog” when he met the shepherd boy. Confirm the fog is part of the traditional account.

## Day 4: A lap full of pebbles

**Story:** A story of the desert monks

**Story source**

- Palladius, Lausiac History, chapter 20 (Paul of Pherme): he prayed three hundred prayers a day, counting with pebbles held in his lap, casting one out at each prayer; troubled on hearing of a virgin in a village who prayed seven hundred, he consulted Macarius, who said he himself made only a hundred prayers a day, worked and received the brothers, and was not condemned by his heart; if Paul was troubled after three hundred, either he did not pray with a pure heart or he could pray more.
- Rosary from Latin rosarium, rose garden; the tradition of 150 Paters/Aves as a ‘poor man’s psalter’ alongside the monastic 150 psalms (see Herbert Thurston, ‘The Rosary’, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912).
- Structure: crucifix, introductory beads, five decades (Our Father on the single bead, ten Hail Marys, Glory Be); the mysteries (Pope John Paul II, Rosarium Virginis Mariae, 2002).
- Comparative note: the Orthodox prayer rope (chotki/komboskini) for the Jesus Prayer; most Protestant traditions do not use the rosary.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? have you ever seen a rosary hanging from a car mirror, or in someone’s purse, and wondered what it was for?

- "Yes, all the time": then today you’ll finally know. it’s simpler than you think.
- "My family has one": then this one might feel like home. listen.
- "I thought it was a charm": lots of people do. the real answer starts in a desert. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. Long ago, in the Egyptian desert, lived a monk named Paul. Every day he prayed three hundred prayers, dropping a pebble from his lap for each one.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Then Paul hears of a woman in a village who prays seven hundred times a day. How does he feel?
>
> A. Inspired to pray more, right away
> B. Sure the story must be exaggerated
> C. Troubled, as if his own prayers weren’t enough  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Three hundred suddenly felt small. Comparing can do that, even to a desert monk.

2. Then he heard of a woman in a village who prayed seven hundred times a day. Paul was deeply troubled. Were his prayers not enough?
3. He walked to the wise elder Macarius. Macarius said: I pray only a hundred a day. I work, I welcome visitors, and my heart doesn’t accuse me.

> **Question 2** (guess before the story continues): What do you think Macarius tells Paul next?
>
> A. Look at the heart you pray with, not at her number  **(right answer)**
> B. Pray only a hundred, like me
> C. Stop listening to stories about other people
>
> **Why:** He measures by the heart, not the count. His own hundred left him at peace.

4. If three hundred leave you uneasy, he said, either you aren’t praying with a pure heart, or you could pray more. Look at the heart, not the count.

> **Question 3** (why?): So what were Paul’s pebbles actually for?
>
> A. Proving he prayed more than others
> B. Keeping count, so his mind could stay on the prayer  **(right answer)**
> C. Keeping time, like a clock
>
> **Why:** Pebbles just keep track. The prayer is somewhere else: in the heart.

5. Centuries later, monks prayed all 150 psalms. People who couldn’t read prayed 150 short prayers instead, counting on knotted strings. _(label: a traditional account)_
6. That grew into the rosary: five sets of ten beads. On each single bead, the Our Father. On each of the ten, a Hail Mary. Then a Glory Be.

> **Question 4** (guess the word): Rosary comes from the Latin rosarium: a rose ____ .
>
> A. garden  **(right answer)**
> B. petal
> C. crown
>
> **Why:** A rose garden. The prayers are like a garland of roses.


> **Question 5** (on the string): Match each part of the rosary to the prayer said there.
>
> Right pairs:
> - the single bead = the Our Father
> - each of the ten beads = a Hail Mary
> - after the ten = the Glory Be
>
> **Why:** One set of ten takes only a few minutes, and plenty of people start there.

7. Today it hangs from rearview mirrors and sits in grandmothers’ purses. It was never a lucky charm. It’s for counting. _(label: today)_

> **The guide teaches:**
> - here’s the plain secret. while the fingers and lips keep the count, the mind is free to walk through scenes from the lives of jesus and mary. catholics call them the mysteries. that’s the real prayer.
> - the rosary is mostly a catholic practice. orthodox christians pray with a knotted prayer rope, and many protestants don’t use beads at all. like paul’s pebbles, the beads just hold the count.


> **Question 6** (the hard one): A friend says, “The rosary is just saying the same words over and over.” From the lesson, what would a Catholic say the real prayer is?
>
> A. Saying the words as many times as you can
> B. Holding the beads, which bless you on their own
> C. Walking through scenes of Jesus and Mary while the beads keep count  **(right answer)**
>
> **Why:** Like Paul’s pebbles, the beads only keep count. The heart is somewhere else, with Jesus and Mary in one of the mysteries.


**Today (the step the guide suggests for the day)**

> if you’d like, try one decade today, one set of ten: ten slow breaths on your fingers, or ten hail marys if you know them, while you picture one scene, like the road to emmaus. watch, learn, or join in.

- "I’ll try one decade": ten beads, one scene. your fingers keep the count.
- "I’ll just count ten breaths": that’s a real start. one pebble at a time, like paul.

**What the learner keeps:** A rosary. Hands busy, heart free.

**Please confirm**

1. Paul of Pherme’s dates: the lesson says only “long ago, in the Egyptian desert” (4th century). Confirm the Macarius he consulted (Palladius names Macarius; often identified as Macarius of Alexandria).
2. Macarius’s answer is paraphrased; confirm the paraphrase keeps the meaning (the count isn’t the measure; pray with a pure heart).
3. The ‘poor man’s psalter’ (150 short prayers on knotted strings) is labeled “a traditional account”; historians debate the details of the rosary’s origin.
4. The today step suggests picturing the road to Emmaus while praying a decade. Emmaus is not one of the twenty mysteries of the rosary. Confirm it is fine as an example, or suggest a mystery.

## Day 5: The wine runs out

**Story:** From John’s Gospel

**Story source**

- John 2:1-11 (the wedding at Cana in Galilee; the mother of Jesus is there; the wine fails; “They have no wine”; “Woman, what is that to me and to thee? my hour is not yet come”; “Whatsoever he shall say to you, do ye”; six stone waterpots for purification, two or three measures each; filled to the brim; the chief steward tastes: “thou hast kept the good wine until now”; the first of his signs).
- Catechism of the Catholic Church 2132 (veneration of images), 954-962 (communion of saints), 971 (devotion to Mary differs essentially from the adoration given to God), 2618 (Mary’s intercession at Cana), 2683 (the saints’ intercession).
- Comparative note: many Protestants honor Mary and the saints as examples but direct prayer to God alone; Orthodox Christians also ask the prayers of Mary and the saints.

**Opening question from the guide**

> before the story, can i ask you something? has someone ever noticed you needed help before you said a word?

- "Yes, my mom": then you’ll know the woman in this story. listen.
- "A friend did once": hold that friend in mind. this one is about noticing.
- "I’m usually the one who notices": then this story might feel like yours. listen.

**The lesson, in order**

Captions are the lines shown on screen with each picture. Questions are shown where they appear.

1. In the village of Cana there was a wedding. Mary was there, and Jesus and his friends were invited too.
2. In the middle of the feast, the wine ran out. For the young family, it would be a public embarrassment.

> **Question 1** (guess before the story continues): Someone notices. Who, and what do they do?
>
