# Scoring sheet

Score each lesson from 1 to 5 on six things, then write the single biggest problem. Use the descriptions below so a "3"
means the same thing from every reviewer. Whole numbers only. If you can't judge a dimension (for example a test user
judging accuracy), leave it blank rather than guess.

Each lesson takes about 6 minutes in the app: a spoken story or teaching, a short practice, "today's word", a "line to
carry" into the day, then a few quick games (match the pairs, true or myth, "the fork", a word in the original
language, and "the trapdoor": what you thought, what it means, what a scholar hears).

---

## (a) Accuracy to the tradition's own texts and teaching

Does it say what the tradition's texts and mainstream teachers actually say? Are quotes, references and facts right?
Are different schools or readings described fairly?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | A real error a believer or scholar would call wrong or hurtful: a made-up quote, a wrong teaching stated as fact, one school's view presented as everyone's on a contested point. |
| **2** | No outright falsehood, but misleading: an important detail wrong, a reference that doesn't say what's claimed, a big simplification that changes the meaning. |
| **3** | Basically right. Small slips or loose paraphrases a scholar would correct, nothing a practitioner would object to. |
| **4** | Right and careful. References check out, paraphrase is fair, and where readings differ it says so. |
| **5** | Something a respected teacher of the tradition would be happy to put their name to. Precise, fair, and gets the nuance right. |

## (b) Depth: does it make you think, or list facts?

Educators describe levels of thinking (often called "Bloom's levels"). In plain words, from shallow to deep:
**remember** (repeat a fact), **understand** (explain it in your own words), **apply** (use it in your own life or a
new case), **analyze** (see how parts fit, compare readings), **evaluate** (weigh it, argue for or against), **create**
(make something new with it, like your own reading or practice).

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | Trivia. A list of facts or a plot summary; nothing to understand beyond remembering. |
| **2** | Explains one idea clearly, but stays at "remember and understand". The "lesson" is a moral anyone could have guessed. |
| **3** | Explains well and asks you to apply it to your life. One real insight you didn't have before. |
| **4** | Gets you to analyze: shows why the text says it this way, sets two readings side by side, or connects to earlier lessons in a way that changes how you see them. |
| **5** | Leaves you with a genuine question or tension to think through yourself (evaluate or create), and gives you the tools to do it. You'd want to talk about it with someone. |

## (c) Memorability: would you remember it next week?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | Forgettable. You couldn't say what it was about an hour later. |
| **2** | You'd remember the topic, but not anything specific. |
| **3** | One clear image, story or phrase sticks. |
| **4** | A vivid image or story plus the point it makes; you could retell it to a friend. |
| **5** | It would come back to you on its own, in a real moment, days later. |

## (d) Insider respect: would a practitioner feel it was taught properly?

Imagine a sincere, practising member of this tradition (not a scholar) listening over your shoulder.

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | They'd be offended or embarrassed: mocking, tourist-brochure, or treats sacred things as props for self-help. |
| **2** | Polite but outside-in. Feels written about them, not with them; misses what matters to people who practise. |
| **3** | Respectful and fair. Nothing objectionable, though an insider might say "that's not quite how we'd put it". |
| **4** | Feels taught by someone who knows and loves the tradition: right terms, right weight, real practice. |
| **5** | An insider would want to share it with their own family or community. |

## (e) Voice and warmth

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | Cold, preachy, condescending, or salesy. |
| **2** | Flat or generic; could be any app's copy. Or trying too hard to be cute. |
| **3** | Pleasant and clear. |
| **4** | Warm, human, specific; you'd happily hear this voice every morning. |
| **5** | Distinctive. You'd recognize it and miss it. |

## (f) Learning design: does it build over time?

Look at "looking back" at the top, the games, the practice and the line to carry. Does today build on earlier days? Is
there real practice (doing, not just picturing)? Do the games make you recall and think, or just repeat what was said
two minutes ago? Would something learned on day 60 still be there on day 400?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **1** | Stands alone. Games are guessable without listening. No link to anything before or after. |
| **2** | Mentions yesterday, but games only test what was just said, and the practice is only imagined. |
| **3** | Clear link to earlier lessons; at least one game requires real understanding; a practice you could actually do. |
| **4** | Brings back something from weeks earlier and uses it; games make you think; the practice is concrete and doable today. |
| **5** | Feels like part of a designed course: it spaces out review, checks understanding, and steps up the difficulty on purpose. |

## The single biggest problem

In one or two sentences: **if we could fix only one thing about this lesson, what should it be?** Be specific ("the
claim in the second paragraph that X is wrong; the text says Y", "the fork question is obvious", "a Muslim listener
would expect the Prophet's name with an honorific", "this is a plot summary with a moral stuck on").
