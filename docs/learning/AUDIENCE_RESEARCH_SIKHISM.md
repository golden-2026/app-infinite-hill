# Audience research: young Sikh adults (18–35), North America and the UK

Researched 6 October 2026 from public web pages only. No logins, no posting, nothing submitted.

## Summary

Young Sikhs in the diaspora already *live* a lot of Sikhi: most grew up going to the gurdwara, eating langar sitting
on the floor, saying "Waheguru" at home, maybe wearing a kara, and — if their family kept the 5 Ks — not cutting their
hair. What's thin is the *meaning* behind what they already do and say, plus the words they can say but not translate
(Waheguru, the Mool Mantar, Ardas, Japji Sahib) and the symbols friends ask them about (the turban, the kara, the
Khanda). A second, very live thread for this group: being misread by outsiders — mistaken for Muslim, asked if the
turban is "a gang color" or "a hat," asked why they have a knife at school (the kirpan) — which means explaining their
own faith to others is a skill they need and don't always have. The strongest teachers for this audience (Basics of
Sikhi, SikhNet, SikhRI's Sojhi, Nanak Naam) mix short, plain-English explainer videos with story (Guru Nanak, the Panj
Pyare of 1699) and go deliberately light on Punjabi vocabulary and dates up front. Gurdwara Punjabi/Khalsa schools
front-load language and script (Gurmukhi) and history timelines; young adults say that's exactly what didn't stick,
while the story of the Khalsa, kirtan they can still sing, and langar as a felt experience did. So the first lessons
should start from something they already do or already get asked about, give the plain-English meaning in a minute,
and only later attach the Punjabi term, the historical date, or the Gurmukhi spelling.

**Honest gaps up front**

- **Reddit could not be fetched** (old.reddit.com and r/Sikh were refused by this research tool). I did not try to get
  around that. The "their own words" questions below come from Quora titles, FAQ pages written by Sikh advocacy groups
  for outsiders' and youths' questions, personal essays, and journal/magazine pieces about diaspora youth — not a
  direct read of Reddit threads. A person reading r/Sikh by hand for an hour would materially strengthen this section.
- **Instagram and TikTok engagement (likes, comments, which specific post did best) was mostly not visible** to this
  tool. Follower/like counts showed; which individual posts performed best usually did not, except where a creator's
  own interview or press coverage stated a number (e.g., a specific video hitting views in 24 hours).
- Some pages refused access outright: **pluralism.org/growing-up-sikh returned 403** (so that essay is cited only via
  search-result snippets, not a direct read); a Jakara Movement academic PDF and a few forum threads were summary-only.
- **TikTok and Instagram metrics are a snapshot of what public pages showed on 6 Oct 2026**, not a verified or audited
  count.
- "How often it comes up" rankings are my judgment from how many separate sources raise each topic, not a count.
- This report avoids taking a side on, or dwelling on, Punjab's political history (1984, Khalistan, Operation Blue
  Star). These are real and painful subjects that surface in some Sikh media and youth political organizing, but they
  are outside what a "first lessons" product should touch; I note only that they exist and that families disagree.

## 1. Creators and accounts teaching Sikhi to young or diaspora audiences

Reach numbers are what the public page showed on 6 Oct 2026.

### Basics of Sikhi

- **YouTube** [basicsofsikhi](https://www.youtube.com/user/basicsofsikhi/videos): by 2021 had **170K+ subscribers and
  2,500 videos in up to 18 languages**; more recent figures put it at **~280K subscribers**
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basics_of_Sikhi)). Started by the late Jagraj Singh (UK-based) specifically
  to reach young, assimilated Sikhs who "cannot speak or understand Punjabi so much"
  ([Wikipedia: Jagraj Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagraj_Singh)); credited with helping a wave of young Sikhs
  "return to their faith."
- **Instagram** [@basicsofsikhi](https://www.instagram.com/BasicsOfSikhi/): **~186K followers**.
- **TikTok** [@basicsofsikhi](https://www.tiktok.com/@basicsofsikhi?lang=en): **~66.8K followers, 1.4M likes**.
- **Podcast**: [Basics of Sikhi Podcast](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/basics-of-sikhi-podcast/id1543685493)
  on Apple Podcasts.
- **Format and tone**: direct-to-camera explainer videos, katha (storytelling/discourse), kirtan, and debates, pitched
  explicitly "for beginners" through to advanced ([basicsofsikhi.com](https://www.basicsofsikhi.com/projects/basics-of-sikhi-youtube-channel)).
  Plain English first; gentle, earnest tone rather than comic.
- **What they sell**: the project is framed as a free education campaign (YouTube, print material, social media); no
  storefront was visible in these searches, unlike the Hindu-path comparison case.

### SikhNet

- **Website hub** [sikhnet.com/kids-youth](https://www.sikhnet.com/kids-youth): runs a **Kids & Youth section**
  including an annual **SikhNet Online Youth Film Festival**.
- **Instagram** [@sikhnet](https://www.instagram.com/sikhnet/): **~11K followers, 1,340 posts** — a long-running,
  high-volume account rather than a viral one.
- **TikTok** [@sikhnet.com](https://www.tiktok.com/@sikhnet.com): only **~97 followers** — TikTok is not where SikhNet
  has traction.
- **Format**: articles, explainer pages (e.g. ["Why Do Sikhs Wear Turbans?"](https://sikhnet.com/s/WhyTurbans)),
  stories, and the long-running **Sikh Research Institute–style FAQ culture** that treats "why do we…" questions as
  normal and answerable.

### Sikh Research Institute (SikhRI) — Sojhi

- [sikhri.org/sojhi](https://sikhri.org/sojhi): **Sojhi** is SikhRI's K-12 curriculum and resource program, explicitly
  built to help young people "derive insights" and build a "Guru-centered life," with videos, podcasts, courses and
  lesson plans ([Sojhi lesson plans](https://www.sikhri.org/sojhi-lesson-plans)).
- **Kids podcast**: [Sojhi: A Kid's Cast](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/sojhi-a-kids-cast/id1549167681).
- **Youth/young-adult podcasts**: [Wisdom & Youth (English)](https://sikhri.org/podcasts/wisdom-youth-english) and a
  Punjabi-language sister show — short episodes built around a single sakhi (story), e.g. an episode on Guru
  Harikrishan Sahib becoming Guru at age five.
- **The Sikh Cast** ([Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-sikh-cast/id1610410001)): discussion
  show on issues relevant to Sikhs worldwide, more for adults.
- Overall tone: scholarly but accessible, story-first for the youngest tier, discussion-first for teens/adults.

### Nanak Naam

- Founded 2013 by Satpal Singh (UK), started from three YouTube videos and a Facebook page
  ([nanaknaam.org/about-us](https://www.nanaknaam.org/about-us/)).
- **YouTube**: **~100K subscribers** by 2025; separate Punjabi-language channel too.
- **Podcast**: [Mental Health and Wellbeing by Nanak Naam](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/mental-health-and-wellbeing-by-nanak-naam/id1491027968)
  — Gurmat (Guru's teaching) framed explicitly as a mental-health/mindfulness resource, a very "meet them where they
  are" angle for a stressed, online 20-something audience.
- **Format**: videos, tutorials and courses on "discovering your best self" through Sikh teaching of oneness and
  meditation — closer to the self-help framing that works well for the Hindu-path comparison's Gaur Gopal Das/Jay
  Shetty audience.

**Which posts look most engaged?** Visible, concrete numbers were rare. The clearest signal: **a turban-tying tutorial
hit 1M views in 24 hours** on TikTok, and tutorials/explainers about "what's actually under the turban" draw comments
saying people didn't know it wasn't a hat. That mirrors the Hindu-path finding that **myth-busting/surprise and
"explain the thing people always wondered about"** outperforms lecture-style content.

## 3. What families and gurdwaras already use to teach

### Gurdwara Punjabi/Khalsa schools (the most common weekly program)

- Mission, in their own words: "instill love for the Sikh religion along with its beliefs, values and customs" in
  children ([Khalsa School](https://gurunanaksikhheritage.org/khalsa-school/)).
- **Core subjects**: Punjabi language and Gurmukhi script (so children can read scripture), basic vocabulary, poems,
  stories and biographies of "great Sikhs," Sikh history from the Mughal period "up to Operation Blue Star," kirtan,
  and in some schools Gatka (martial art) and Gurbani santheya (scripture study) ([khalsaschool.us/curriculum](https://khalsaschool.us/curriculum/punjabi-curriculum)).
- Classes are **integrated with gurdwara life**: kids present to the whole sangat (congregation) during Gurpurab
  (Guru anniversary) celebrations ([Khalsa School Calgary](https://khalsaschoolcalgary.ca/punjabi/)).
- In short: **the gurdwara's own first-years program is language, script and history first**, with story and kirtan
  woven in — the opposite order of what young adults later say they wished for (see below).

### Jakara Movement youth camps (older teens, more intensive)

- **Bhujangi Youth Academy** (boys 13–17) and **Bhujangan Leadership Academy** (girls 13–18): 10-day camps combining
  paath (reading scripture), history classes (e.g. "Guru Gobind Singh: the early years"), Sikh arts/scholarship, and
  leadership/brotherhood-building challenges ([jakara.org/bhujangi](https://www.jakara.org/bhujangi); [jakara.org/bhujangan](https://www.jakara.org/bhujangan)).
- College level shifts toward **civic engagement and political voice** through campus chapters — useful to know as a
  pipeline, but a different register from a first-lessons product ([Jakara Movement Chapter program](https://www.jakara.org/)).

### SikhRI's Sojhi (curriculum-first, not gurdwara-run)

- A dedicated K-12 curriculum project aimed at building "insights" toward a "Guru-centered life" rather than starting
  from language or history; includes video, podcast and course formats by age band ([sikhri.org/sojhi](https://sikhri.org/sojhi)).
- Its youngest-tier podcast (**Sojhi: A Kid's Cast**) and **Wisdom & Youth** series build single episodes around one
  sakhi (story) at a time — closer to the "one story, one meaning" approach this audience responds to.

### SikhLens Digital Voices Program

- Rather than only teaching kids about Sikhi, it **teaches them filmmaking** and has them make 125+ short films about
  it — a strong signal that **making and sharing**, not just absorbing, is something this age group already does well
  when given the tools ([sikhlens.com/initiatives/sdvp](https://sikhlens.com/initiatives/sdvp/)).

### What young adults say they remember, and what they missed

- **Remember**: reciting Japji Sahib with a parent on the way to school, kirtan and harmonium, sewa (community
  service) at the gurdwara, and the phrase "Waheguru Ji Ka Khalsa, Waheguru Ji Ki Fateh" as a greeting — all from one
  essayist's own account of his childhood ([SALDEF](https://saldef.org/one-young-sikh-in-america/)).
- **Missed, in their own words**: what the words they can recite actually mean; feeling caught between "too
  religious" for non-Sikh classmates and "not religious enough" for the wider Sikh community once he stopped keeping
  his hair ([SALDEF](https://saldef.org/one-young-sikh-in-america/)); fluency in Punjabi despite literacy in Gurmukhi,
  and the shame that gap can carry ([Substack essay summary](https://ayushyakhanna.substack.com/p/dadi-and-punjabi/comments)).
- A recurring theme across sources: **the "questioning stage" is treated as normal and expected**, not a failure —
  camps and seminars exist specifically to help Sikh teens and young adults work through questions like "why should I
  bow to a book?" (search-result summary of [pluralism.org/growing-up-sikh](https://pluralism.org/growing-up-sikh)).
- The language-decline data point is stark: most of an estimated 20 million Sikhs worldwide reportedly cannot speak
  Punjabi or read Gurmukhi fluently (search-result summary, multiple sources on Punjabi language decline) — meaning a
  product that assumes no Punjabi fluency is describing the *majority* experience, not an edge case.

## 4. Everyday "explain my childhood" moments

- **At the gurdwara**: covering your head (everyone, every time, regardless of belief), taking your shoes off,
  sitting on the floor as everyone does, bowing before the Guru Granth Sahib, hearing Ardas and standing for it,
  receiving karah parshad (the warm sweet handful at the end) with cupped hands, and langar afterward — sitting in
  rows, eating the same simple vegetarian food as everyone else regardless of who they are
  ([Wikipedia: Karah Parshad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karah_Parshad); [NBC News on langar](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/asian-america/langar-tradition-expresses-key-sikh-principles-n436326)).
- **The 5 Ks, if your family keeps them**: kesh (uncut hair), the turban that holds it, the kara on the wrist, the
  kirpan (and the very real, very common question from classmates and TSA agents about carrying "a knife"), the
  kanga (small comb), the kachera (specific undergarment) — each one a daily, physical habit most kids grow up with
  and few can explain fully ([Five Ks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ks)).
- **Words said at home without translation**: "Waheguru" as an exclamation, a thank-you, a comfort; the Mool Mantar
  heard at the start of scripture; Japji Sahib recited with a parent in the morning; the greeting "Waheguru Ji Ka
  Khalsa, Waheguru Ji Ki Fateh."
- **The Khanda**: the symbol on the Nishan Sahib (flagpole) outside every gurdwara and on the orange/blue Sikh flag —
  most recognize it instantly and can't explain the two swords, the circle, or the center blade
  ([Khanda, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanda_(Sikh_symbol))).
- **Vaisakhi**: experienced as a big spring festival, a Nagar Kirtan parade with free food handed out along the
  route, new clothes — and, separately, "the birthday of the Khalsa," without most people connecting the harvest
  festival and the 1699 story as the same day
  ([kdsross.com](https://kdsross.com/vaisakhi/what-is-vaisakhi); [Nagar Kirtan, SikhiWiki](https://www.sikhiwiki.org/index.php/Nagar_Kirtan)).
- **Being misread by outsiders**: "is that a hat?", "what's under there?", assumptions the turban means gang
  affiliation, classmates or TSA treating the kirpan as a weapon, and — the sharpest one — being mistaken for Muslim
  and, especially for anyone who grew up after 2001, the family memory of hate crimes and slurs tied to that
  confusion ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/crsj/resources/human-rights/2024-november/misconceptions-misidentification-sikhs/); [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2016/09/15/us/sikh-hate-crime-victims/index.html)).
- **Names**: every Sikh boy/man with "Singh" and every girl/woman with "Kaur" as a middle or last name — experienced
  from birth, rarely explained.
- **Family language gap**: understanding spoken Punjabi at home but not always being able to speak it back, or being
  able to read Gurmukhi phonetically in scripture without understanding the words — a near-universal, quietly
  embarrassing experience for this age group ([Substack essay](https://ayushyakhanna.substack.com/p/dadi-and-punjabi/comments)).
- **Weddings and Bhangra**: Anand Karaj (the wedding ceremony around the Guru Granth Sahib), the laavan (four hymns
  walked around scripture), Bhangra at nearly every family function — a joyful, very visible cultural thread distinct
  from the religious ceremony itself, and one this audience already connects strongly with music and dance.

## How the best creators hook people

1. **Answer the question outsiders (and friends) actually ask.** Sikh Coalition's FAQ is literally titled around
   "Can I touch your turban?" and "What's under there?" — Basics of Sikhi and the TikTok educators above do the same
   thing for a Sikh audience itself. Example opener: "You've probably been asked 'is that a hat?' Here's the real answer."
2. **Use a short story with people in it, not a doctrine.** The Panj Pyare of 1699 — a scholar, a farmer, a
   water-carrier, a cloth-printer, a barber, from completely different backgrounds, choosing to stand up together —
   is the single most-cited "start here" story across camp curricula and kids' content (Jakara, maastarji.com).
3. **Show, don't just tell, the physical practice.** Sunny Osahn's turban-tying tutorials (1M views in 24 hours) work
   because they're visual and hands-on, the Sikh-path equivalent of the Hindu-path finding that short, visual,
   "myth-buster" content outperforms lecture.
4. **Give the word before the history.** Basics of Sikhi and SikhNet lead with "what does this mean right now, for
   you" rather than a date or a Gurmukhi spelling; dates and script come later, if at all, in the early lessons.
5. **Reframe old practice as a modern need.** Nanak Naam's whole podcast is Gurmat (Guru's teaching) explicitly
   packaged as mental health and mindfulness — meeting a 20-something's real daily worry (stress, anxiety) rather
   than opening with theology.
6. **Use the shared joke/shared wound.** Jus Reign's comedy about his parents and his own airport turban story works
   the same way Zarna Garg's "Indian mom" bit works for young Hindus — a laugh that's really a door into identity.
7. **Let them make it, not just watch it.** SikhLens's Digital Voices Program trained 1,000+ kids to make 125+ short
   films; a "make something about your own 5 Ks moment" prompt would fit this audience's habits.
8. **Keep the Punjabi light at first, and explain, don't assume.** Jagraj Singh built Basics of Sikhi specifically
   for Sikhs who "cannot speak or understand Punjabi so much" — that's the explicit design principle behind the
   single most successful creator in this space.

## What to avoid early

- **Opening with Punjabi vocabulary, Gurmukhi script, or a date-heavy history timeline.** That's the gurdwara
  Khalsa-school order (language and script first, "Mughal period up to Operation Blue Star" as a syllabus line), and
  it's exactly what the audience's own essays say didn't stick. Meaning first, word second, date last (if at all).
- **Treating "I don't speak Punjabi" or "I haven't taken amrit" as disqualifying.** Most of the worldwide Sikh
  population reportedly can't read Gurmukhi fluently; amrit is explicitly framed by Sikh sources themselves as a
  mature, voluntary choice, not a childhood requirement. Shaming language here ("not a real Sikh," "too religious" /
  "not religious enough") is the exact wound essayists describe from their own community.
- **Treating the 5 Ks as costume or aesthetic.** The turban, kara and kirpan are articles of faith with specific
  meaning (truth, restraint, courage); content that reduces them to "cool fashion facts" without the meaning risks
  the same flattening the Hindu-path report warned about with mixed-up stories.
- **Leading with Sikh/Hindu or Sikh/Muslim comparison as a "which is right" debate.** The confusion is real and worth
  answering plainly and respectfully, but framing it as a contest or grievance (either direction) is out of scope for
  early lessons and risks the exact political territory this project wants to avoid.
- **1984, Khalistan, Operation Blue Star, and related political history.** These are real, still-painful, and still
  actively discussed within Sikh youth organizing (e.g., some Jakara Movement college programming); they deserve
  careful, reviewed treatment by people with real expertise, not an early, casual lesson. Note their existence; take
  no side; don't put them in a first week.
- **"Scientific explanation" oversell for practices that are really about faith and identity** (e.g., reducing the
  kara or kesh to only a physiological or practical rationale). Give the meaning the tradition itself gives; don't
  over-rationalize to make it seem more "modern."
- **Assuming one regional or sectarian practice is universal.** Not all Sikh households are vegetarian; not all
  Sikhs keep the 5 Ks or are baptized (amritdhari); kesdhari (keeping unshorn hair) and non-kesdhari identities both
  exist within the community. Say "many Sikh families…" rather than "Sikhs always…"

## A proposed first week

| Day | Topic | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Waheguru: the word you already say** | It's the single most universal, most-said, least-translated word this audience has. Starting here says "you already know more than you think," the same opening move that worked for the Hindu-path report. |
| 2 | **Langar: why everyone sits on the floor and eats the same food** | A concrete, visual, already-lived experience with a clean, emotionally satisfying answer (equality) and zero controversy — a safe, warm second day. |
| 3 | **The turban and the kara: what they are, what they mean** | Answers the question this audience is asked most by outsiders and often can't fully answer themselves; pairs lived identity with real meaning (truth, restraint). |
| 4 | **The Panj Pyare: five strangers who said yes** | Moves from objects/words into story — the most-cited "start here" narrative across camp curricula, with built-in drama and a diverse cast that resists any single "type" of Sikh. |
| 5 | **Ardas: the prayer everyone stands for** | Takes a ritual moment nearly everyone has physically experienced (standing at the end of a gurdwara service) and finally explains what's being said and why. |
| 6 | **"Are Sikhs Hindu? Are Sikhs Muslim?"** | Directly answers the single most common outside-world confusion this audience is asked to field, handled plainly and without putting down either tradition. |
| 7 | **The Khanda and Vaisakhi: the flag outside every gurdwara** | Closes the week on a symbol they've seen their whole life, tying it to the Panj Pyare story from day 4 and giving a natural bridge into the yearly festival calendar. |
