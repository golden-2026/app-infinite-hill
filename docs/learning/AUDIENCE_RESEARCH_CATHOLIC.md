# Audience research: young American Catholics (18–35)

Researched 6 October 2026 from public web pages only. No logins, no posting, nothing submitted.

## Summary

Most young Americans raised Catholic already carry a bodyful of half-explained habits — crossing themselves, dipping
fingers in holy water, wearing ashes on a Wednesday, a rosary or a saint's medal in a drawer, a grandmother's altarcito,
a confirmation name nobody remembers choosing why — and a much thinner layer of explanation underneath. The data backs
the founder's worry directly: when Pew asked Catholics what they believe actually happens at Mass, only **31% picked
the Church's own teaching** that the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ; 69% called it symbolic
([Pew 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/08/05/transubstantiation-eucharist-u-s-catholics/)) — meaning
most people who sat through years of First Communion prep never absorbed the one idea the whole sacrament rests on.
About half of U.S. adults raised Catholic have left the Church at some point in their life
([Pew 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2015/09/15/half-of-u-s-adults-raised-catholic-have-left-the-church-at-some-point/)),
and among people born in the 1980s or later only about half who were raised Catholic still are, versus six-in-ten or
more born before 1960 ([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/religious-upbringing-and-childhood-education/)).
Yet a real counter-current exists: Gen Z identification with Catholicism rose from 15% to 21% in one year per a Harvard
survey cited by Fox News ([Fox News/Hallow](https://www.foxnews.com/lifestyle/christian-app-hallow-100-million-prayer-mark-wide-audience-believers.amp)),
the prayer app Hallow has passed 30 million downloads with 40% of its users not even Catholic
([Hallow via Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/lifestyle/christian-app-hallow-100-million-prayer-mark-wide-audience-believers.amp)),
and a wave of young creators — comedians, converts, a USC junior doing Q&A videos — are pulling hundreds of thousands
of mostly Gen Z followers by being funny and personal rather than doctrinal. The pattern that works, on TikTok,
Instagram and in Bala-Vihar-style religious ed alike, is the same one the founder suspects: **open with the familiar
object or memory ("why do we get ashes," "why a wafer," "why confess to a guy in a box"), answer it warmly and briefly,
and only later use the churchy word for it** (transubstantiation, sacramental, catechesis). Hispanic, Filipino, Irish
and Italian family practice supplies a huge shared bank of these moments — quinceañeras, Simbang Gabi, saints' feast
days, "don't step over the rosary" — that this audience already lived through but rarely had explained plainly.

**Honest gaps up front**

- **Reddit (r/Catholicism, r/Christianity, r/CatholicMemes) could not be reached directly** by this tool, same as the
  Hindu report before it. The "their own words" questions below come from Quora question titles (some asked by
  non-Catholics or international users, not only young Americans), parish FAQ pages written to answer the questions
  families actually ask, and a few first-person essays. A person reading Reddit by hand for an hour would strengthen
  this section a lot.
- **TikTok and Instagram engagement (likes, comments, which specific post did best) was mostly not visible.** Follower
  and "top post view count" numbers came through on a few pages (Modash, Feedspot, Yahoo/NCR coverage) but most
  individual creator profiles could not be opened directly by this tool.
- Some pages were summarized by the fetch tool rather than read in full (noted inline); a couple (NCR, full Modash
  listing) returned only partial content or a 403.
- "How often it comes up" rankings are judgment calls based on how many separate sources raise each topic, not a
  formal count.
- This report, like the Hindu one, is written to be read by a non-technical founder: plain language, links on every
  claim, judgment flagged as judgment.

## 3. What families, CCD, Catholic schools and Confirmation prep already use

### CCD / parish religious education (the most common path)

- Most children start around **first grade, age 6–7**, in classes still commonly called "CCD" (Confraternity of
  Christian Doctrine) even though the name is decades old ([CatechismClass](https://www.catechismclass.com/catholic_sunday_school.php)).
- First-grade theme across many parish programs: **"God our Creator" / "God is all around us"** — simple, sensory,
  not yet doctrinal. Each grade layers in basic prayer, scripture stories, Jesus, Mary and the saints, the feasts and
  seasons of the Church year, and the parts of the Mass ([search summary of parish curricula](https://sscmc.org/ccd-yearly-curriculum/)).
- Broad first-years content across programs: the Gospel story of Jesus, the seven sacraments (names and purpose),
  the Ten Commandments, basic prayers (Our Father, Hail Mary, the Creed), Mary and the saints, and "how to live as a
  practicing Catholic day to day" ([CatechismClass](https://www.catechismclass.com/catholic_sunday_school.php)).
- **69% of all Americans** report at least some formal religious education growing up (Catholic school or CCD/Sunday
  school); of those raised Catholic, **35% had "a lot" (7+ years), 18% "a fair amount" (4–6 years), 16% "a little"
  (1–3 years)**, and 30% had none ([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/religious-upbringing-and-childhood-education/)).

### First Communion and Confirmation prep (the two sacraments this audience actually remembers attending)

- First Communion prep centers on the Eucharist and the sacrament of Reconciliation (first confession usually
  precedes First Communion); Confirmation prep covers the Holy Spirit and the "gifts of the Spirit," plus choosing a
  sponsor and (often) a Confirmation name ([Archdiocese of Denver](https://archden.org/sacraments/confirmation/);
  [CatechismClass](https://www.catechismclass.com/sacrament-prep.php)).
- This search could not find a study or survey of what young adults specifically say they wish they'd learned in
  First Communion/Confirmation prep (flagged as a gap above). What's known instead is the proxy evidence: the
  transubstantiation belief gap (only 31% hold the taught belief, Section 2) strongly suggests the *meaning* of First
  Communion is the part that doesn't stick, even though the *event* (white dress, big family party) clearly does.

### Quinceañera and padrino prep (Hispanic family-specific)

- Treated by many parishes as its own sacramental-adjacent milestone: a Mass, renewal of baptismal vows, and a
  "padrinos de velación" couple assigned as the girl's ongoing faith mentors — explicitly pre-Hispanic in cultural
  roots (Aztec/Maya coming-of-age customs blended with Catholic liturgy) as well as Catholic in form
  ([Catholic Company](https://www.catholiccompany.com/blogs/magazine/quinceanera-celebration-5825); [Bridges UU summary](https://bridgesuu.org/day-21-hispanic-and-latine-family-traditions/)).

### What sticks and what gets missed (synthesis, not one single study)

- **Sticks:** the sacramental *events* and their trappings — First Communion dresses, godparents, a Confirmation
  party, ashes on the forehead, giving something up for Lent, the Christmas Eve or Simbang Gabi Mass with the whole
  family, a saint's medal worn since childhood.
- **Missed:** the *meaning* underneath almost all of it — what transubstantiation is actually claiming (Pew's 31%
  figure), the difference between worship (God alone) and honor (saints/Mary), why confession needs a priest, why
  Lent lands on specific days, what a Confirmation name is for. The same pattern the Hindu report found: ritual
  transmits fine through family life; the "why" needs to be taught separately, or it never arrives.
- **Leaving is common and multi-causal, not purely a knowledge gap:** losing belief in the teachings (46%), the
  clergy abuse scandals (39%), and disagreement with Church positions (37%) are the three top reasons former
  Catholics give for leaving ([Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/12/15/why-some-americans-have-left-catholicism-while-others-stay/)).
  This app cannot and should not try to resolve the scandals or doctrinal disagreements — but it can close the
  knowledge gap, which is squarely in scope and is the one lever within reach.
- Childhood experience correlates with what happens later: only **26%** of people who left Catholicism for "no
  religion" recall a mostly positive childhood religious experience, versus **73%** of people who stayed Catholic
  ([same Pew report](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/12/15/why-some-americans-have-left-catholicism-while-others-stay/)).
  Tone and warmth in how this is taught plausibly matters as much as content.

## 4. Everyday "explain my childhood" moments

- **At Mass:** dipping fingers in the holy water font and crossing yourself on the way in, genuflecting toward the
  altar, standing/kneeling/sitting at specific points, the priest's vestment color changing with the season (purple
  for Advent and Lent, green for "Ordinary Time," white or gold for feasts, red for martyrs/Pentecost, rose twice a
  year) ([liturgical color FAQ summary](https://stcolumbacatholic.com/colors-of-the-liturgical-seasons)), the bell
  at the consecration, receiving a wafer on the tongue or in the hand, the sign of peace.
- **Ash Wednesday:** a cross of ash smudged on the forehead, worn all day at school or work, with the priest saying
  either "remember you are dust" or "repent and believe"
  ([search summary](https://www.coraevans.com/blog/article/why-ashes-on-ash-wednesday)).
- **The rosary:** counting beads, not knowing if it's 53 or 59 of them, a rosary hanging from a rearview mirror or a
  grandmother's nightstand, being told not to let it touch the floor.
- **Confession:** the booth or "reconciliation room," a kid's nervous first confession, the formula "Bless me Father
  for I have sinned," wondering why a person has to be in the room at all.
- **Confirmation:** picking a saint's name you may never use again, a sponsor who isn't your parent, being slapped
  lightly on the cheek (an old custom some parishes still reference) or anointed with oil.
- **Lent:** giving up chocolate, soda, or social media for 40 days, fish on Fridays (fish fries at the parish hall),
  not saying "Alleluia" until Easter.
- **Saints and medals:** a St. Christopher medal on a keychain for travel, a St. Michael medal, a Miraculous Medal, a
  patron saint picked for your name or your job, holy cards tucked in a wallet or missal.
- **Hispanic family home:** an altarcito with candles, a rosary, and photos of the dead; a quinceañera's Mass before
  the party; padrinos who show up at every major event, not just the wedding; novenas said for nine nights after a
  death or before a feast day.
- **Filipino family home:** a Santo Niño statue somewhere in the house; waking up at 4 a.m. nine days before
  Christmas for Simbang Gabi, then eating street food after Mass with the whole extended family
  ([St. Louis Review](https://www.stlouisreview.com/story/simbang-gabi-is-a-filipino-is-a-tradition-that-honors-the-blessed-mother-in-the-days-leading-up-to-christmas/)).
- **Irish and Italian family home:** a crucifix or Sacred Heart picture on the wall, a patron feast day tied to the
  family's town of origin, grace before big holiday meals, a grandmother who still won't eat meat on any Friday, not
  just during Lent.
- **Words people use without defining:** "cradle Catholic," "lapsed Catholic," "CEO Catholic" (Christmas/Easter
  only, sometimes rendered "A&P Catholic")
  ([Wikipedia: Lapsed Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapsed_Catholic)), "cultural Catholic."
- **Outside the home:** a Protestant friend asking "why do you pray to Mary, isn't that idolatry," or "why do you
  need a priest, can't you just talk to God," or "why do Catholics have more books in the Bible."

## How the best creators hook people

1. **Open with a Q&A or myth-busting title aimed at a real misconception.** Fr. David Michael Moses' "A Weekend As a
   Catholic Priest" and pop-culture mashups ("The Gospel According to Shrek"); Jacob Palchak's whole format is
   "addressing misconceptions in the church" ([Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/celebrity/articles/gen-z-catholic-influencers-church-110000187.html)).
2. **Make the joke first, and let the teaching ride in on it.** The top Filipino creator's entire premise, stated in
   his own bio, is "holy doesn't have to be boring" ([@thecatholiccomedian](https://creators.feedspot.com/catholic_tiktok_influencers/)):
   comedy as the delivery mechanism, not a decoration on top of teaching.
3. **Tell it as a personal story, not a doctrine.** Emily Dinneny's whole account is her own conversion story;
   Amber-Rose Schneider started posting because she wanted to see herself, a teenager, reflected; Fr. Mike talks
   about his own "reversion" after college rather than lecturing about reversion in general.
4. **Cross over into a non-faith niche the audience already follows.** Anthony Gross mixes fitness content with
   faith messaging; David Henrie brings a Disney-era celebrity audience with him.
5. **Use an app/format built for a tiny daily habit, not a long sit-down lesson.** Hallow's whole growth story is
   bite-size guided audio prayer; Fr. Mike's "Bible in a Year" works because it's one short episode a day, not one
   long book.
6. **Pair a big public spike with the everyday product.** Hallow's #1 App Store moment came from a 30-second Super
   Bowl ad, but the app itself is a daily-use habit loop — the ad got attention, the habit kept people.
7. **Let real misconceptions be the hook, then correct gently.** Nearly every top "explainer" article in this
   research (Immaculate Conception vs. Virgin Birth, purgatory vs. limbo, 53 vs. 59 rosary beads) is structured as
   "you probably think X, actually it's Y" — the same curiosity-gap pattern the Hindu report found with Project
   Shivoham.
8. **Give people something to do with their hands or voice, not just something to know.** The rosary explainer sites
   all land on the same point — the physical counting is what frees the mind to pray; Hallow sells a guided *action*
   (a prayer to follow along with), not an article to read.

## What to avoid early

- **Opening with doctrinal vocabulary:** "transubstantiation," "latria vs. dulia," "soteriology," denominational
  comparison tables. These are the right *destination*, never the right *opening line* — Pew's own transubstantiation
  finding shows that leading with the term for years still left 69% of Catholics describing it as symbolic.
- **Leading with the scandal or the politics.** Clergy abuse and the Church's social/political positions are real,
  cited reasons people leave (39% and 37%; [Pew 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/12/15/why-some-americans-have-left-catholicism-while-others-stay/)),
  but they are heavy, contested, and not what this audience is asking about in their daily-habit questions. They need
  careful, later, separately-reviewed treatment, not a first lesson.
- **Comparing Catholicism against Protestantism, or any other faith, as "who's right."** The audience's own Quora
  questions ask what Catholics believe and why; turning that into a scorecard against another tradition is a
  different, riskier conversation this app should not start.
- **Treating cradle/cultural/lapsed Catholics as failures or "bad Catholics."** The Hindu report flagged the same
  trap ("bad Hindu" shaming); here it maps onto "CEO Catholic," "Chreaster," or "A&P Catholic" — all mildly
  derogatory nicknames ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapsed_Catholic)) that this app's voice should
  never use about its own learners. Start from "you already carry more of this than you think."
- **One regional or ethnic version presented as "the" Catholic experience.** Quinceañeras, Simbang Gabi, and Italian
  or Irish family habits are all real and all different; say "in many families…" rather than picking one as default,
  the same caution the Hindu report gives about Diwali's regional stories.
- **Debating the Immaculate Conception/Virgin Birth or purgatory/limbo distinctions as "gotcha" trivia.** They're
  genuinely confused pairs worth untangling gently, but framed as a quiz to catch people out, they read as mocking
  rather than teaching.
- **Claiming settled science or psychology for devotional practice** (e.g., "the rosary is proven to calm your nervous
  system"). Give the meaning and the history; the Hindu report flagged the same overreach with kalava bracelets and
  mundan haircuts — resist it here too.
- **Starting First Communion/Confirmation-style content with the sacrament's legal/canonical requirements** (minimum
  age, required classes, paperwork). That's parish-administration content, not a hook; it's also exactly what already
  exists everywhere and is exactly what the founder worries reads as "hardcore."

## A proposed first week

One topic a day, each starting from an object or moment this audience has already lived, not a term they haven't.

1. **Day 1 — The sign of the cross.** The single most common, most physical, least-explained gesture; nearly
   everyone raised Catholic has done it thousands of times without being told what each part means.
2. **Day 2 — What's really happening at Communion.** The idea the Pew data shows is the single biggest known gap
   between what's taught and what's believed — worth giving it its own day early, gently, as a "here's the actual
   claim" rather than a test.
3. **Day 3 — Confession: why a person, not just a prayer.** One of the most-asked "why" questions in this research
   and one of the most viscerally remembered childhood experiences (the booth, the nerves).
4. **Day 4 — The rosary in your hand.** A concrete object most families have somewhere in the house; teaches the
   "why beads" logic and gives one thing to actually do, mirroring what worked for the Hindu path's "learn one
   shloka" format.
5. **Day 5 — Mary and the saints: honor, not worship.** Clears up the most repeated outside-friend question
   ("isn't that idolatry?") with the latria/dulia distinction explained in plain words, no Latin needed up front.
6. **Day 6 — Ashes, Lent, and giving something up.** Timely, visible, physically worn in public — a natural "explain
   my childhood" moment that also opens the door to fasting and food practices across other doors on this app.
7. **Day 7 — Your people's version: quinceañeras, Simbang Gabi, saints' medals and family altars.** Closes the first
   week by handing the lesson back to the learner's own family, the same way the Hindu week one ends on home and
   family rather than a text — reinforcing "you already know more of this than you think."
